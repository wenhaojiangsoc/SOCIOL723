\begin{frame}{A Third Source of Variation}
So far: adjust for observables (Week 6), find an instrument (Week 7), find a
threshold (Week 9). Today: use \alert{variation over time within the same unit}.
\vskip6pt
\begin{block}{The core idea}
Observe the same unit before and after treatment.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item What is \textbf{stable} about the unit (ability, upbringing) is the same in both
        periods, so the before--after change removes it, measured or not:
        \textbf{fixed effects}.
  \item What \textbf{changes for everyone} (a recession, getting older) does not cancel,
        but units that were not treated went through it too, so their change measures
        it: \textbf{difference-in-differences}.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\vskip6pt
One idea, one assumption: nothing else changes differently for the treated units.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Example: Does Marriage Raise Men's Wages?\core}
\textbf{The question.} Married men earn more than unmarried men. Is it marriage
(specialization at home, employers' favour), or do men with high earning potential marry?
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} NLSY men observed every year 1980--87: 545 men, 4,360
person-years \srcp{Vella and Verbeek 1998; \texttt{wooldridge::wagepan}}.
$Y_{it}$: log hourly wage of man $i$ in year $t$; $D_{it} = 1$ if married that year.
\vskip3pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lc@{}}
\toprule
men never married in the panel & 162 \\
men married in all eight years & 73 \\
men whose status changes & 310 \\
\midrule
share married, 1980 $\to$ 1987 & 0.19 $\to$ 0.61 \\
mean log wage, 1980 $\to$ 1987 & 1.39 $\to$ 1.87 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{Start simple}: 1980 and 1987 only; the 528 men who married or did not change
(17 who left marriage set aside). All men and years come later \srcp{figs/w10\_panel\_slow.R}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pooled OLS\core}
Treat the 1,056 person-years (528 men, 1980 and 1987) as if they were 1,056 different men:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} &= a + \beta D_{it} + u_{it}, \qquad \hat\beta = \alert{0.358}\ (0.031)
   && \text{\footnotesize married men earn about 43\% more} \\
  \plim \hat\beta &= \beta + \frac{\Cov(D_{it}, u_{it})}{\Var(D_{it})}
   && \text{\footnotesize omitted variables (Week 2)}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $u_{it}$ holds everything else that sets man $i$'s wage in year $t$:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
    \item \emph{stable} traits: ability, ambition, schooling, family background;
    \item what \emph{changes over time}: experience, seniority, prices, the economy.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Both can move with marriage: men with high-wage traits may marry more, and
        men marry as they age while every man's wage grows. Either way
        $\Cov(D_{it}, u_{it}) > 0$ and $0.358$ is too big.
  \item We cannot measure all of them. But we see each man more than once.
\end{itemize}
{\footnotesize Standard errors clustered by man throughout \srcp{figs/w10\_panel\_slow.R}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Compare Each Man With Himself\core}
Split the error in three: $u_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \epsilon_{it}$.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\alpha_i$: the combined effect on his wage of \emph{everything stable} about
        man $i$ (ability, ambition, schooling, family background), measured or not.
  \item $\lambda_t$: what changes for \emph{every} man in year $t$ (experience, prices).
  \item $\epsilon_{it}$: the rest.
\end{itemize}
Take 1987 and 1980:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{i,87} &= \alpha_i + \lambda_{87} + \beta D_{i,87} + \epsilon_{i,87}
   && \text{\footnotesize 1987} \\
  Y_{i,80} &= \alpha_i + \lambda_{80} + \beta D_{i,80} + \epsilon_{i,80}
   && \text{\footnotesize 1980} \\
  Y_{i,87} - Y_{i,80} &= \underbrace{(\lambda_{87} - \lambda_{80})}_{\text{intercept}}
   + \beta\,(D_{i,87} - D_{i,80}) + (\epsilon_{i,87} - \epsilon_{i,80})
   && \text{\footnotesize subtract: $\alpha_i$ cancels}
\end{align*}
\textbf{First differences}: regress each man's wage growth on his change in marital
status, with an intercept. \alert{Idea 1: compare each man with himself.} Every stable
trait is gone, measured or not.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Men Who Did Not Change Are the Control Group\core}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
change in married, 1980 $\to$ 1987 & men & mean wage growth \\
\midrule
married ($\Delta D = 1$) & 251 & 0.488 \\
no change ($\Delta D = 0$) & 277 & 0.476 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
OLS: $\hat\lambda_{87} - \hat\lambda_{80} = 0.476$ (0.037), \ $\hat\beta = \alert{0.012}$ (0.053).
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The intercept is the year effect, $\lambda_{87} - \lambda_{80}$, estimated from the 277 men
        whose status did not change. \alert{Idea 2: the year fixed effect makes them the
        control group.}
  \item $\hat\beta = 0.488 - 0.476$ is the \emph{extra} growth of men who married:
        difference-in-differences. If the timing of marriage were random, this is
        the comparison an experiment would make.
  \item Pooled OLS said 0.358. Most of it was stable traits and common growth.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{All Men, All Years: The Model\core}
Now all 545 men (the 17 who left marriage too) and every year, $t = 1980, \dots, 1987$:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \underbrace{\alpha_i}_{\text{who he is}}
         + \underbrace{\lambda_t}_{\text{what year it is}}
         + \underbrace{\beta D_{it}}_{\text{marriage}}
         + \underbrace{\epsilon_{it}}_{\text{everything else}} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item $\alpha_i$: one number per man, for everything stable about him (ability,
        ambition, family background), measured or not: man fixed effects.
  \item $\lambda_t$: one number per year, for what happens to every man that year
        (the economy, a year more experience): year fixed effects.
  \item With both sets of fixed effects in the model, people call it two-way fixed
        effects (TWFE).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two-Way FE Can Be Demeaned: the Algebra\adv}
Subtract the man's mean \emph{and} the year's mean. In a balanced panel:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} &= \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta D_{it} + \epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize the model} \\
  \bar Y_{i\cdot} &= \alpha_i + \bar\lambda + \beta\bar D_{i\cdot} + \bar\epsilon_{i\cdot}
   && \text{\footnotesize average over $t$: man $i$'s mean} \\
  \bar Y_{\cdot t} &= \bar\alpha + \lambda_t + \beta\bar D_{\cdot t} + \bar\epsilon_{\cdot t}
   && \text{\footnotesize average over $i$: year $t$'s mean} \\
  \bar Y &= \bar\alpha + \bar\lambda + \beta\bar D + \bar\epsilon
   && \text{\footnotesize average over both} \\
  Y_{it} - \bar Y_{i\cdot} - \bar Y_{\cdot t} + \bar Y
  &= \beta(D_{it} - \bar D_{i\cdot} - \bar D_{\cdot t} + \bar D) + \ddot\epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize line 1 $-$ 2 $-$ 3 $+$ 4: $\alpha_i$ and $\lambda_t$ cancel}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Double demeaning}: subtract the man's mean and the year's mean, add back
        the grand mean (subtracted twice). Same $\hat\beta$ as man and year dummies:
        0.0606 both ways.
  \item With only 1980 and 1987 (528 men) it gives 0.012, the same as first differences.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Fixed Effects Averages: One Man's Own Estimate}
Man effects only, binary $D$. Run the within regression for \emph{one} man:
\begin{align*}
  b_i = \text{(his mean wage in married years)} - \text{(his mean wage in unmarried years)},
\end{align*}
because a regression on one 0/1 variable with an intercept is a difference in means (Week 1).
\textbf{Example} (hypothetical): married in 3 of 8 years.
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
 & log wages & mean \\
\midrule
5 unmarried years & 1.50, 1.55, 1.60, 1.62, 1.63 & 1.58 \\
3 married years & 1.70, 1.75, 1.80 & 1.75 \\
\midrule
$b_i$ & & $1.75 - 1.58 = 0.17$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item A man who never changes status has no $b_i$: he adds nothing.
  \item Fixed effects combines the 310 men's $b_i$ into one number. The question is
        how it weights them.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Weights, Step 1: Fixed Effects Is an Average of the $b_i$}
Tilde: minus the man's own mean. Write man $i$'s piece of the fixed-effects formula as
$N_i = \sum_t \tilde D_{it}\tilde Y_{it}$ (numerator) and $S_i = \sum_t \tilde D_{it}^2$ (denominator):
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_{FE} &= \frac{\sum_i\sum_t \tilde D_{it}\tilde Y_{it}}{\sum_i\sum_t \tilde D_{it}^2}
   = \frac{N_1 + N_2 + \dots}{S_1 + S_2 + \dots}
   && \text{\footnotesize add up man by man} \\
  b_i &= N_i / S_i, \quad\text{so } N_i = S_i\, b_i
   && \text{\footnotesize man $i$'s own regression} \\
  \hat\beta_{FE} &= \frac{S_1 b_1 + S_2 b_2 + \dots}{S_1 + S_2 + \dots}
   = \sum_i w_i\, b_i, \qquad w_i = \frac{S_i}{S_1 + S_2 + \dots}
   && \text{\footnotesize substitute}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Fixed effects is a weighted average of the men's own estimates $b_i$.
  \item Man $i$'s weight is his $S_i$, the variation in his own marriage status, as a
        share of everyone's.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Weights, Step 2: What a Man's $S_i$ Is}
Man $i$ is married in $m_i$ of $T$ years, so $\bar D_i = m_i/T = p_i$. His demeaned 0/1 values:
\begin{align*}
  \tilde D_{it} &= 1 - p_i
   && \text{\footnotesize in his $m_i$ married years} \\
  \tilde D_{it} &= -p_i
   && \text{\footnotesize in his $T - m_i$ unmarried years} \\
  S_i &= m_i(1-p_i)^2 + (T-m_i)\,p_i^2
   && \text{\footnotesize square and add} \\
  &= Tp_i(1-p_i)^2 + T(1-p_i)\,p_i^2 = T\,p_i(1-p_i)
   && \text{\footnotesize $m_i = Tp_i$; factor out $Tp_i(1-p_i)$}
\end{align*}
\textbf{Check}: $T = 8$, married 2 years, $p = 0.25$: $2(0.75)^2 + 6(0.25)^2 = 1.125 + 0.375 = 1.5
= 8(0.25)(0.75)$.
\vskip2pt
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.0}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccccc@{}}
\toprule
years married, of 8 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 \\
weight per man, $8p(1-p)$ & 0.88 & 1.50 & 1.88 & 2.00 & 1.88 & 1.50 & 0.88 \\
share of total weight & .08 & .11 & .19 & .15 & .22 & .14 & .12 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
Largest for men married half the time; a man married 1 of 8 years counts less than half as much.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Weights, Step 3: Two Men}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
 & married & own estimate $b_i$ & weight $S_i = 8p(1-p)$ \\
\midrule
man A & 4 of 8 years & 0.10 & 2.000 \\
man B & 1 of 8 years & 0.40 & 0.875 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_{FE} &= \frac{2.000 \times 0.10 + 0.875 \times 0.40}{2.000 + 0.875} = \frac{0.55}{2.875} = 0.19 \\
  \text{plain average} &= \frac{0.10 + 0.40}{2} = 0.25
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Fixed effects leans toward man A, whose status varies more, not toward the
        typical man. If effects differ across men, FE is not the average effect.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
{\footnotesize man effects only: $\hat\beta_{FE} = 0.243$ = weighted mean of 310 men's $b_i$ (unweighted 0.255).
Adding year effects: 0.061; wages rise with age for everyone, and marriage comes with age \srcp{figs/w10\_panel.R}.}
{\footnotesize Toy numbers \srcp{figs/w10\_toy\_staggered.R}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Budig and England (2001)}
\textbf{The question.} Why do mothers earn less? Does motherhood lower wages, or do women
with lower earning potential have more children?
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} NLSY79 women 1982--93: 5,287 women, 41,842 person-years.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Their model} (p.~213), ``effects are fixed for years and persons'':
\begin{align*}
  \ln w_{it} = \beta\,\text{children}_{it} + X_{it}'\gamma + u_i + v_t + e_{it}
\end{align*}
\vskip-6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\text{children}_{it}$: number of children (0, 1, 2, \dots), not a 0/1 treatment.
  \item $u_i$: person effects (aptitude, preferences, early socialization); $v_t$: year effects.
  \item $X_{it}$ added in blocks: marital status; human capital (education, seniority,
        experience, breaks in employment); job characteristics.
  \item No age: person and year effects already fix it (age $=$ year $-$ birth year).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Two-Way FE Assumes\core}
The model, with the textbook's three assumptions \srcp{Wooldridge 2010, ch.~10}:
\vskip-6pt
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \beta D_{it} + \underbrace{\alpha_i}_{\text{stable: the man}}
         + \underbrace{\lambda_t}_{\text{common: the year}}
         + \underbrace{\epsilon_{it}}_{\text{the rest}} .
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.4cm}l>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{2.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
 & assumption & needed for & marriage data \\
\midrule
FE.1 & strict exogeneity: $\E[\epsilon_{it}\mid D_{i1},\dots,D_{iT},\alpha_i] = 0$, all $t$
  & unbiased, consistent & tested below \\
FE.2 & $D_{it}$ varies over time within some units & identification & 310 of 545 men \\
FE.3 & $\Var(\epsilon_{it}\mid D_{i1},\dots,D_{iT},\alpha_i) = \sigma^2$ and
  $\Cov(\epsilon_{it},\epsilon_{is}\mid D_{i1},\dots,D_{iT},\alpha_i) = 0$, $t \ne s$
  & usual s.e. & false: cluster by man \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Nothing is assumed about $\alpha_i$: it may correlate with $D_{it}$ in any way.
  \item Random effects adds $\Cov(\alpha_i, D_{it}) = 0$, all $t$: men who marry, early
        or late, have the same stable traits on average as men who do not.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Strict Exogeneity, in Words\core}
Two-way FE compares each man's married years with his \emph{own average over all years}.
So FE.1 asks: is a man's marriage in any year unrelated to his wage shocks in \emph{every}
year, past, present, and future?
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{Feedback} breaks it. A raise in 1983 ($\epsilon_{i,83}$ high) makes a man
        more likely to marry in 1984. The raise sits in his \emph{unmarried} years and lifts
        his own baseline, so FE understates the effect; if men marry after a bad year,
        FE overstates it.
  \item \textbf{Something else changing at the same time} breaks it. A man who moves
        for a better job and marries that year: FE credits marriage with the new job.
  \item What it does \emph{not} require: anything about $\alpha_i$. Stable traits may be
        related to marriage in any way.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
Feedback leaves a trace we can test: future marriage predicting today's wage.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Strict Exogeneity, Step by Step: The Estimator's Error\adv}
Man effects only, one regressor, $T$ years. Tilde: minus the man's own mean.
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} &= \alpha_i + \beta D_{it} + \epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize the model} \\
  \bar Y_{i} &= \alpha_i + \beta \bar D_{i} + \bar\epsilon_{i}
   && \text{\footnotesize average over man $i$'s $T$ years} \\
  \tilde Y_{it} &= \beta \tilde D_{it} + \tilde\epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize line 1 $-$ line 2: $\alpha_i$ cancels} \\
  \hat\beta_{FE} &= \frac{\sum_i\sum_t \tilde D_{it}\tilde Y_{it}}{\sum_i\sum_t \tilde D_{it}^2}
   && \text{\footnotesize OLS on the demeaned data} \\
  &= \beta + \frac{\sum_i\sum_t \tilde D_{it}\,\tilde\epsilon_{it}}{\sum_i\sum_t \tilde D_{it}^2}
   && \text{\footnotesize substitute line 3 for $\tilde Y_{it}$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Unbiased if the last fraction averages to zero: $\tilde D_{it}$ unrelated to
        $\tilde\epsilon_{it}$.
  \item But $\tilde\epsilon_{it} = \epsilon_{it} - \bar\epsilon_i$, and
        $\bar\epsilon_i = (\epsilon_{i1} + \dots + \epsilon_{iT})/T$ holds \emph{every} year's
        shock. So marriage in one year meets shocks from all years.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Random Trends: Each Man His Own Slope}
FE allows men to differ in wage \emph{levels}. If men on steeper wage paths
marry, FE still mistakes their growth for a marriage effect. Give each man his own line:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_i\,x_{it} + \lambda_t + \beta D_{it} + \epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize own intercept $\alpha_i$, own slope $\gamma_i$ in experience $x$}
\end{align*}
Year effects stay: own lines absorb differences in \emph{growth} between men; $\lambda_t$ absorbs
shocks common to a year (a recession), which lie on no one's line.
\par\vskip2pt
Estimate $\beta$ in three steps:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item For each man, regress his wages on $(1, x_{it})$ using only his own years.
        Keep the residuals $\ddot Y_{it}$: his departures from his own line.
  \item Do the same for marriage, giving $\ddot D_{it}$, and for each year dummy.
  \item Pool all men and regress $\ddot Y_{it}$ on $\ddot D_{it}$ and the detrended year dummies.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
Fixed effects is the same recipe with a line of slope zero: step 1 subtracts only his mean.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Random Trends: What Changes}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The \textbf{random trend model} \srcp{Wooldridge 2010, ch.~11}; sociology calls it
        \textbf{FEIS}, fixed effects with individual slopes: detrend each unit
        separately, then pool \srcp{Brüderl and Ludwig 2015; Rüttenauer and Ludwig 2023}.
        Needs at least three observations per unit.
  \item Identifies $\beta$ from \emph{departures} from each man's own trajectory
        at marriage: a jump, not a steeper path.
  \item Our 8-year panel: FE 0.047 (0.021), FEIS 0.055 (0.024). Eight years
        per man is short for estimating a slope; the NLSY79 over 34 years
        (next frames) tells a different story.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two-Way Fixed Effects: Strengths and Weaknesses\core}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
\textbf{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Removes every stable trait of the unit, \emph{measured or not}.
  \item Removes everything common to a year.
  \item Simple and transparent: a difference-in-differences with many units and
        periods.
  \item Testable in part: leads (no feedback), event studies.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.50\linewidth}
\textbf{Weaknesses}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Needs strict exogeneity: no feedback, nothing else changing at the same time.
  \item Misses differences in \emph{growth}: men on steeper paths (FEIS).
  \item Only units whose treatment changes identify $\beta$, weighted by how much
        it varies.
  \item Less precise; measurement error grows. \alert{Cluster by unit.}
  \item With a lagged outcome: Nickell bias (below).
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Put Last Year's Outcome in a Fixed-Effects Model?}
Marriage can follow wages, not only the other way round: a good year makes a man more
likely to marry next year, and wages persist. So control for last year's wage:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \rho\, Y_{i,t-1} + \beta D_{it} + \alpha_i + \epsilon_{it}
\end{align*}
This compares men who start the year at the same wage, one who marries and one who does not;
$\alpha_i$ still removes everything stable about the man.
\vskip4pt
\textbf{Other questions where the outcome feeds back into the treatment:}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Health and employment}: illness leads to job loss; job loss worsens health.
  \item \textbf{Parental help and test scores}: parents step in after a bad report card.
  \item \textbf{Policing and crime}: cities hire police after a crime spike; crime persists.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
The catch: with $\alpha_i$ in the model, a lagged outcome breaks strict exogeneity.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Fixed Effects or a Lagged Outcome?}
Sociologists often control for last year's outcome, to absorb differences
between units that are \emph{changing} rather than fixed. Combining it with unit
fixed effects creates a new bias (Nickell, next frame).
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{FE and LDV embody different selection stories}: FE assumes
        selection on time-invariant traits; LDV assumes selection on prior
        outcomes. They can both be valid, but they are different assumptions and
        will generally give different answers.
  \item \textbf{Bracketing} \srcp{Angrist and Pischke 2009, sec.~5.4}: for a
        positive effect, if LDV is the right model, FE overstates it; if FE is
        the right model, LDV understates it. Run both: the truth lies between.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dynamic Panels: Nickell Bias}
The outcome depends on its own past:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \rho Y_{i,t-1} + X_{it}'\beta + \alpha_i + \epsilon_{it} .
\end{align*}
FE.1 fails by construction: $Y_{i,t-1}$ contains $\epsilon_{i,t-1}$, which is in
$\bar\epsilon_{i\cdot}$. Demeaned lag and demeaned error are correlated
\srcp{Nickell 1981}:
\begin{align*}
  \plim_{N\to\infty}\, \hat\rho_{FE} - \rho \;\approx\; -\frac{1 + \rho}{T - 1}
   && \text{\footnotesize large-$T$ approximation; exact formula in the script}
\end{align*}

\midrule

Nickell's exact fixed-$T$ value & $-0.04$ & 0.27 & 0.39 \\

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anderson--Hsiao: Difference, Then Instrument}
First-difference to remove $\alpha_i$:
\begin{align*}
  \Delta Y_{it} &= \rho\,\Delta Y_{i,t-1} + \Delta X_{it}'\beta + \Delta\epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize $\alpha_i$ gone} \\
  \Delta Y_{i,t-1} &= Y_{i,t-1} - Y_{i,t-2}, \quad
  \Delta\epsilon_{it} = \epsilon_{it} - \epsilon_{i,t-1}
   && \text{\footnotesize both contain $\epsilon_{i,t-1}$: endogenous}
\end{align*}
Instrument $\Delta Y_{i,t-1}$ with $Y_{i,t-2}$ \srcp{Anderson and Hsiao 1981}, the
Week 7 conditions:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Relevance}: $Y_{i,t-2}$ is part of $\Delta Y_{i,t-1}$.
  \item \textbf{Exogeneity}: $Y_{i,t-2}$ depends on errors up to $t-2$;
        $\Delta\epsilon_{it}$ on $t$ and $t-1$. Holds \alert{only if $\epsilon_{it}$
        is serially uncorrelated}.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.05}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
same simulation, $\rho = 0.5$ & $T = 4$ & $T = 8$ & $T = 16$ \\
\midrule
mean of $\hat\rho_{AH}$ (sd) & 0.51 (0.13) & 0.51 (0.05) & 0.50 (0.03) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Arellano--Bond: Use Every Valid Lag}
If $\epsilon_{it}$ is serially uncorrelated, \emph{every} lag from $t-2$ back is a
valid instrument for period $t$'s differenced equation:
\begin{align*}
  \E\big[Y_{i,t-s}\,\Delta\epsilon_{it}\big] = 0, \qquad s \ge 2,\; t = 3,\dots,T .
\end{align*}
GMM combines all these moment conditions \srcp{Arellano and Bond 1991}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item More instruments, more precision: period $t$ has $t - 2$ of them. With $T = 8$
        and year effects: 29 instruments.
  \item \textbf{The key check}: the differenced residuals $\Delta\hat\epsilon_{it}$.
        First-order correlation is expected: $\Delta\epsilon_{it}$ and
        $\Delta\epsilon_{i,t-1}$ share $\epsilon_{i,t-1}$. \alert{Second-order correlation must be zero}; if it is not,
        $\epsilon_{it}$ is serially correlated and $Y_{i,t-2}$ is invalid. Start at
        $Y_{i,t-3}$ and check \emph{third}-order correlation instead.
  \item Too many instruments overfit the first stage and pull GMM toward FE-style
        bias; report the count \srcp{Roodman 2009}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fixed or Random Intercepts: the Question}
\textbf{Goal}: the same model whenever members share a group (students in a school,
siblings in a family, workers in a firm, the years of one life):
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ij} = \alpha_j + \beta X_{ij} + \epsilon_{ij} .
\end{align*}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & assumption on $\alpha_j$ & uses \\
\midrule
fixed effects & none: $\alpha_j$ may covary with $X$ & within groups only \\
random effects & $\Cov(\alpha_j, X_{ij}) = 0$, all $i$: unrelated to $X$ & within and between \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item Both let $\alpha_j$ vary across groups. The names mislead: the key difference is
        whether $\alpha_j$ may covary with $X$ \srcp{Wooldridge 2010, ch.~10}.
  \item If it may not, random effects is more precise and estimates group-level variables.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Worlds in a Picture}
Six groups, $\beta = 1$; same $\Var(\alpha_j)$ in both worlds, only its covariance with $X$ differs
\srcp{figs/w10\_fe\_sim.R}:
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{figs/fere_worlds.pdf}
\end{center}
\vskip-8pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item A: high-$X$ groups are no higher; pooled and within slopes agree (1.04 vs 0.99).
  \item B: high-$X$ groups have high intercepts; the pooled slope adds that gap (1.83 vs 1.01).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Estimators, One Formula}
Start from the model, average it over the group's members, and subtract a share $\theta$
of that average:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ij} &= \beta X_{ij} + \alpha_j + \epsilon_{ij}
   && \text{\footnotesize the model} \\
  \bar Y_j &= \beta \bar X_j + \alpha_j + \bar\epsilon_j
   && \text{\footnotesize average over group $j$'s members} \\
  Y_{ij} - \theta\bar Y_j
  &= \beta(X_{ij} - \theta\bar X_j) + (1-\theta)\,\alpha_j + (\epsilon_{ij} - \theta\bar\epsilon_j)
   && \text{\footnotesize line 1 minus $\theta \times$ line 2}
\end{align*}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
$\theta$ & estimator & share of $\alpha_j$ left in the error \\
\midrule
$0$ & pooled OLS & all \\
between 0 and 1 & random effects & $1 - \theta$ \\
$1$ & fixed effects & none \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item For $\theta < 1$ a share $(1-\theta)$ of $\alpha_j$ stays in the error. That is
        harmless only if $\alpha_j$ is unrelated to $X$: the random-effects assumption.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What $\theta$ Is: How Much to Trust the Group Mean}
Random effects picks $\theta$ from two variances, $\tau^2 = \Var(\alpha_j)$ and $\sigma^2 = \Var(\epsilon_{ij})$,
with $n$ members per group:
\begin{align*}
  \theta = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + n\tau^2}}
  \qquad \text{\footnotesize (GLS; Wooldridge 2010, ch.~10)} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The group mean $\bar Y_j$ carries $\alpha_j$ plus noise of variance $\sigma^2/n$.
  \item Many members or large group differences ($n\tau^2 \gg \sigma^2$): the group mean is
        mostly $\alpha_j$, so remove nearly all of it, $\theta \to 1$, like fixed effects.
  \item Few members or small group differences: the group mean is mostly noise, so
        remove little of it, $\theta \to 0$, like pooled OLS.
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
$\sigma = 1.5$, $\tau = 2$ & $n = 1$ & $n = 4$ (simulation) & $n = 20$ \\
\midrule
$\theta$ & 0.40 & $1 - \sqrt{2.25/18.25} = 0.65$ & 0.84 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Testing Fixed Against Random: Hausman}
$H_0$: $\alpha_j$ is unrelated to $X$ \srcp{Hausman 1978}. Fixed effects is consistent either way;
random effects is consistent and more precise under $H_0$, inconsistent otherwise.
\begin{align*}
  H &= \frac{(\hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE})^2}{\Var(\hat\beta_{FE}) - \Var(\hat\beta_{RE})}
   \;\dto\; \chi^2_1
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Numerator}: how far apart the two estimates are. Under $H_0$ both are right,
        so they should differ only by noise.
  \item \textbf{Denominator}: how much noise to expect. Under $H_0$ random effects is the
        most precise, so $\Var(\hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE}) = \Var(\hat\beta_{FE}) - \Var(\hat\beta_{RE})$.
  \item Large $H$: the gap is more than noise, so reject random effects. In the 1,000
        simulated samples it rejects 5.6\% of the time in world A, 100\% in world B.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
{\small Marriage data: $\chi^2_{10} = 37.0$, $p < 0.001$. Caveat: the denominator assumes random
effects is fully efficient, which fails when errors are serially correlated.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{On the Marriage Data: Random Effects}
RE is OLS after subtracting a share $\theta$ of each man's mean.
In a balanced panel, one $\theta$ for everyone:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} - \theta\bar Y_{i\cdot} &= (X_{it} - \theta\bar X_{i\cdot})'\beta + \dots,
  \qquad \theta = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{\sigma_\epsilon^2 + T\sigma_\alpha^2}} \\
  \hat\theta &= 1 - \sqrt{\frac{0.123}{0.123 + 8(0.105)}} = 0.64
   && \text{\footnotesize marriage data}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The man effect is 46\% of the error variance; RE removes 64\% of each man's mean.
  \item RE needs FE.1 \emph{and} $\Cov(\alpha_i, X_{it}) = 0$, all $t$: the man effect
        unrelated to his whole marriage history.
  \item RE's 0.064 lies between pooled OLS with controls (0.108) and FE (0.047): it partly
        uses the between-man comparison, and so can estimate education and race.
\end{itemize}
{\footnotesize Swamy--Arora variance components, \texttt{plm} \srcp{figs/w10\_panel\_std.R}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Canonical $2\times 2$\core}
Two groups, two periods. One group is treated in period 2.
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & Before & After & Difference \\
\midrule
Treated & $\E[Y\mid g{=}1,t{=}1]$ & $\E[Y\mid g{=}1,t{=}2]$ & $\Delta_1$ \\
Control & $\E[Y\mid g{=}0,t{=}1]$ & $\E[Y\mid g{=}0,t{=}2]$ & $\Delta_0$ \\

 & & & $\tau = \Delta_1 - \Delta_0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The first difference removes anything fixed about each group.
  \item The second difference removes anything common to both groups over time.
  \item Equivalent regression:
        $Y_{it} = \alpha + \gamma\,\text{treated}_i + \delta\,\text{post}_t
        + \tau\,(\text{treated}_i \times \text{post}_t) + \epsilon_{it}$.
  \item Example from the panel section: wage growth 1980--87 was 0.488 for men who
        married and 0.476 for men who did not change, so $\tau = 0.012$.

\begin{frame}{The $2\times2$ in Regression Form}
Group $g_i \in\{0,1\}$, period $t\in\{1,2\}$; the four cell means follow:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha + \gamma\, g_i + \delta\, \text{post}_t
         + \tau\,\big(g_i \times \text{post}_t\big) + \epsilon_{it} .
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & Before & After & Difference \\
\midrule
Treated ($g=1$) & $\alpha+\gamma$ & $\alpha+\gamma+\delta+\tau$ & $\delta+\tau$ \\
Control ($g=0$) & $\alpha$ & $\alpha+\delta$ & $\delta$ \\
\midrule
 & & & $\tau$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\gamma$ absorbs fixed differences in level; $\delta$ absorbs the common
        time trend; $\tau$ is what is left.
  \item Card and Krueger: $\alpha = 23.33$ (PA, February), $\gamma = -2.89$ (NJ minus PA
        in February), $\delta = -2.16$ (PA's change), $\tau = 2.76$.
  \item Unit and period fixed effects in place of $g_i$ and $\text{post}_t$: two-way FE,
        \alert{identical with two groups and two periods}, not otherwise.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Parallel Trends\core}
\begin{block}{The identifying assumption}
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid g_i = 1] = \E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0)\mid g_i = 0] .
\end{align*}
\emph{Absent treatment}, the two groups' average outcomes would have moved in
parallel.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Levels may differ arbitrarily. Only the \emph{trends} in the untreated
        potential outcome must agree.
  \item \alert{Untestable}: it concerns a counterfactual trend. Pre-treatment
        parallelism is suggestive evidence, not proof.
  \item \textbf{Not scale-free}: parallel trends in levels and parallel trends in
        logs are different assumptions, and both cannot generally hold.
  \item Needs \textbf{no anticipation}: units must not change behaviour before
        treatment in expectation of it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\end{column}

\begin{frame}{What the Double Difference Identifies, Exactly\core}
Decompose the treated group's observed change. Its period-2 outcome is
$Y_{i2}(1)$, its period-1 outcome is $Y_{i1}(0)$ (no anticipation); add and
subtract the counterfactual $Y_{i2}(0)$:
\begin{align*}
  \Delta_1 = \E[Y_{i2}(1) - Y_{i1}(0)\mid g_i{=}1]
  = \underbrace{\E[Y_{i2}(1) - Y_{i2}(0)\mid g_i{=}1]}_{\text{ATT}}
  + \underbrace{\E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0)\mid g_i{=}1]}_{\text{treated group's \emph{untreated} trend}} .
\end{align*}
The control group supplies an estimate of that second, unobservable term:
$\Delta_0 = \E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0)\mid g_i{=}0]$. Subtracting,
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;\Delta_1 - \Delta_0 \;=\; \text{ATT}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\E[\Delta Y(0)\mid g{=}1] - \E[\Delta Y(0)\mid g{=}0]}_{\text{differential trend; } =\,0 \text{ under parallel trends}}\;}
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the Double Difference\core}
$\Delta_1 - \Delta_0 = \text{ATT} + \text{differential trend}$, so:
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item The estimand is the \alert{ATT}, not the ATE: we borrowed a
        counterfactual trend \emph{for the treated group}. Nothing in the design
        identifies what treatment would have done to the controls.
  \item The bias term \emph{is} the parallel-trends violation, exactly. Every
        diagnostic in this lecture (pre-trends, placebos, Rambachan--Roth
        bounds) is aimed at that one object.
  \item Card and Krueger: NJ's change $0.59 = \text{ATT} +$ NJ's untreated trend. PA says
        that trend is $-2.16$, so ATT $= 2.76$. Had NJ's untreated trend really been
        $-1.00$, the DiD would still say 2.76 but the ATT would be $0.59 + 1.00 = 1.59$:
        the bias term is $-1.00 - (-2.16) = 1.16$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Parallel Trends Is Not Scale-Free\core}
Suppose untreated outcomes would have grown by $10\%$ in both groups, from a base
of $20$ in the treated group and $40$ in the control group.
\vskip3pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & Before & After & Change \\
\midrule
Treated & 20 & 22 & $+2$ \\
Control & 40 & 44 & $+4$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Parallel trends holds \alert{in logs} (both $+10\%$) but \alert{fails in
        levels} ($+2$ versus $+4$). A DID in levels would report a spurious
        $-2$.
  \item Here both hold only if base levels are equal or nothing grows.
        \alert{There is no neutral choice}: the transformation is part of the
        identifying assumption.
  \item Choose before looking at results: would untreated outcomes grow by the same
        amount, or the same percentage? It matters most when levels are far apart.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When Does the Scale Not Matter? \srcp{Roth and Sant'Anna 2023a}}
Parallel trends in levels, in logs, in ranks: when can all hold at once?
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{Result}
Parallel trends holds for \emph{every} monotone transformation of $Y$ if and only if
the whole \emph{distribution} of $Y(0)$ satisfies a parallel-trends condition.
In practice: the population splits into a part where treatment is as good as
random and a part whose $Y(0)$ distribution does not change over time.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item In any real DiD the scale is therefore part of the assumption. Choose it
        before seeing results, on substantive grounds (previous frame).
  \item The condition can be falsified in the pre-period: compare the groups'
        outcome distributions, not only means.
  \item For effects on whole distributions under weaker assumptions:
        \srct{Callaway and Li (2019)} (later frame).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Control for Covariates in a DiD?}
Fixed effects already remove every \emph{level} difference between units. A covariate $X$
matters only if it predicts the \emph{trend}:
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item treated and control units differ in $X$, and
  \item units with different $X$ would have changed differently even without treatment.
\end{itemize}
Then parallel trends fails for the groups as a whole but can hold \emph{within} levels of $X$:
\begin{align*}
  \E[\Delta Y(0) \mid X = x,\, D = 1] = \E[\Delta Y(0) \mid X = x,\, D = 0]
  \qquad \text{for each } x .
\end{align*}
\textbf{Examples}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Minimum wage}: NJ and PA have different mixes of store types, and the types
        were shrinking at different rates.
  \item \textbf{Job training}: trainees are younger, and younger workers' wages grow faster anyway.
  \item \textbf{Marriage}: college men marry later and have steeper wage growth.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Condition on $X$: A Worked Example}
Hypothetical: two kinds of stores. Without the law, type A loses 3 workers and type B
loses 1. The law adds 2 to every NJ store. NJ is mostly type B, PA mostly type A.
\vskip3pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc|c@{}}
\toprule
 & share in NJ & share in PA & NJ change & PA change & DiD \\
\midrule
type A & 0.2 & 0.8 & $-3 + 2 = -1$ & $-3$ & 2 \\
type B & 0.8 & 0.2 & $-1 + 2 = \phantom{-}1$ & $-1$ & 2 \\
\midrule
all stores & & & $0.6$ & $-2.6$ & \alert{3.2} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item NJ all: $0.2(-1) + 0.8(1) = 0.6$. PA all: $0.8(-3) + 0.2(-1) = -2.6$.
  \item The plain DiD says 3.2, not 2: PA declines faster because it has more type A
        stores. Parallel trends fails for all stores but holds within each type.
  \item Compare within type, then average with NJ's shares: $0.2(2) + 0.8(2) = 2$.
        That is the regression estimator; weighting and doubly robust do the same.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimator 1, Regression: Predict NJ's Untreated Change}
Same hypothetical stores. Learn the untreated change by type from PA, then predict it for NJ:
\vskip4pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep5pt
  \item From PA stores: untreated change $m(\text{A}) = -3$, $m(\text{B}) = -1$.
  \item Predict each NJ store's untreated change from its type, and average over NJ:
        $0.2(-3) + 0.8(-1) = -1.4$.
  \item ATT $=$ NJ's actual change $-$ predicted untreated change
        $= 0.6 - (-1.4) = \alert{2.0}$.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip6pt
This is g-computation (Week 6) on the change $\Delta Y$; with discrete $X$ and one cell per value,
it is cell-by-cell matching. In symbols, with $m_{0,\Delta}(x) = \E[\Delta Y \mid X = x, D = 0]$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATT} = \E[\Delta Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \E[m_{0,\Delta}(X_i) \mid D_i = 1] .
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimator 2, Weighting: Make PA Look Like NJ}
Say 100 stores in each state: type A is 20 NJ and 80 PA stores, type B is 80 NJ and 20 PA.
\vskip4pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep5pt
  \item Propensity score: share in NJ within each type, $e(\text{A}) = 20/100 = 0.2$,
        $e(\text{B}) = 0.8$.
  \item Weight each PA store by $e/(1-e)$: type A $0.2/0.8 = 0.25$, type B $0.8/0.2 = 4$.
  \item Weighted PA now has $80 \times 0.25 = 20$ type A and $20 \times 4 = 80$ type B
        stores: NJ's mix.
  \item Weighted PA change $= (20 \cdot (-3) + 80 \cdot (-1))/100 = -1.4$, so
        ATT $= 0.6 - (-1.4) = \alert{2.0}$.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip6pt
\textbf{Doubly robust} combines the two: right if either the predictions $m$ or the
weights $e$ are right.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Parallel Trends Given Covariates: Three Estimators}
Assume parallel trends only within levels of pre-treatment $X$; two periods,
$\Delta Y_i = Y_{i2} - Y_{i1}$, $e(x) = \Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i=x)$,
$m_{0,\Delta}(x) = \E[\Delta Y_i\mid X_i=x, D_i=0]$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{regression:}\quad \text{ATT} &= \E[\Delta Y_i\mid D_i=1] - \E[m_{0,\Delta}(X_i)\mid D_i=1] \\
  \text{weighting:}\quad \text{ATT} &= \E\Big[\frac{D_i - e(X_i)}{\Pr(D=1)\,(1-e(X_i))}\,\Delta Y_i\Big] \\
  \text{doubly robust:}\quad \text{ATT} &= \E\big[(w_1(D_i) - w_0(D_i, X_i))\,(\Delta Y_i - m_{0,\Delta}(X_i))\big]
\end{align*}
with $w_1 = D/\E[D]$ and $w_0 \propto e(X)(1-D)/(1-e(X))$, normalized to mean one
\srcp{Heckman, Ichimura, and Todd 1997; Abadie 2005; Sant'Anna and Zhao 2020}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item This is Week 6 with $\Delta Y$ as the outcome: compare treated and control
        units with the same pre-treatment $X$. Needs overlap, $e(x) < 1$.
  \item The DR version is consistent if either $e$ or $m_{0,\Delta}$ is right
        (\texttt{DRDID}); it is what \texttt{did} uses inside each $ATT(g,t)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Covariates in TWFE: What Works}
Same hypothetical stores, 100 per state, two periods \srcp{figs/w10\_did\_cov.R}:
\vskip3pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
 & effect $+2$ for both types & effect A $+4$, B $+1$ \\
\midrule
true ATT & 2.00 & 1.60 \\
\midrule
TWFE & 3.20 & 2.80 \\
TWFE $+$ type dummy & 3.20 & 2.80 \\
TWFE $+$ type $\times$ period effects & \alert{2.00} & 2.50 \\
regression (g-computation) & \alert{2.00} & \alert{1.60} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item A type dummy does nothing: type never changes, so the store effects absorb it.
  \item Type $\times$ period effects give each type its own year path: the TWFE way to compare
        within type. Right when the effect is the same for every type.
  \item When it differs, TWFE weights each type by its treatment variance (here equal, so
        $(4 + 1)/2 = 2.5$); the ATT weights by NJ's mix: $0.2 \times 4 + 0.8 \times 1 = 1.6$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Not Just Add Covariates to TWFE?}
$Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta D_{it} + X_{it}'\delta + \epsilon_{it}$
is the default. Even with two periods it fails when
\srcp{Caetano and Callaway 2024}:
\vskip3pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item $X_{it}$ is affected by treatment (a ``bad control'', Week 5). Example: hours
        per worker after a minimum-wage rise;
  \item trends depend on the \emph{level} of $X$, not only its change: the
        within transformation keeps only changes. Example: large stores shrink faster;
  \item trends depend on time-invariant $X$ (region, education), which the
        unit effects absorb, unless interacted with period (previous table);
  \item effects vary with $X$, so the regression weights the subgroups in its
        own way (Week 6's regression weights again);
  \item the linear form is wrong.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
Fix: use pre-treatment $X$ (or $X$ at a baseline period) in one of the three
estimators on the previous frame; never post-treatment $X$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Event Studies\core}
Many periods: one effect for each period relative to treatment:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t
  + \sum_{k \neq -1} \tau_k \cdot \mathds{1}\{t - G_i = k\} + \epsilon_{it} ,
\end{align*}
where $G_i$ is unit $i$'s treatment date (for example, the year a man marries) and
$k = -1$ is the omitted reference.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Pre-period coefficients} ($k<0$) are the parallel-trends check.
        Flat and near zero is what you want to see.
  \item \textbf{Post-period coefficients} trace out the dynamics: does the effect
        grow, fade, or appear only after a lag?
  \item \alert{Caution}: a flat pre-trend is weak evidence. The test often has low
        power, and conditioning your analysis on passing it distorts inference
        \srcp{Roth 2022}.
  \item With staggered $G_i$ and effects that differ by cohort, these TWFE leads
        are contaminated by post-period effects (Staggered section).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the Event-Study Equation}
One man married in 1984 ($G_i = 1984$), observed 1980--87. $k = t - G_i$ is years since marriage:
\vskip4pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccccccc@{}}
\toprule
year $t$ & 1980 & 1981 & 1982 & 1983 & 1984 & 1985 & 1986 & 1987 \\
$k = t - 1984$ & $-4$ & $-3$ & $-2$ & $-1$ & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 \\
dummy that is 1 & $\tau_{-4}$ & $\tau_{-3}$ & $\tau_{-2}$ & none & $\tau_0$ & $\tau_1$ & $\tau_2$ & $\tau_3$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Each year switches on exactly one dummy, except $k = -1$, the reference: every
        $\tau_k$ compares year $k$ with the year before marriage.
  \item A man who never marries has every dummy 0: he only helps estimate $\alpha_i$ and
        $\lambda_t$.
  \item $\tau_{-4}, \tau_{-3}, \tau_{-2}$: marriage cannot have caused anything yet, so they
        should be near 0.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Event Studies: Specification Details That Matter}
Three choices inside the specification change the picture; report each.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Normalization}: one lead must be omitted (here $k=-1$). Every
        $\tau_k$ is relative to it, so the picture shifts if you change the
        reference period. Say which you used.
  \item \textbf{Binning the endpoints}: with unbalanced exposure, far leads and
        lags are estimated off few units. Bin them ($k \leq -5$, $k \geq 5$) or
        restrict to a balanced window, and say which.
  \item \textbf{Never-treated units} anchor the time effects $\lambda_t$. If there
        are none, the fully dynamic TWFE specification is not identified (a linear
        trend in $k$ is indistinguishable from $\alpha_i + \lambda_t$): drop a second
        lead or bin \srcp{Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess 2024}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pre-Trend Tests Have Low Power \srcp{Roth 2022}}
\textbf{The practice}: estimate the leads, see that they are insignificant,
proceed as if parallel trends holds.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The audit}: 12 event-study papers in leading economics journals
(2014--18) with replication data. Roth asks what happens under a linear
violation of parallel trends that the usual pre-test would catch only half the
time.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The bias such a violation produces is often as large as the estimated
        effect, sometimes larger.
  \item In the worst case a nominal 95\% interval covers the truth \alert{24\%}
        of the time.
  \item \textbf{Conditioning on passing makes it worse}: among draws that pass the
        pre-test, the bias is usually larger, sometimes twice as large, because
        passing selects draws whose noise happened to offset the trend.
\end{itemize}
\vskip1pt
Practical: report the power of the pre-test against violations you care about
(\texttt{pretrends}), and replace ``test and proceed'' with sensitivity
analysis (\srct{Rambachan and Roth 2023}, below).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Base Periods: Reading the Two Plots}
Same setup: no effect, the treated group drifts by $\gamma = 0.1$ per period:
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item Callaway--Sant'Anna's default (``varying'') draws a flat pre-period and

        The imputation estimator's default draws a jump.
  \item The pre-period points answer different questions under the two bases.
        Do not read a varying-base plot with TWFE eyes, and do not feed it to
        \texttt{HonestDiD}.
  \item For plots: \texttt{base\_period = "universal"} in \texttt{did}. All event

\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Drawing an Event Study \srcp{Freyaldenhoven, Hansen, Pérez Pérez, and Shapiro 2025}}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Normalize} $\hat\beta_{-1} = 0$ and label that point with the
        outcome's mean, so readers can judge magnitudes.
  \item \textbf{Two kinds of bands}: pointwise 95\% intervals, and a
        \alert{sup-$t$ band} that covers the whole path at once. A path shape
        (``no pre-trend at all leads'') is a joint claim; only the uniform band
        tests it.
  \item \textbf{Report two $p$-values} in the legend: no pre-trends (leads jointly
        zero) and effects levelling off (last lags equal).
  \item \textbf{Show the least wiggly confound} consistent with the leads: if a
        smooth trend through the pre-period would explain the post-period, the
        plot should show it.
  \item \textbf{Bin or trim the end points} and say which.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
{\footnotesize \srct{Freyaldenhoven, Hansen, and Shapiro (2019)}: a covariate
affected by the confound but not by the policy can identify the effect even
with pre-trends (instrument the confound with leads of the policy).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Kleven, Landais, and Søgaard (2019)}
\textbf{The question.} How much of the gender gap in earnings is caused by
children? Danish registers 1980--2013; parents with a first birth 1985--2003,
followed from 5 years before to 10 years after.
\vskip2pt
\textbf{The design}: an event study around the first birth, separately for
mothers and fathers, with age and year dummies:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ist}^g &= \sum_{j\neq -1}\alpha_j^g\,\mathds 1\{j = t\}
   + \sum_k \beta_k^g\,\mathds 1\{k = \text{age}_{is}\}
   + \sum_y \gamma_y^g\,\mathds 1\{y = s\} + \nu_{ist}^g \\
  P_t &= \big(\hat\alpha_t^{m} - \hat\alpha_t^{w}\big)\big/\E[\tilde Y_{ist}^{w}\mid t]
\end{align*}
with $g \in \{m, w\}$ men or women, $t$ event time, $s$ year, and $\tilde Y$ the prediction from age and year
alone. $P_t$ is the \textbf{child penalty}: the gap that opens, as a share of
women's counterfactual earnings.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item No control group of non-parents: identification is smoothness, the
        same argument as RD in time, with fathers as a check.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Kleven, Landais, and Søgaard: Reading the Child Penalty}
$P_t = \big(\hat\alpha_t^{m} - \hat\alpha_t^{w}\big)\big/\E[\tilde Y_{ist}^{w}\mid t]$, step by step,
with round numbers (hypothetical, in DKK):
\vskip4pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep5pt
  \item Fathers, five years after the first birth: earnings at their $t = -1$ level,
        so $\hat\alpha_5^m = 0$.
  \item Mothers: 40,000 below their $t = -1$ level, so $\hat\alpha_5^w = -40{,}000$.
  \item Gap: $\hat\alpha_5^m - \hat\alpha_5^w = 0 - (-40{,}000) = 40{,}000$.
  \item Scale by what mothers would have earned without children (age and year
        alone predict 200,000): $P_5 = 40{,}000 / 200{,}000 = 0.20$.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip4pt
$P_5 = 0.20$: five years on, children have opened a gap of 20\% of women's counterfactual
earnings.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Setting Everyone Uses\core}
Units adopt the policy at \emph{different times}: states pass a law in 1995,
2001, 2008. The standard specification is
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta\, D_{it} + \epsilon_{it},
  \qquad D_{it} = \mathds{1}\{t \geq G_i\} .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
For thirty years this was assumed to estimate ``the'' treatment effect. It does
when the effect is \alert{constant across units and over time since treatment}.
With heterogeneous or dynamic effects it need not estimate any sensible
average.
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{Goodman-Bacon (2021)}
The TWFE estimate is a weighted average of \emph{all possible} $2\times2$ DID
comparisons in the data, including comparisons that use
\alert{already-treated units as controls}.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Running Example: Unilateral Divorce and Female Suicide}
\srct{Stevenson and Wolfers (2006)}: did letting either spouse end a marriage
reduce women's suicide? States adopted unilateral divorce in different years.
\vskip1pt

  \item 51 states, 1964--96; outcome: female suicides per million women (mean 51 in 1964).
  \item 9 states adopted before 1964 (always treated), 37 adopted 1969--85, 5 never.
\end{itemize}

  \item In the shaded window, TWFE uses the \emph{early-treated} unit as a control

  \item If the early unit's effect is still evolving, that evolution is subtracted

  \item With dynamic effects, TWFE can be the wrong \emph{sign} even when every

\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{The Forbidden Comparison, in Algebra\core}
Take the late-versus-early $2\times2$ from the shaded window: late switches from
untreated to treated, early is \emph{treated throughout}. Suppose parallel
trends holds exactly and untreated outcomes are flat, so every change in $Y$ is
a treatment effect. Then
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta^{2\times2}_{\text{late vs.\ early}}
  = \underbrace{\text{ATT}_{\text{late}}(\text{post})}_{\text{what we want}}
  \;-\; \underbrace{\Big[\text{ATT}_{\text{early}}(\text{post}) - \text{ATT}_{\text{early}}(\text{pre})\Big]}_{\alert{\text{the \emph{growth} in early's effect over the window}}} .
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The first term is what we want: late's effect after it switches.
  \item The second term is \emph{early's} effect growing over the same window. The
        comparison treats that growth as if it were a trend, and subtracts it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Forbidden Comparison, in Numbers\core}
No noise, no trends, every true effect positive:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Late's true effect is $+1$; this comparison returns $0$. If early's

  \item If effects were constant ($1,1,1$), the bracketed term would vanish and
        the comparison would be harmless, which is exactly why the problem
        stayed invisible for thirty years of constant-effect thinking.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Goodman-Bacon Decomposition\adv}
With staggered timing, the TWFE estimate is a weighted average of every possible
$2\times2$ comparison in the data:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta^{\text{TWFE}} = \sum_{\text{pairs}} s_{ab}\;\hat\beta^{2\times2}_{ab} ,
  \qquad \sum s_{ab} = 1 ,
\end{align*}
where the weights $s_{ab}$ depend on group sizes and on the \emph{variance of
treatment} within each pair, largest for pairs whose treatment switches
mid-window.
\vskip3pt
Three kinds of comparison arise:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item Treated versus \textbf{never-treated}, clean.
  \item \textbf{Earlier} treated versus \textbf{later} treated, before the later
        group switches, clean.
  \item \alert{\textbf{Later} treated versus \textbf{earlier} treated, after the
        earlier group switches, the forbidden comparison.}
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
Type 3 uses already-treated units as controls. If their effects are still
evolving, that evolution is subtracted from the late group's effect.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{The Goodman-Bacon Weights, Explicitly\adv}
Let $n_k, n_l, n_U$ be the groups' sample shares and $\bar D_j$ the fraction of
the panel that group $j$ spends treated ($k$ treated earlier than $l$). Up to a
common normalization, the weight on each $2\times2$ is the product of the pair's
group sizes and the \emph{variance of the treatment dummy within that pair's
window}:
\begin{align*}
  s_{kU} &\propto n_k\, n_U\;\bar D_k\big(1-\bar D_k\big)
  && \text{treated vs.\ never-treated} \\[2pt]
  s^{\,k}_{kl} &\propto n_k\, n_l\;\big(\bar D_k - \bar D_l\big)\big(1-\bar D_k\big)
  && \text{early vs.\ late, before late switches} \\[2pt]
  s^{\,l}_{kl} &\propto n_k\, n_l\;\bar D_l\big(\bar D_k-\bar D_l\big)
  && \alert{\text{late vs.\ early, after early switches}}
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Every weight has the $\bar D(1-\bar D)$ shape of a Bernoulli variance,
        so \alert{groups treated near the middle of the panel dominate}: the
        weights come from OLS mechanics, not from group sizes alone and not from
        any estimand you chose (Week 1's variance weighting).
  \item The forbidden weight $s^{\,l}_{kl}$ is not negligible in general: it
        grows with $\bar D_k$, i.e.\ with how long the early group has already
        been treated when the late group switches.
  \item All of this is computable from the data alone:
        \texttt{bacondecomp::bacon()} lists every $2\times2$, its estimate, and
        its weight. We decompose a real TWFE estimate in lab.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Where the Negative Weights Come From\adv}
FWL (Week 1) reduces TWFE to a bivariate regression on the
\emph{double-demeaned} treatment. With $\bar D_i$, $\bar D_t$, $\bar D$ the
unit, period, and grand means of $D_{it}$:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta^{\text{TWFE}}
  = \frac{\sum_{it} \tilde D_{it}\, Y_{it}}{\sum_{it} \tilde D_{it}^2},
  \qquad
  \tilde D_{it} = D_{it} - \bar D_i - \bar D_t + \bar D .
\end{align*}
Substitute $Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \tau_{it} D_{it} + \epsilon_{it}$:
the fixed effects drop out because $\tilde D$ has zero unit and period
sums, $\sum_{it}\tilde D_{it}\epsilon_{it}/NT \to 0$ under parallel trends, and
$\sum_{it}\tilde D_{it}^2 = \sum_{(i,t):D_{it}=1}\tilde D_{it}$, so
\begin{align*}
  \plim\,\hat\beta^{\text{TWFE}}
  = \sum_{(i,t):\,D_{it}=1} w_{it}\,\tau_{it},
  \qquad
  w_{it} = \frac{\tilde D_{it}}{\sum_{(i,t):\,D_{it}=1}\tilde D_{it}},
  \qquad \sum w_{it} = 1 .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item For a \emph{treated} cell, $\tilde D_{it} = 1 - \bar D_i - \bar D_t + \bar D$,
        which is \alert{negative exactly when $\bar D_i + \bar D_t > 1 + \bar D$}:
        a unit treated for most of the panel, observed in a period when most of
        the sample is treated. That is the early adopter, late in the panel.
  \item Intuition: OLS rewards cells whose treatment status is
        \emph{surprising} given their unit and period averages. An early
        adopter's late-period treatment is completely unsurprising, so its
        effect is priced at zero, or below zero.
  \item The weights are a mechanical fact about OLS and are there even under
        constant effects, harmlessly. Heterogeneous $\tau_{it}$ is what turns
        strange weights into a wrong answer.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Negative Weights: Consequences and Diagnostics\adv}
\srct{de Chaisemartin and D'Haultf\oe uille (2020)} generalize the previous
frame's calculation, allowing covariates and non-binary treatments, and show
that negative weights are the rule in staggered designs, not a corner case.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Consequence: TWFE can have the \alert{wrong sign} even when every
        $\tau_{it} > 0$. This is not a small-sample problem; it survives
        $n\to\infty$, because the weights, not the noise, are at fault.
  \item By the formula, the negative weights fall on units treated \emph{early}
        and observed over a \emph{long} post-period, exactly the units carrying
        the most information about long-run effects.
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic}: compute the weights
        (\texttt{TwoWayFEWeights::twowayfeweights}) and report how many are
        negative and what share of the total mass they carry. Small negative
        mass is reassuring about \emph{this particular} problem; it says nothing
        about parallel trends or about dynamic-effect contamination, so it is
        one diagnostic, not a validity test.
  \item \srct{Sun and Abraham (2021)} show the same contamination in event-study
        coefficients: with staggered timing, the TWFE $\hat\tau_k$ at lag $k$ is
        the cohorts' effects at lag $k$ (weights sum to one) \emph{plus} their
        effects at other lags (weights sum to zero, some negative). So
        apparent pre-trends can be artefacts of post-treatment dynamics, and a
        clean pre-trend plot from raw TWFE proves nothing.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\alert{TWFE is fine when treatment timing is common, or when effects are
genuinely constant.} It is unreliable exactly where sociologists use it most.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Callaway--Sant'Anna: Build Clean Blocks}
Define the \alert{group-time average treatment effect} for cohort $g$ at time $t$:
\begin{align*}
  ATT(g,t) = \E\big[Y_t(g) - Y_t(\infty) \mid G_i = g\big] ,
\end{align*}
identified by comparing cohort $g$ to units \emph{not yet treated} at $t$ (or to
never-treated units), using only periods before $g$ as the baseline. Under
cohort-level parallel trends and no anticipation,
\begin{align*}
  ATT(g,t) = \E\big[Y_{t} - Y_{g-1} \mid G_i = g\big]
           - \E\big[Y_{t} - Y_{g-1} \mid \text{not yet treated at } t\big] :
\end{align*}
a clean $2\times2$. The baseline $g-1$ predates cohort $g$'s treatment, the
comparison group has not started treatment by $t$, and no already-treated unit
is ever used as a control.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Toy example} (the forbidden-comparison numbers, plus a never-treated unit with $Y = 0$):
late's block at $t = 4$ is $ATT(4,4) = (1 - 0) - (0 - 0) = 1$, its true effect. The early unit
is never used as a control \srcp{figs/w10\_toy\_staggered.R}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Callaway--Sant'Anna: Then Aggregate}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Under \emph{conditional} parallel trends, each block can be estimated by
        IPW, outcome regression, or a doubly robust estimator, \alert{Week 6,
        reappearing inside a DID}.
  \item \textbf{Then choose an aggregation}, and the choice is an estimand choice:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
    \item \emph{simple}: average over all treated $(g,t)$;
    \item \emph{dynamic}: average by $t-g$, giving an event-study path;
    \item \emph{group}: average within cohort, showing who benefited;
    \item \emph{calendar}: average within period, showing when.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Sun--Abraham reaches the same place through an interaction-weighted
        regression; Borusyak--Jaravel--Spiess by imputing $Y(0)$ from untreated
        cells.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Imputation, Step by Step \srcp{Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess 2024}}
Use only untreated cells to learn the untreated model, then fill in:
\begin{align*}
  \text{1.}&\quad Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \epsilon_{it}\ \text{ on cells with } D_{it} = 0
   && \text{\footnotesize never-treated, and pre-periods of the treated} \\
  \text{2.}&\quad \hat Y_{it}(0) = \hat\alpha_i + \hat\lambda_t\ \text{ for cells with } D_{it} = 1
   && \text{\footnotesize impute the missing potential outcome} \\
  \text{3.}&\quad \hat\tau_{it} = Y_{it} - \hat Y_{it}(0),\quad
   \hat\tau = \textstyle\sum_{D_{it}=1} v_{it}\,\hat\tau_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize average with weights you choose}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item No treated cell is ever a control; any aggregation ($v_{it}$) is
        allowed, so the estimand is chosen, not inherited from OLS.
  \item \textbf{Efficient} when errors are homoskedastic and serially
        uncorrelated: it uses all pre-periods, not just $g-1$.
  \item \textbf{Pre-trend tests are separate}: run on untreated cells only, so
        testing does not change the estimate.
  \item \srct{Gardner (2022)}'s two-stage DiD is the same idea as two regressions
        (\texttt{did2s}).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\end{center}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item Fit $\alpha_i + \lambda_t$ on the 8 untreated cells. All are 0, so every
        $\hat\alpha_i = \hat\lambda_t = 0$.
  \item Impute $\hat Y_{it}(0) = 0$ in the 4 treated cells.
  \item $\hat\tau_{it} = Y_{it} - 0$: 1, 2, 3 and 1. Average: $7/4 = \alert{1.75}$, the true
        average effect on the treated cells.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip2pt
TWFE on the same 12 cells: 1.10; without the never-treated unit: 0.50
\srcp{figs/w10\_toy\_staggered.R}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extended TWFE: the Regression Was Not the Problem \srcp{Wooldridge 2025}}
TWFE failed because it forced one $\beta$ on all treated cells. Give every
cohort--period its own coefficient:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t
   + \sum_{g}\sum_{t\geq g} \tau_{gt}\,\mathds 1\{G_i = g\}\,\mathds 1\{\text{period} = t\}
   + \epsilon_{it} .
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The unit and period effects are then fitted on untreated cells only (every
        treated cell has its own dummy), so $\hat\tau_{gt}$ equals the imputation
        estimate. \alert{Divorce data: both give $-4.85$}.
  \item Adding the pre-period interactions too, against never-treated states,
        reproduces Sun--Abraham and Callaway--Sant'Anna with a universal base:
        \alert{all three give $-10.56$}.
  \item Covariates enter as cohort-by-period interactions (the ``two-way
        Mundlak'' regression). Nonlinear versions (logit, Poisson) follow
        (\texttt{etwfe}).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stacked DiD and Its Weights}
\textbf{Stacking} \srcp{Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer 2019}: for each
adoption cohort, build a sub-experiment (the cohort plus clean controls, in a
fixed window around adoption), stack them, and run one regression with
sub-experiment-specific unit and time effects.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Never uses already-treated controls, and is easy to explain.
  \item \alert{Unweighted stacking does not estimate a clean average}: each
        sub-experiment's treated and control shares differ, so OLS weights
        their trends unequally \srcp{Wing, Freedman, and Hollingsworth 2024}.
        Corrective sample weights recover a ``trimmed aggregate ATT''.
  \item Used in sociology: Walmart Supercenters and unions
        \srcp{Choper, Lehner, and Parolin 2026}; welfare reform and children's
        home environments \srcp{Kalil et al.\ 2023}, both later.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}




\begin{frame}{The Modern Estimators\core}
All of them share one principle: \alert{never use an already-treated unit as a
control}. Build clean $2\times 2$ comparisons, then aggregate with weights you
choose.

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Honest Inference About Pre-Trends}
Rather than testing pre-trends and proceeding as if they had passed,
\srct{Rambachan and Roth (2023)} propose bounding how badly parallel trends could
fail.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Their ``relative magnitudes'' restriction: after treatment, the
        violation of parallel trends may change from one period to the next by
        at most $\bar M$ times the largest such change before treatment. The
        confidence sets account for sampling error in the pre-period estimates.
  \item Report the \emph{breakdown value}: how large $\bar M$ would have to be
        before your conclusion reverses.
  \item This turns an untestable assumption into a quantitative claim, exactly
        the move sensitivity analysis made in Week 6.
  \item \texttt{HonestDiD}.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{Inference reminder}
\srct{Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan (2004)}: cluster on the unit at which
treatment varies. With few clusters, use CR2 or a wild cluster bootstrap
(Week 2).
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Beyond]{Beyond a Binary, Absorbing Treatment}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Doses: a Continuous Treatment}
\srct{Callaway, Goodman-Bacon, and Sant'Anna (2025)}.
Units receive dose $d$ (minimum-wage increase in dollars, hours of a program);
some get $d = 0$. Two different questions:
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATT}(a\mid d) &= \E[Y_t(a) - Y_t(0)\mid D = d]
   \qquad \text{\footnotesize effect of dose $a$ on those who got $d$} \\
  \text{ATT}(d\mid d) - \text{ATT}(d'\mid d') &= \underbrace{\text{ATT}(d\mid d) - \text{ATT}(d'\mid d)}_{\text{causal: more dose, same units}}
   + \underbrace{\text{ATT}(d'\mid d) - \text{ATT}(d'\mid d')}_{\text{selection: different units}}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Ordinary parallel trends (each dose group vs.\ $d=0$) identifies each
        $\text{ATT}(d\mid d)$, but \alert{not} how effects change with the dose.
  \item Comparing doses needs \textbf{strong parallel trends}: every dose group
        would have trended like any other under every dose. It rules out
        selection on gains.
  \item TWFE on a continuous $D$ mixes both terms with weights that do not
        follow the dose distribution; its weights on level effects sum to zero
        (\texttt{contdid}).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Treatments That Switch On and Off}
Union membership, unemployment, living with a partner: units enter and leave
treatment, and past treatment may still matter.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item TWFE with $D_{it}$ switching assumes no carryover (Imai--Kim) and
        constant effects, and uses switchers as each other's controls.
  \item \srct{de Chaisemartin and D'Haultf\oe uille (2026)}: compare units whose
        treatment first changes at $t$ with units that had the \emph{same}
        treatment history up to $t$ and have not yet changed. The effect of
        having been exposed for $\ell$ periods, $\text{DID}_\ell$, is an event
        study for non-absorbing, non-binary treatments (\texttt{DIDmultiplegtDYN}).
  \item Matching on treatment histories \srct{(Imai, Kim, and Wang 2023)} does
        the same with explicit lag length $L$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
Cost: only units with a matching history count, so fewer comparisons and
wider intervals. That is the price of not assuming carryover away.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Triple Differences}
A policy hits one group (say, mothers) in treated states. Difference the
state DiD for mothers against the state DiD for a group the policy should not
touch (women without children):
\begin{align*}
  \text{DDD} = \text{DiD}_{\text{mothers}} - \text{DiD}_{\text{non-mothers}} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Needs only \textbf{one} parallel-trends assumption: the bias in the two
        DiDs is the same. Neither DiD needs to be unbiased on its own
        \srcp{Olden and Møen 2022}.
  \item With covariates or staggered timing, ``the difference of two DiDs''
        and three-way fixed effects regressions are generally \alert{not}
        valid; pooling not-yet-treated units can add bias even without
        covariates. Use estimators built for DDD
        \srcp{Ortiz-Villavicencio and Sant'Anna 2025; \texttt{triplediff}}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Distributions, and a Design-Based View}
\textbf{Effects on the distribution} \srcp{Callaway and Li 2019}: with three
or more panel periods, distributional parallel trends plus copula stability
(the link between a unit's change in $Y(0)$ and its level stays the same over
time) identify quantile effects on the treated.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Where does uncertainty come from?} Standard errors here usually imagine
resampling units. In policy panels we observe every state.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \srct{Athey and Imbens (2022)}: treat the \emph{adoption date} as the random
        element; TWFE then has a design-based reading, and the usual variance
        is conservative.
  \item \srct{Rambachan and Roth (2026)}: design-based uncertainty for
        quasi-experiments; usual standard errors can remain valid, if
        conservative, for a well-defined estimand.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Factor models} (unit-specific responses to common shocks) relax
parallel trends further: synthetic DiD, generalized synthetic control,
counterfactual estimators (\texttt{fect}). Next week.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Harknett, Schneider, and Irwin (2021)}

  Y_{ict} = \beta_1\,\text{Seattle}_c\times\text{Year1}_t + \beta_2\,\text{Seattle}_c\times\text{Year2}_t
   + \text{city and wave effects} + X_{ict}'\delta + \epsilon_{ict} ,
\end{align*}
linear probability models, weighted to the American Community Survey.
Same firms on both sides narrows differences in business model and jobs, as
Card and Krueger held the industry fixed.

\section[Practice]{Practice}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What a Reanalysis of 49 Studies Found \srcp{Chiu, Lan, Liu, and Xu 2026}}
Political scientists reran 49 published panel studies with the
heterogeneity-robust estimators of this lecture.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The new estimators usually agree with TWFE \emph{qualitatively}, but the
        estimates vary a lot across estimators.
  \item Many studies lack evidence for parallel trends once pre-periods are
        examined properly.
  \item Many are underpowered: the robust estimators' intervals are wide.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
Read with the divorce data in mind: the estimator mattered (TWFE vs.\ robust),
and so did the window over which trends are assumed parallel. The practical
guide that collects these steps: \srct{Baker, Callaway, Cunningham,
Goodman-Bacon, and Sant'Anna (2026)}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A DID Checklist\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item State the estimand: ATT for whom, over which post-treatment window?
  \item Plot the raw group means over time. Before any regression.
  \item Date treatment and defend no anticipation; if in doubt, shift $G_i$ earlier.
  \item Is timing staggered? If so, \alert{do not use plain TWFE}. Use a modern
        estimator, and report the TWFE estimate only for comparison.
  \item Report an event-study plot with pre-period coefficients, using
        Sun--Abraham or Callaway--Sant'Anna rather than raw TWFE leads.
  \item Justify the scale (levels or logs) on substantive grounds.
  \item Cluster on the treatment-assignment unit; report the number of clusters.
  \item Report a sensitivity analysis for parallel trends.
  \item Placebo: a fake treatment date, or an outcome the policy should not affect.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking Ahead}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Fixed effects difference away time-invariant confounders, bought with
        precision and with strict exogeneity; DiD adds parallel trends.
  \item The staggered-adoption literature is a case study in how a method can be
        standard for decades and still be wrong. Read papers published before
        2021 with this in mind, including your own field's classics.
  \item \textbf{Next week}: what to do when there is only \emph{one} treated
        unit, and ``the control group'' has to be constructed.
  \item Notice that conditional parallel trends brought Week 6 back inside a
        DID: once trends are only parallel given covariates, you need a weighting
        or outcome model again, and all of Week 6's cautions return with it.
\end{itemize}
