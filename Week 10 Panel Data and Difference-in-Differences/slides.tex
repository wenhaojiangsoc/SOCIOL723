\begin{frame}{A Third Source of Variation}
So far: adjust for observables (Week 6), find an instrument (Week 7), find a
threshold (Week 9). Today: use \alert{variation over time within the same unit}.
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{The core idea}
If we observe the same unit before and after, we can difference away everything
about that unit that does not change, including confounders we never measured
and could not name.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item That is a genuinely powerful trick, and it is why panel data are prized.
  \item It buys nothing against confounders that \emph{do} change over time, and
        the price is a new and demanding assumption about counterfactual trends.
  \item The last decade has shown that the standard implementation, 
        two-way fixed effects, is badly behaved in exactly the setting
        sociologists use most.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Plan}
\small
\textbf{Goal}: what fixed effects and difference-in-differences estimate, under
which assumptions, and how to estimate it when the textbook regression fails.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep-1pt
  \item \textbf{Fixed and random effects without time}: students in schools; Hausman, Mundlak.
  \item \textbf{Panel fixed effects}: two-way FE, strict exogeneity, FE vs FD,
        clustering, dynamic panels; the marriage wage premium on real data.
  \item \textbf{The $2\times2$}: parallel trends, scale, covariates (three
        estimators), inference with serial correlation.
  \item \textbf{Event studies}: what pre-trend tests can and cannot show; base
        periods; sensitivity to parallel-trends violations.
  \item \textbf{Staggered adoption}: why TWFE fails; the decomposition; the
        new estimators; all of them on one dataset (divorce laws and suicide).
  \item \textbf{Beyond the binary absorbing treatment}: doses, switching on and
        off, triple differences, distributions, design-based views.
  \item \textbf{Applications in sociology}.
\end{enumerate}
{\footnotesize Running data: NLSY men 1980--87 (\texttt{wooldridge::wagepan});
Stevenson--Wolfers state panel 1964--96 (\texttt{bacondecomp::divorce}).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fixed Effects: Goal and Plan}
\textbf{Goal}: compare each unit only with other units \emph{in the same group}, so
that nothing the group members share can confound the comparison.
\vskip3pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
group & member & what the group effect absorbs \\
\midrule
school & student & resources, teachers, neighborhood \\
family & sibling & parents, home, family income \\
firm & worker & pay policy, industry, location \\
person & year of that person's life & ability, upbringing (next section) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
  \item The model, and why pooled OLS is biased.
  \item Route 1: a separate intercept for each group. Route 2: subtract group means.
  \item Why the two routes agree, and which variation they use.
  \item Random effects, and testing fixed against random.
\end{enumerate}
{\footnotesize Data: High School and Beyond, 7,185 students in 160 schools (Week 4)
\srcp{figs/w10\_fe.R}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 1: The Model and the Problem}
Student $i$ in school $j$; $Y_{ij}$ math score, $X_{ij}$ family SES:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ij} = \beta X_{ij} + \alpha_j + \epsilon_{ij}, \qquad
  \alpha_j = \text{everything about school $j$ that all its students share.}
\end{align*}
Pooled OLS of $Y_{ij}$ on $X_{ij}$ leaves $\alpha_j$ in the error. By the
omitted-variable formula (Week 1):
\begin{align*}
  \plim \hat\beta_{\text{pooled}}
  &= \beta + \frac{\Cov(X_{ij}, \alpha_j)}{\Var(X_{ij})}
  && \text{\footnotesize $\alpha_j$ is the omitted variable} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item High-SES students attend schools with more resources: $\Cov(X_{ij},\alpha_j) > 0$,
        so the pooled slope is too large.
  \item HSB: pooled 3.18 (0.13); comparing students only with schoolmates, 2.19 (0.13).
        Clustered by school.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 2, Route 1: A Separate Intercept for Each School}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fe_dummies.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.44\linewidth}
\small
Treat each $\alpha_j$ as a parameter: one dummy per school.
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ij} = \beta X_{ij} + \textstyle\sum_{k} \alpha_k \mathds{1}\{j = k\} + \epsilon_{ij}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Each school gets its own height; all schools share one slope, 1.99.
  \item Each line passes through its school's means (\textbf{$\times$}):
        $\hat\alpha_j = \bar Y_j - \hat\beta\bar X_j$, the usual intercept formula,
        school by school.
  \item The difference in heights is no longer credited to SES.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 2, Route 2: Subtract the School Means}
Average the model over the $n_j$ students of school $j$, then subtract:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ij} &= \beta X_{ij} + \alpha_j + \epsilon_{ij}
   && \text{\footnotesize the model} \\
  \bar Y_j &= \beta \bar X_j + \alpha_j + \bar\epsilon_j
   && \text{\footnotesize average over $i$ in school $j$; $\alpha_j$ is the same for all $i$} \\
  Y_{ij} - \bar Y_j &= \beta\,(X_{ij} - \bar X_j) + (\epsilon_{ij} - \bar\epsilon_j)
   && \text{\footnotesize subtract: $\alpha_j - \alpha_j = 0$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $\alpha_j$ is gone without being named or measured.
  \item OLS of $Y_{ij} - \bar Y_j$ on $X_{ij} - \bar X_j$, no intercept, estimates
        $\beta$ from within-school differences only.
  \item The question becomes: is a student with more SES \emph{than her
        schoolmates} also higher in math \emph{than her schoolmates}?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 3: Why the Two Routes Agree}
\textbf{FWL (Week 1)}: the coefficient on $X$ in a regression with dummies equals
the slope from regressing $Y$-net-of-dummies on $X$-net-of-dummies.
\begin{align*}
  \text{fit of $X_{ij}$ on dummies}
  &= \argmin_{c_1,\dots,c_J}\textstyle\sum_j\sum_i (X_{ij} - c_j)^2
  && \text{\footnotesize one constant per school} \\
  &= \bar X_j
  && \text{\footnotesize the mean minimizes squared deviations} \\
  X_{ij} \text{ net of dummies} &= X_{ij} - \bar X_j
  && \text{\footnotesize the residual; same for $Y_{ij}$}
\end{align*}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.05}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
HSB, all 160 schools & SES & (se) \\
\midrule
160 school dummies, \texttt{lm} & 2.1912 & (0.1086) \\
demeaned by hand, \texttt{lm} & 2.1912 & (0.1074) \\
\texttt{feols(math $\sim$ SES | School)}, clustered & 2.1912 & (0.1298) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
{\footnotesize By hand, \texttt{lm} counts $N-1$ degrees of freedom; 160 means were
estimated, so the truth is $N-1-160$. Small here (1\%); with siblings ($n_j=2$)
the by-hand s.e.\ is too small by a factor of about $\sqrt2$. Use FE software.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 3: Which Variation Fixed Effects Uses}
Split each student's SES into the school's part and her own part:
\begin{align*}
  X_{ij} - \bar X
  &= \underbrace{(\bar X_j - \bar X)}_{\text{between}}
   + \underbrace{(X_{ij} - \bar X_j)}_{\text{within}}
   && \text{\footnotesize add and subtract $\bar X_j$} \\
  \textstyle\sum_{ij}(X_{ij} - \bar X)^2
  &= \textstyle\sum_{ij}(\bar X_j - \bar X)^2 + \sum_{ij}(X_{ij} - \bar X_j)^2
   && \text{\footnotesize cross term is 0} \\
  4{,}364 &= 1{,}229 + 3{,}135
   && \text{\footnotesize HSB SES}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item FE uses only the 72\% of SES variation that lies within schools; the
        28\% between schools is discarded.
  \item A group with no within variation contributes nothing: siblings with the
        same value, or a school where every student has the same SES.
  \item Precision depends on within variation. Here FE removes $\alpha_j$ from
        the error too, so its s.e.\ (0.130) is close to pooled OLS's (0.134).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fixed Effects Averages the Schools' Own Slopes}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fe_weights.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
\small
With $\tilde X_{ij} = X_{ij} - \bar X_j$ and $\tilde Y_{ij} = Y_{ij} - \bar Y_j$:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_{FE}
  = \frac{\sum_j\sum_i \tilde X_{ij}\tilde Y_{ij}}{\sum_j\sum_i \tilde X_{ij}^2}
  = \sum_j w_j\, b_j
\end{align*}
$b_j = \sum_i \tilde X_{ij}\tilde Y_{ij}/\sum_i \tilde X_{ij}^2$ is school $j$'s own
slope; $w_j = \sum_i \tilde X_{ij}^2/\sum_k\sum_i \tilde X_{ik}^2$ (multiply and
divide by $\sum_i \tilde X_{ij}^2$).
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item 160 slopes from $-2.02$ to $6.27$; weighted mean 2.19.
  \item Weight grows with school size and with SES spread inside the school:
        from 0.22 (20 students) to 1.94 (53 students) times an equal share.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Comparison Fixed Effects Throws Away}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
\includegraphics[height=0.52\textheight]{figs/fe_between.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
\small
The \textbf{between} estimator regresses the 160 school means $\bar Y_j$ on $\bar X_j$:
slope 5.91 (0.37), almost three times the within slope.
\vskip3pt
Pooled OLS mixes the two. Let $W$, $B$ be the within and between sums of squares of
SES (previous frame), and $b_W$, $b_B$ the within and student-weighted between slopes.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_{\text{pooled}}
  &= \frac{W b_W + B b_B}{W + B}
  && \text{\footnotesize split the covariance, like the variance} \\
  &= \tfrac{3{,}135(2.19) + 1{,}229(5.72)}{4{,}364} = 3.18
  && \text{\footnotesize HSB}
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Fixed Effects Cannot Do}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{No school-level variables.} Catholic is the same for every student in a
        school: $\text{Catholic}_j - \overline{\text{Catholic}}_j = 0$. With 160 dummies,
        \texttt{lm} returns \texttt{NA} for Catholic.
  \item \textbf{A narrower question.} $\hat\beta_{FE}$ is the SES gap among schoolmates.
        It says nothing about moving a student to a higher-SES school.
  \item \textbf{Only what is shared is removed.} A confounder that varies among
        schoolmates (a parent's involvement) still biases $\hat\beta_{FE}$.
  \item \textbf{Noise looms larger.} Measurement error in SES is not shared by
        schoolmates, so it survives demeaning while part of the signal does not:
        attenuation is worse within than pooled.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 4: Random Effects}
Same equation, but $\alpha_j = \mu + u_j$ is treated as a random draw (Week 4's
random intercept):
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ij} = \mu + \beta X_{ij} + u_j + \epsilon_{ij}, \qquad
  u_j \sim \mathcal N(0, \tau^2), \qquad
  \E[u_j \mid X_{1j},\dots,X_{n_j j}] = 0 .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The last condition is the price: the school effect is unrelated to the
        SES of \emph{every} student in the school. Pooled OLS needs the same.
  \item If it holds, RE uses between and within variation, so it is more
        efficient, and it estimates school-level variables: Catholic 2.10 (0.34).
  \item If it fails, RE is biased, as pooled OLS is.
  \item Fixed effects asks nothing about $\alpha_j$: it may correlate with SES in
        any way.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Random Effects Is Partial Demeaning}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/re_theta.pdf}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
\small
RE is OLS after subtracting only a \emph{share} $\theta_j$ of each school mean:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ij} - \theta_j\bar Y_j &= \beta(X_{ij} - \theta_j\bar X_j) + \dots \\
  \theta_j &= 1 - \sqrt{\frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2 + n_j\tau^2}}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\tau^2 = 0$: $\theta_j = 0$, pooled OLS. $n_j\tau^2$ large: $\theta_j \to 1$, FE.
  \item HSB: $\hat\tau^2 = 4.73$, $\hat\sigma^2 = 37.0$; $\theta_j$ from 0.40
        ($n_j = 14$) to 0.68 ($n_j = 67$).
  \item OLS on the quasi-demeaned data: 2.3915 = \texttt{lmer}'s 2.3915.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four Estimators of One Coefficient}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lllc@{}}
\toprule
estimator & subtracts from each school & uses & SES (se) \\
\midrule
pooled OLS & nothing & all variation & 3.18 (0.13) \\
between & (uses the 160 means only) & between schools & 5.91 (0.37) \\
random effects & $\theta_j$ of the mean, 0.40--0.68 & a weighted mix & 2.39 (0.11) \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The less of the school mean we subtract, the more the estimate leans on
        the between comparison, and the larger it gets.
  \item RE sits close to FE here because schools are large ($n_j \approx 45$),
        so $\theta_j$ is large. With siblings ($n_j = 2$) and the same variances,
        $\theta = 0.11$: RE would sit close to pooled OLS.
  \item Is the gap 2.39 vs 2.19 more than noise? That is the test.
\end{itemize}
{\footnotesize Pooled, between, FE: clustered by school. RE: model-based, \texttt{lmer}, ML.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Step 4: The Hausman Test, the Logic \srcp{Hausman 1978}}
$H_0$: $\E[u_j \mid X] = 0$.
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
 & $H_0$ true & $H_0$ false \\
\midrule
fixed effects & consistent & consistent \\
random effects & consistent, efficient & inconsistent \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A large gap $\hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE}$ is evidence against $H_0$. To scale it:
\begin{align*}
  \Var(\hat\beta_{FE})
  &= \Var\big(\hat\beta_{RE} + (\hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE})\big)
  && \text{\footnotesize add and subtract $\hat\beta_{RE}$} \\
  &= \Var(\hat\beta_{RE}) + \Var(\hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE})
  && \text{\footnotesize under $H_0$, $\hat\beta_{RE}$ is uncorrelated with the gap} \\
  H &= \frac{(\hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE})^2}{\Var(\hat\beta_{FE}) - \Var(\hat\beta_{RE})}
   \;\dto\; \chi^2_1
  && \text{\footnotesize $K$ coefficients: the matrix version, $\chi^2_K$}
\end{align*}
{\footnotesize Why uncorrelated: if the gap moved with $\hat\beta_{RE}$, adding a bit
of the gap to $\hat\beta_{RE}$ would give a consistent estimator with smaller variance,
so $\hat\beta_{RE}$ was not efficient.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Hausman Test on HSB}
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_{FE} - \hat\beta_{RE} &= 2.1912 - 2.3915 = -0.2003
  && \text{\footnotesize the gap} \\
  \Var(\hat\beta_{FE}) - \Var(\hat\beta_{RE}) &= 0.10865^2 - 0.10569^2 = 0.000633
  && \text{\footnotesize model-based s.e.s} \\
  H &= 0.2003^2 / 0.000633 = 63.4, \quad p < 0.001
  && \text{\footnotesize $\chi^2_1$}
\end{align*}
Reject: SES is correlated with the school effect, and RE's 2.39 is biased.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The denominator is a small difference between two similar numbers; in
        other samples it can be negative, and $H$ is then undefined.
  \item It requires RE to be efficient: homoskedastic errors, no correlation within
        school beyond $u_j$. Clustered s.e.s break the formula.
  \item It depends on how $\tau^2$ is estimated: \texttt{plm}'s default RE gives
        $H = 112$.
  \item Not rejecting does not show $H_0$ holds; it may be low power.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Robust Version: Mundlak's Regression \srcp{Mundlak 1978}}
Let the school effect depend on the school's mean SES:
$\alpha_j = \mu + \gamma \bar X_j + a_j$, $a_j$ unrelated to $X$.
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ij} &= \beta X_{ij} + \alpha_j + \epsilon_{ij}
  && \text{\footnotesize the FE model} \\
  &= \mu + \beta X_{ij} + \gamma\bar X_j + (a_j + \epsilon_{ij})
  && \text{\footnotesize substitute $\alpha_j$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item OLS of this equation gives $\hat\beta = 2.1912$, \alert{exactly the FE slope}
        (FWL: $X_{ij}$ net of $\bar X_j$ is $X_{ij} - \bar X_j$).
  \item $\hat\gamma = 3.53 = 5.72 - 2.19$: student-weighted between slope minus
        within slope.
  \item Random effects is this model with $\gamma = 0$. Test $\gamma = 0$ with
        clustered s.e.: $3.53/0.36$, $\chi^2_1 = 96.8$, $p < 0.001$.
  \item Valid under heteroskedasticity and any within-school correlation, needs
        no variance difference, and extends to several regressors (add each mean).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Panel Fixed Effects: Goal and Plan}
\textbf{Goal}: the standard panel toolkit. What each estimator assumes, how to
check it, and what to do when the outcome depends on its own past.

  \item Random effects, Hausman, Mundlak on the marriage data; what FE averages.
  \item Dynamic panels: Nickell bias, Anderson--Hsiao, Arellano--Bond.
  \item Random trends and the impact function; two studies.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt

(\texttt{wooldridge::wagepan}) \srcp{figs/w10\_panel.R, figs/w10\_panel\_std.R}.}

\begin{frame}{The Error Components Model\core}
Unit $i = 1,\dots,N$, period $t = 1,\dots,T$; $N$ large, $T$ small:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = X_{it}'\beta + \underbrace{\alpha_i}_{\text{unit effect}}
         + \underbrace{\lambda_t}_{\text{time effect}} + \epsilon_{it} .
\end{align*}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & assumption & needed for \\
\midrule
FE.1 & strict exogeneity: $\E[\epsilon_{it}\mid X_{i1},\dots,X_{iT},\alpha_i] = 0$ for all $t$
  & consistency \\
FE.2 & every column of $X_{it}$ varies over time within some units
  & identification \\
FE.3 & $\Var(\epsilon_{it}\mid X_i,\alpha_i) = \sigma^2$, no correlation across $t$
  & efficiency, usual s.e. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Nothing is assumed about $\alpha_i$: it may correlate with $X_{it}$ in any way.
  \item Random effects adds $\E[\alpha_i \mid X_{i1},\dots,X_{iT}] = 0$, as with schools.
  \item FE.3 is usually false; drop it and cluster (below).
\end{itemize}
{\footnotesize Labels as in \srct{Wooldridge (2010, ch.\ 10)}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two-Way Fixed Effects: Double Demeaning\core}
In a balanced panel, average the model over $t$, over $i$, and over both:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} &= X_{it}'\beta + \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize the model} \\
  \bar Y_{i\cdot} &= \bar X_{i\cdot}'\beta + \alpha_i + \bar\lambda + \bar\epsilon_{i\cdot}
   && \text{\footnotesize average over $t$} \\
  \bar Y_{\cdot t} &= \bar X_{\cdot t}'\beta + \bar\alpha + \lambda_t + \bar\epsilon_{\cdot t}
   && \text{\footnotesize average over $i$} \\
  \bar Y &= \bar X'\beta + \bar\alpha + \bar\lambda + \bar\epsilon
   && \text{\footnotesize average over both} \\
  Y_{it} - \bar Y_{i\cdot} - \bar Y_{\cdot t} + \bar Y
  &= (X_{it} - \bar X_{i\cdot} - \bar X_{\cdot t} + \bar X)'\beta + \ddot\epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize line 1 $-$ 2 $-$ 3 $+$ 4: $\alpha_i$ and $\lambda_t$ cancel}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item OLS on double-demeaned data = OLS with man and year dummies (FWL).
        Marriage data: 0.04668 both ways.
  \item Unbalanced panels have no closed form; software demeans by alternating
        between unit and period means until nothing changes (\texttt{fixest}).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Strict Exogeneity: What Demeaning Requires\core}
With one regressor and unit effects only, the within estimator is
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_{FE} - \beta
  &= \frac{\sum_i\sum_t (X_{it} - \bar X_{i\cdot})(\epsilon_{it} - \bar\epsilon_{i\cdot})}
          {\sum_i\sum_t (X_{it} - \bar X_{i\cdot})^2}
   && \text{\footnotesize substitute the demeaned model} \\
  \bar\epsilon_{i\cdot} &= \tfrac1T(\epsilon_{i1} + \dots + \epsilon_{iT})
   && \text{\footnotesize contains every period's error}
\end{align*}
The numerator has mean zero only if $X_{is}$ is uncorrelated with $\epsilon_{it}$
for \emph{every} pair $s, t$: past, present, and future. That is FE.1.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $\E[\epsilon_{it}\mid X_{it}] = 0$ alone is not enough.
  \item It fails under \textbf{feedback}: a wage shock this year ($\epsilon_{it}$)
        makes marriage next year ($X_{i,t+1}$) more likely.
  \item If only feedback fails, FE's bias shrinks like $1/T$; FD's does not
        \srcp{Wooldridge 2010, ch.~10}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When Is Fixed Effects Causal? \srcp{Imai and Kim 2019}}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.46\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.9,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (U) at (1.6,2.6) {$\alpha_i$};
  \node (D1) at (0,1.2) {$D_{t-1}$};
  \node (D2) at (3.2,1.2) {$D_t$};
  \node (Y1) at (0,-0.6) {$Y_{t-1}$};

  \draw[uarr] (U) -- (D1); \draw[uarr] (U) -- (D2);
  \draw[uarr] (U) to[bend right=55] (Y1); \draw[uarr] (U) to[bend left=55] (Y2);
  \draw[arr] (D1) -- (Y1); \draw[arr] (D2) -- (Y2);
  \draw[bad] (D1) -- (Y2);
  \draw[bad] (Y1) -- (D2);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
{\scriptsize Solid: allowed. Dashed orange: ruled out.}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.52\linewidth}
\small
FE removes $\alpha_i$ and nothing else. It needs:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{No time-varying confounders} of $D_t$ and $Y_t$.
  \item \textbf{No carryover}: past treatment does not affect today's outcome
        ($D_{t-1}\not\to Y_t$).
  \item \textbf{No feedback}: past outcomes do not affect today's treatment
        ($Y_{t-1}\not\to D_t$).
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
Together: \alert{strict exogeneity}. A man whose wage rises and who then
marries is feedback; a marriage effect that builds over years is carryover.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip4pt
{\footnotesize Relaxing these turns FE into matching on treatment histories
\srcp{Imai, Kim, and Wang 2023; \texttt{PanelMatch}} or into an event study (below).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Testing Strict Exogeneity: Add the Lead}
Under FE.1, next year's treatment cannot predict this year's outcome once
$\alpha_i$ and this year's treatment are held fixed:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \beta D_{it} + \delta D_{i,t+1} + X_{it}'\gamma + \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \epsilon_{it},
  \qquad H_0: \delta = 0 .
\end{align*}

  \item The s.e.\ (0.022) is as large as the effect: a small feedback would go unseen.

\begin{frame}{First Differences}
Subtract last year's equation from this year's:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} - Y_{i,t-1}
  &= (X_{it} - X_{i,t-1})'\beta + (\lambda_t - \lambda_{t-1})
     + (\epsilon_{it} - \epsilon_{i,t-1})
   && \text{\footnotesize difference; $\alpha_i - \alpha_i = 0$} \\
  \Delta Y_{it} &= \Delta X_{it}'\beta + \Delta\lambda_t + \Delta\epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize $\Delta$ for the change}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Consistent if $\E[\Delta X_{it}\,\Delta\epsilon_{it}] = 0$: only adjacent periods
        matter, slightly weaker than FE.1.
  \item $T = 2$: FD and FE are the \alert{same} estimator. Marriage data, 1986--87
        only: 0.00488 both ways.
  \item $T > 2$: they differ. Marriage data, all 8 years: FE 0.047 (0.021),
        FD 0.038 (0.024).
  \item Both consistent under FE.1. Which is more precise depends on how
        $\epsilon_{it}$ is correlated over time.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{FE or FD? It Depends on the Serial Correlation}
If FE.3 holds ($\epsilon_{it}$ uncorrelated over time, variance $\sigma^2$):
\begin{align*}
  \Cov(\Delta\epsilon_{it}, \Delta\epsilon_{i,t-1})
  &= \Cov(\epsilon_{it} - \epsilon_{i,t-1},\; \epsilon_{i,t-1} - \epsilon_{i,t-2}) = -\sigma^2
   && \text{\footnotesize only $\epsilon_{i,t-1}$ is shared} \\
  \text{Corr}(\Delta\epsilon_{it}, \Delta\epsilon_{i,t-1}) &= -\sigma^2/2\sigma^2 = -0.5
   && \text{\footnotesize $\Var(\Delta\epsilon_{it}) = 2\sigma^2$}
\end{align*}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
$\epsilon_{it}$ is & $\Delta\epsilon_{it}$ is & efficient \\
\midrule
uncorrelated over time & correlated, $-0.5$ & FE \\
a random walk & uncorrelated, $0$ & FD \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
\textbf{Check}: regress the FD residual on its own lag. Marriage data: $-0.40$ (0.02).
Neither $-0.5$ nor 0: errors are positively correlated over time, short of a random
walk. Neither estimator is fully efficient; cluster either way.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Inference: Cluster by Unit\core}
Errors of the same man are correlated across years (previous frame: FE.3 fails).
The cluster-robust variance allows any correlation within a unit \srcp{Arellano 1987}:
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{\Var}(\hat\beta_{FE})
  = \Big(\sum_i \ddot{\X}_i'\ddot{\X}_i\Big)^{-1}
    \Big(\sum_i \ddot{\X}_i'\hat{\bm e}_i\hat{\bm e}_i'\ddot{\X}_i\Big)
    \Big(\sum_i \ddot{\X}_i'\ddot{\X}_i\Big)^{-1}
\end{align*}
$\ddot{\X}_i$: man $i$'s $T$ rows of demeaned regressors; $\hat{\bm e}_i$: his $T$ residuals.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Week 2's sandwich, with a man's $T$ years as one cluster. Valid as
        $N \to \infty$ with $T$ fixed.
  \item Marriage data: s.e.\ 0.0183 assuming FE.3, 0.0210 clustered (15\% larger).
  \item With a treatment that is persistent over time, the gap is much larger:
        placebo laws in the DiD section.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Random Effects in a Panel}
As with schools, RE is OLS after subtracting a share $\theta$ of each man's mean.
In a balanced panel, one $\theta$ for everyone:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} - \theta\bar Y_{i\cdot} &= (X_{it} - \theta\bar X_{i\cdot})'\beta + \dots,
  \qquad \theta = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_\epsilon^2}{\sigma_\epsilon^2 + T\sigma_\alpha^2}} \\
  \hat\theta &= 1 - \sqrt{\frac{0.123}{0.123 + 8(0.105)}} = 0.64
   && \text{\footnotesize marriage data}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The man effect is 46\% of the error variance; RE removes 64\% of each man's mean.
  \item RE needs FE.1 \emph{and} $\E[\alpha_i\mid X_{i1},\dots,X_{iT}] = 0$: the man
        effect unrelated to his whole marriage history.
  \item RE's 0.064 lies between pooled 0.108 and FE 0.047 (next frame): it partly
        uses the between-man comparison, and so can estimate education and race.
\end{itemize}
{\footnotesize Swamy--Arora variance components, \texttt{plm} \srcp{figs/w10\_panel\_std.R}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fixed Effects as a Regression on Unit Means \srcp{Mundlak 1978}}
Suppose the unit effect depends on the regressors only through their mean,
$\alpha_i = \gamma\bar D_i + a_i$ with $a_i$ unrelated to $D_{i1},\dots,D_{iT}$:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} &= \beta D_{it} + \alpha_i + \epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize the FE model} \\
  &= \beta D_{it} + \gamma \bar D_i + (a_i + \epsilon_{it})
   && \text{\footnotesize substitute $\alpha_i$} \\
  &= \beta (D_{it} - \bar D_i) + (\beta + \gamma)\bar D_i + (a_i + \epsilon_{it})
   && \text{\footnotesize regroup: within and between parts}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Pooled OLS of this equation gives \alert{exactly} the FE $\hat\beta$
        (FWL: $D_{it}$ net of $\bar D_i$ is $D_{it} - \bar D_i$). Marriage data:
        0.0467 both ways.
  \item $\gamma$ is the between-minus-within gap: 0.094 (0.045) here. Random
        effects is this model with $\gamma = 0$; testing $\gamma = 0$ is a
        Hausman test that works with clustered errors.
  \item Sociology's \textbf{hybrid model} \srcp{Allison 2009} is the last line plus
        time-invariant covariates, which FE cannot estimate; their coefficients
        are not causal merely because the model is FE-consistent.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Fixed Effects Averages}
Binary $D$, unit $i$ treated in $m_i$ of $T$ periods, $p_i = m_i/T$. With
tildes for deviations from unit means:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_{FE}
  &= \frac{\sum_i\sum_t \tilde D_{it}\tilde Y_{it}}{\sum_i\sum_t \tilde D_{it}^2}
   = \sum_i \underbrace{\frac{\sum_t\tilde D_{it}^2}{\sum_j\sum_t\tilde D_{jt}^2}}_{w_i}\;
     \underbrace{\frac{\sum_t\tilde D_{it}\tilde Y_{it}}{\sum_t\tilde D_{it}^2}}_{b_i}
   && \text{\footnotesize multiply and divide by $\textstyle\sum_t\tilde D_{it}^2$} \\
  \textstyle\sum_t \tilde D_{it}^2 &= T\,p_i(1-p_i)
   && \text{\footnotesize variance of a 0/1 variable}
\end{align*}
$b_i$ is unit $i$'s own before--after difference. \alert{Units are weighted by
how much their treatment varies}, not by their number.
\vskip3pt

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.05}

\toprule
years married, of 8 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 \\
weight per man, $8p(1-p)$ & 0.88 & 1.50 & 1.88 & 2.00 & 1.88 & 1.50 & 0.88 \\
share of total weight & .08 & .11 & .19 & .15 & .22 & .14 & .12 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Adding year effects: 0.061; wages rise with age for everyone, and marriage comes with age \srcp{figs/w10\_panel.R}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Fixed Effects Costs\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Precision}. You are using only within-unit variation. If $D_{it}$
        barely moves within units, the standard error explodes, FWL again.
  \item \textbf{Measurement error is amplified}. Differencing removes signal and
        keeps noise, so attenuation bias gets worse.
  \item \textbf{Nickell bias}: adding a lagged outcome to a fixed-effects model
        creates its own bias (dynamic panels, below).
  \item \alert{Always cluster on the unit} (Week 2). Serial correlation within
        units is the norm.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dynamic Panels: Nickell Bias}
The outcome depends on its own past:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \rho Y_{i,t-1} + X_{it}'\beta + \alpha_i + \epsilon_{it} .
\end{align*}
FE.1 fails by construction: $Y_{i,t-1}$ contains $\epsilon_{i,t-1}$, which is in
$\bar\epsilon_{i\cdot}$. Demeaned lag and demeaned error are correlated
\srcp{Nickell 1981}:
\begin{align*}
  \plim_{N\to\infty}\, \hat\rho_{FE} - \rho \;\approx\; -\frac{1 + \rho}{T - 1}
   && \text{\footnotesize large-$T$ approximation; exact formula in the script}
\end{align*}

\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
simulation, $\rho = 0.5$, $N = 500$, 200 draws & $T = 4$ & $T = 8$ & $T = 16$ \\
\midrule
mean of $\hat\rho_{FE}$ & $-0.04$ & 0.27 & 0.40 \\
Nickell's exact fixed-$T$ value & $-0.04$ & 0.27 & 0.39 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
{\footnotesize Large $N$ does not help; only large $T$ does \srcp{figs/w10\_panel\_std.R}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anderson--Hsiao: Difference, Then Instrument}
First-difference to remove $\alpha_i$:
\begin{align*}
  \Delta Y_{it} &= \rho\,\Delta Y_{i,t-1} + \Delta X_{it}'\beta + \Delta\epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize $\alpha_i$ gone} \\
  \Delta Y_{i,t-1} &= Y_{i,t-1} - Y_{i,t-2}, \quad
  \Delta\epsilon_{it} = \epsilon_{it} - \epsilon_{i,t-1}
   && \text{\footnotesize both contain $\epsilon_{i,t-1}$: endogenous}
\end{align*}
Instrument $\Delta Y_{i,t-1}$ with $Y_{i,t-2}$ \srcp{Anderson and Hsiao 1981}, the
Week 7 conditions:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Relevance}: $Y_{i,t-2}$ is part of $\Delta Y_{i,t-1}$.
  \item \textbf{Exogeneity}: $Y_{i,t-2}$ depends on errors up to $t-2$;
        $\Delta\epsilon_{it}$ on $t$ and $t-1$. Holds \alert{only if $\epsilon_{it}$
        is serially uncorrelated}.
\end{itemize}

same simulation, $\rho = 0.5$ & $T = 4$ & $T = 8$ & $T = 16$ \\

mean of $\hat\rho_{AH}$ (sd) & 0.51 (0.13) & 0.51 (0.05) & 0.50 (0.03) \\

\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Arellano--Bond: Use Every Valid Lag}
If $\epsilon_{it}$ is serially uncorrelated, \emph{every} lag from $t-2$ back is a
valid instrument for period $t$'s differenced equation:
\begin{align*}
  \E\big[Y_{i,t-s}\,\Delta\epsilon_{it}\big] = 0, \qquad s \ge 2,\; t = 3,\dots,T .
\end{align*}
GMM combines all these moment conditions \srcp{Arellano and Bond 1991}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item More instruments, more precision: period $t$ has $t - 2$ of them. With $T = 8$
        and year effects: 29 instruments.
  \item \textbf{The key check}: the differenced residuals $\Delta\hat\epsilon_{it}$.
        First-order correlation is expected ($-0.5$ under no serial correlation,
        two frames back). \alert{Second-order correlation must be zero}; if it is not,
        $\epsilon_{it}$ is serially correlated and $Y_{i,t-2}$ is invalid. Start at
        $Y_{i,t-3}$.
  \item Too many instruments overfit the first stage and pull GMM toward FE-style
        bias; report the count \srcp{Roodman 2009}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Fixed Effects or a Lagged Outcome?}
Sociologists routinely control for last year's outcome, to absorb differences
between units that are \emph{changing} rather than fixed. It is a reasonable
instinct. But combining that move with unit fixed effects creates a new bias, and
it is worth knowing why before you reach for it.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{FE and LDV embody different selection stories}: FE assumes
        selection on time-invariant traits; LDV assumes selection on prior
        outcomes. They can both be valid, but they are different assumptions and
        will generally give different answers.
  \item \srct{Angrist and Pischke (2009)} suggest running both: under some
        conditions the two \alert{bracket} the truth, so agreement is reassuring
        and disagreement tells you how much the selection story matters. Treat
        that as a heuristic, not a theorem.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Random Trends: Each Man His Own Slope}
FE allows men to differ in wage \emph{levels}. If men on steeper wage paths
marry, FE still mistakes their growth for a marriage effect.
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} &= \alpha_i + \gamma_i\,x_{it} + \beta D_{it} + \epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize own intercept $\alpha_i$, own slope $\gamma_i$ in experience $x$} \\
  \ddot Y_{it} &= \beta \ddot D_{it} + \ddot\epsilon_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize double dots: residuals from man $i$'s own regression on $(1, x_{it})$}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The \textbf{random trend model} \srcp{Wooldridge 2010, ch.~11}; sociology calls it
        \textbf{FEIS}, fixed effects with individual slopes: detrend each unit
        separately, then pool \srcp{Brüderl and Ludwig 2015; Rüttenauer and Ludwig 2023}.
        Needs at least three observations per unit.
  \item Identifies $\beta$ from \emph{departures} from each man's own trajectory
        at marriage: a jump, not a steeper path.
  \item Our 8-year panel: FE 0.047 (0.021), FEIS 0.055 (0.024). Eight years
        per man is short for estimating a slope; the NLSY79 over 34 years
        (next frames) tells a different story.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Canonical $2\times 2$\core}
Two groups, two periods. One group is treated in period 2.
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & Before & After & Difference \\
\midrule
Treated & $\E[Y\mid g{=}1,t{=}1]$ & $\E[Y\mid g{=}1,t{=}2]$ & $\Delta_1$ \\

 & & & $\tau = \Delta_1 - \Delta_0$ \\

  \item The second difference removes anything common to both groups over time.

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{The $2\times2$ in Regression Form}
Write $g_i \in\{0,1\}$ for group and $t\in\{1,2\}$ for period:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha + \gamma\, g_i + \delta\, \text{post}_t
         + \tau\,\big(g_i \times \text{post}_t\big) + \epsilon_{it} .
\end{align*}
Read off the four cell means:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & Before & After & Difference \\
\midrule
Treated ($g=1$) & $\alpha+\gamma$ & $\alpha+\gamma+\delta+\tau$ & $\delta+\tau$ \\
Control ($g=0$) & $\alpha$ & $\alpha+\delta$ & $\delta$ \\
\midrule
 & & & $\tau$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\gamma$ absorbs fixed differences in level; $\delta$ absorbs the common
        time trend; $\tau$ is what is left.
  \item With unit and period fixed effects instead of $g_i$ and $\text{post}_t$;
        this is the two-way fixed-effects model, \alert{identical when there are
        two groups and two periods}, and, as we will see, not identical otherwise.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Parallel Trends\core}
\begin{block}{The identifying assumption}
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0) \mid g_i = 1] = \E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0)\mid g_i = 0] .
\end{align*}
\emph{Absent treatment}, the two groups' average outcomes would have moved in
parallel.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Levels may differ arbitrarily. Only the \emph{trends} in the untreated
        potential outcome must agree.
  \item \alert{Untestable}: it concerns a counterfactual trend. Pre-treatment
        parallelism is suggestive evidence, not proof.
  \item \textbf{Not scale-free}: parallel trends in levels and parallel trends in
        logs are different assumptions, and both cannot generally hold. The next
        slide shows why this is a real choice and not a technicality.
  \item Needs \textbf{no anticipation}: units must not change behaviour before
        treatment in expectation of it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{What the Double Difference Identifies, Exactly\core}
Decompose the treated group's observed change. Its period-2 outcome is
$Y_{i2}(1)$; add and subtract the counterfactual $Y_{i2}(0)$:
\begin{align*}
  \Delta_1 = \E[Y_{i2}(1) - Y_{i1}(0)\mid g_i{=}1]
  = \underbrace{\E[Y_{i2}(1) - Y_{i2}(0)\mid g_i{=}1]}_{\text{ATT}}
  + \underbrace{\E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0)\mid g_i{=}1]}_{\text{treated group's \emph{untreated} trend}} .
\end{align*}
The control group supplies an estimate of that second, unobservable term:
$\Delta_0 = \E[Y_{i2}(0) - Y_{i1}(0)\mid g_i{=}0]$. Subtracting,
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;\Delta_1 - \Delta_0 \;=\; \text{ATT}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\E[\Delta Y(0)\mid g{=}1] - \E[\Delta Y(0)\mid g{=}0]}_{\text{differential trend; } =\,0 \text{ under parallel trends}}\;}
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The estimand is the \alert{ATT}, not the ATE: we borrowed a
        counterfactual trend \emph{for the treated group}. Nothing in the design
        identifies what treatment would have done to the controls.
  \item The bias term \emph{is} the parallel-trends violation, exactly. Every
        diagnostic in this lecture (pre-trends, placebos, Rambachan--Roth
        bounds) is aimed at that one object.
  \item Note also what was \emph{not} assumed: no restriction on the level
        difference $\gamma$, and no randomization anywhere.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Parallel Trends Is Not Scale-Free\core}
Suppose untreated outcomes would have grown by $10\%$ in both groups, from a base
of $20$ in the treated group and $40$ in the control group.
\vskip3pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & Before & After & Change \\
\midrule
Treated & 20 & 22 & $+2$ \\
Control & 40 & 44 & $+4$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Parallel trends holds \alert{in logs} (both $+10\%$) but \alert{fails in
        levels} ($+2$ versus $+4$). A DID in levels would report a spurious
        $-2$.
  \item The two assumptions are mutually exclusive unless base levels are equal.
        \alert{There is no neutral choice}, and the transformation is part of the
        identifying assumption, not a presentational detail.
  \item Choose on substantive grounds, is the intervention plausibly additive
        or multiplicative?, and \emph{before} looking at the results.
  \item One diagnostic: if pre-treatment levels are far apart, be especially
        careful. If they are similar, the choice matters less.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When Does the Scale Not Matter? \srcp{Roth and Sant'Anna 2023}}
Parallel trends in levels, in logs, in ranks: when can all hold at once?
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{Result}
Parallel trends holds for \emph{every} monotone transformation of $Y$ if and only if
the whole \emph{distribution} of $Y(0)$ satisfies a parallel-trends condition.
Essentially only two cases deliver that: treatment is as good as random, or
$Y(0)$'s distribution does not change over time for the groups that differ.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item In any real DiD the scale is therefore part of the assumption. Choose it
        before seeing results, on substantive grounds (previous frame).
  \item The distributional condition is testable in the pre-period: compare
        the groups' outcome distributions, not only means.
  \item For effects on whole distributions under weaker assumptions:
        \srct{Callaway and Li (2019)} (later frame).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Parallel Trends Given Covariates: Three Estimators}
Assume parallel trends only within levels of pre-treatment $X$; two periods,
$\Delta Y_i = Y_{i2} - Y_{i1}$, $e(x) = \Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i=x)$,
$m_{0,\Delta}(x) = \E[\Delta Y_i\mid X_i=x, D_i=0]$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{regression:}\quad \text{ATT} &= \E[\Delta Y_i\mid D_i=1] - \E[m_{0,\Delta}(X_i)\mid D_i=1] \\
  \text{weighting:}\quad \text{ATT} &= \E\Big[\frac{D_i - e(X_i)}{\Pr(D=1)\,(1-e(X_i))}\,\Delta Y_i\Big] \\
  \text{doubly robust:}\quad \text{ATT} &= \E\big[(w_1(D_i) - w_0(D_i, X_i))\,(\Delta Y_i - m_{0,\Delta}(X_i))\big]
\end{align*}
with $w_1 = D/\E[D]$ and $w_0 \propto e(X)(1-D)/(1-e(X))$, normalized to mean one
\srcp{Heckman, Ichimura, and Todd 1997; Abadie 2005; Sant'Anna and Zhao 2020}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item This is Week 6 applied to $\Delta Y$: matching and weighting on the
        \emph{change}, not the level.
  \item The DR version is consistent if either $e$ or $m_{0,\Delta}$ is right
        (\texttt{DRDID}); it is what \texttt{did} uses inside each $ATT(g,t)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Not Just Add Covariates to TWFE?}
$Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta D_{it} + X_{it}'\delta + \epsilon_{it}$
is the default. Even with two periods it fails when
\srcp{Caetano and Callaway 2024}:
\vskip3pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item $X_{it}$ is affected by treatment (a ``bad control'', Week 5);
  \item trends depend on the \emph{level} of $X$, not only its change: the
        within transformation keeps only changes;
  \item trends depend on time-invariant $X$ (region, education), which the
        unit effects absorb;
  \item effects vary with $X$, so the regression weights the subgroups in its
        own way (Week 6's regression weights again);
  \item the linear form is wrong.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
Fix: use pre-treatment $X$ (or $X$ at a baseline period) in one of the three
estimators on the previous frame; never post-treatment $X$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Event Studies\core}
Generalize to many periods by estimating an effect for each period relative to
treatment:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t
  + \sum_{k \neq -1} \tau_k \cdot \mathds{1}\{t - g_i = k\} + \epsilon_{it} ,
\end{align*}
where $g_i$ is unit $i$'s treatment date and $k = -1$ is the omitted reference.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Pre-period coefficients} ($k<0$) are the parallel-trends check.
        Flat and near zero is what you want to see.
  \item \textbf{Post-period coefficients} trace out the dynamics: does the effect
        grow, fade, or appear only after a lag?
  \item \alert{Caution}: a flat pre-trend is weak evidence. The test often has low
        power, and conditioning your analysis on passing it distorts inference
        \srcp{Roth 2022}. Report the power of your pre-trend test.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Event Studies: Specification Details That Matter}
That specification looks innocent. Three choices inside it change the picture,
and each must be reported.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Normalization}: one lead must be omitted (here $k=-1$). Every
        $\tau_k$ is relative to it, so the picture shifts if you change the
        reference period. Say which you used.
  \item \textbf{Binning the endpoints}: with unbalanced exposure, far leads and
        lags are estimated off few units. Bin them ($k \leq -5$, $k \geq 5$) or
        restrict to a balanced window, and say which.
  \item \textbf{Never-treated units} anchor the time effects $\lambda_t$. If there
        are none, the $\lambda_t$ are identified only off variation in timing, and
        the contamination problem below is unavoidable.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pre-Trend Tests Have Low Power \srcp{Roth 2022}}
\textbf{The practice}: estimate the leads, see that they are insignificant,
proceed as if parallel trends holds.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The audit}: 12 event-study papers in leading economics journals
(2014--18) with replication data. Roth asks what happens under a linear
violation of parallel trends that the usual pre-test would catch only half the
time.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The bias such a violation produces is often as large as the estimated
        effect, sometimes larger.
  \item In the worst case a nominal 95\% interval covers the truth \alert{24\%}
        of the time.
  \item \textbf{Conditioning on passing makes it worse}: among draws that pass the
        pre-test, the bias is usually larger, sometimes twice as large, because
        passing selects draws whose noise happened to offset the trend.
\end{itemize}
\vskip1pt
Practical: report the power of the pre-test against violations you care about
(\texttt{pretrends}), and replace ``test and proceed'' with sensitivity
analysis (\srct{Rambachan and Roth 2023}, below).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Base Periods: Why Event-Study Plots Disagree \srcp{Roth 2026}}
No effect at all; untreated outcomes of the treated group drift by $\gamma$
per period relative to controls; treatment starts at $r = 0$.
\begin{align*}
  \text{universal base } (r = -1):&\quad \hat\beta_r = \gamma\,(r+1)\ \text{ for every } r
   && \text{\footnotesize as TWFE: one straight line} \\
  \text{varying base, } r < 0:&\quad \hat\beta_r = \gamma
   && \text{\footnotesize compares } r \text{ with } r-1 \\
  \text{varying base, } r \geq 0:&\quad \hat\beta_r = \gamma\,(r+1)
   && \text{\footnotesize compares } r \text{ with } -1
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Callaway--Sant'Anna's default (``varying'') draws a flat pre-period and
        a rising post-period: a \alert{kink at zero that looks like an effect}.
        The imputation estimator's default draws a jump.
  \item The pre-period points answer different questions under the two bases.
        Do not read a varying-base plot with TWFE eyes, and do not feed it to
        \texttt{HonestDiD}.
  \item For plots: \texttt{base\_period = "universal"} in \texttt{did}. All event
        studies in this lecture use it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Drawing an Event Study \srcp{Freyaldenhoven, Hansen, Pérez Pérez, and Shapiro 2025}}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Normalize} $\hat\beta_{-1} = 0$ and label that point with the
        outcome's mean, so readers can judge magnitudes.
  \item \textbf{Two kinds of bands}: pointwise 95\% intervals, and a
        \alert{sup-$t$ band} that covers the whole path at once. A path shape
        (``no pre-trend at all leads'') is a joint claim; only the uniform band
        tests it.
  \item \textbf{Report two $p$-values} in the legend: no pre-trends (leads jointly
        zero) and effects levelling off (last lags equal).
  \item \textbf{Show the least wiggly confound} consistent with the leads: if a
        smooth trend through the pre-period would explain the post-period, the
        plot should show it.
  \item \textbf{Bin or trim the end points} and say which.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
{\footnotesize \srct{Freyaldenhoven, Hansen, and Shapiro (2019)}: a covariate
affected by the confound but not by the policy can identify the effect even
with pre-trends (instrument the confound with leads of the policy).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Kleven, Landais, and Søgaard (2019)}
\textbf{The question.} How much of the gender gap in earnings is caused by
children? Danish registers 1980--2013; parents with a first birth 1985--2003,
followed from 5 years before to 10 years after.
\vskip2pt
\textbf{The design}: an event study around the first birth, separately for
mothers and fathers, with age and year dummies:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ist}^g &= \sum_{j\neq -1}\alpha_j^g\,\mathds 1\{j = t\}
   + \sum_k \beta_k^g\,\mathds 1\{k = \text{age}_{is}\}
   + \sum_y \gamma_y^g\,\mathds 1\{y = s\} + \nu_{ist}^g \\
  P_t &= \big(\hat\alpha_t^{m} - \hat\alpha_t^{w}\big)\big/\E[\tilde Y_{ist}^{w}\mid t]
\end{align*}
with $t$ event time, $s$ year, and $\tilde Y$ the prediction from age and year
alone. $P_t$ is the \textbf{child penalty}: the gap that opens, as a share of
women's counterfactual earnings.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item No control group of non-parents: identification is smoothness, the
        same argument as RD in time, with fathers as a check.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Staggered]{The Trouble with Two-Way Fixed Effects}

\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Setting Everyone Uses\core}
Units adopt the policy at \emph{different times}: states pass a law in 1995,
2001, 2008. The standard specification is
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta\, D_{it} + \epsilon_{it},
  \qquad D_{it} = \mathds{1}\{t \geq g_i\} .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
For thirty years this was assumed to estimate ``the'' treatment effect. It does
when the effect is \alert{constant across units and over time since treatment}
, but with heterogeneous or dynamic effects it need not estimate any sensible
average at all.
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{Goodman-Bacon (2021)}
The TWFE estimate is a weighted average of \emph{all possible} $2\times2$ DID
comparisons in the data, including comparisons that use
\alert{already-treated units as controls}.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Running Example: Unilateral Divorce and Female Suicide}
\srct{Stevenson and Wolfers (2006)}: did letting either spouse end a marriage
reduce women's suicide? States adopted unilateral divorce in different years.
\vskip1pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item 51 states, 1964--96; outcome: female suicides per million women (mean 51 in 1964).
  \item 9 states adopted before 1964 (always treated), 37 adopted 1969--85, 5 never.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item In the shaded window, TWFE uses the \emph{early-treated} unit as a control

  \item If the early unit's effect is still evolving, that evolution is subtracted

  \item With dynamic effects, TWFE can be the wrong \emph{sign} even when every

\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{The Forbidden Comparison, in Algebra\core}
Take the late-versus-early $2\times2$ from the shaded window: late switches from
untreated to treated, early is \emph{treated throughout}. Suppose parallel
trends holds exactly and untreated outcomes are flat, so every change in $Y$ is
a treatment effect. Then
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta^{2\times2}_{\text{late vs.\ early}}
  = \underbrace{\text{ATT}_{\text{late}}(\text{post})}_{\text{what we want}}
  \;-\; \underbrace{\Big[\text{ATT}_{\text{early}}(\text{post}) - \text{ATT}_{\text{early}}(\text{pre})\Big]}_{\alert{\text{the \emph{growth} in early's effect over the window}}} .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
With numbers; no noise, no trends, every true effect positive:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & $t=3$ & $t=4$ & Change \\
\midrule
Late (treated at $t{=}4$; effect $1$) & $0$ & $1$ & $+1$ \\
Early (treated at $t{=}2$; effects $1,2,3$) & $2$ & $3$ & $+1$ \\
\midrule
 & & & $\hat\beta^{2\times2} = 0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Late's true effect is $+1$; this comparison returns $0$. If early's
        effect grew by $2$ per period instead, it would return $\alert{-1}$.
  \item If effects were constant ($1,1,1$), the bracketed term would vanish and
        the comparison would be harmless, which is exactly why the problem
        stayed invisible for thirty years of constant-effect thinking.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Goodman-Bacon Decomposition\adv}
With staggered timing, the TWFE estimate is a weighted average of every possible
$2\times2$ comparison in the data:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta^{\text{TWFE}} = \sum_{\text{pairs}} s_{ab}\;\hat\beta^{2\times2}_{ab} ,
  \qquad \sum s_{ab} = 1 ,
\end{align*}
where the weights $s_{ab}$ depend on group sizes and on the \emph{variance of
treatment} within each pair, largest for pairs whose treatment switches
mid-window. (The next frame writes them out.)
\vskip3pt
Three kinds of comparison arise:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item Treated versus \textbf{never-treated}, clean.
  \item \textbf{Earlier} treated versus \textbf{later} treated, before the later
        group switches, clean.
  \item \alert{\textbf{Later} treated versus \textbf{earlier} treated, after the
        earlier group switches, the forbidden comparison.}
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
Type 3 uses already-treated units as controls. If their effects are still
evolving, that evolution is subtracted from the late group's effect.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{The Goodman-Bacon Weights, Explicitly\adv}
Let $n_k, n_l, n_U$ be the groups' sample shares and $\bar D_j$ the fraction of
the panel that group $j$ spends treated ($k$ treated earlier than $l$). Up to a
common normalization, the weight on each $2\times2$ is the product of the pair's
group sizes and the \emph{variance of the treatment dummy within that pair's
window}:
\begin{align*}
  s_{kU} &\propto n_k\, n_U\;\bar D_k\big(1-\bar D_k\big)
  && \text{treated vs.\ never-treated} \\[2pt]
  s^{\,k}_{kl} &\propto n_k\, n_l\;\big(\bar D_k - \bar D_l\big)\big(1-\bar D_k\big)
  && \text{early vs.\ late, before late switches} \\[2pt]
  s^{\,l}_{kl} &\propto n_k\, n_l\;\bar D_l\big(\bar D_k-\bar D_l\big)
  && \alert{\text{late vs.\ early, after early switches}}
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Every weight has the $\bar D(1-\bar D)$ shape of a Bernoulli variance,
        so \alert{groups treated near the middle of the panel dominate}: the
        weights come from OLS mechanics, not from group sizes alone and not from
        any estimand you chose. Compare Week 1's variance-weighting result: same
        phenomenon, new costume.
  \item The forbidden weight $s^{\,l}_{kl}$ is not negligible in general: it
        grows with $\bar D_k$, i.e.\ with how long the early group has already
        been treated when the late group switches.
  \item All of this is computable from the data alone:
        \texttt{bacondecomp::bacon()} lists every $2\times2$, its estimate, and
        its weight. We decompose a real TWFE estimate in lab.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Where the Negative Weights Come From\adv}
FWL (Week 1) reduces TWFE to a bivariate regression on the
\emph{double-demeaned} treatment. With $\bar D_i$, $\bar D_t$, $\bar D$ the
unit, period, and grand means of $D_{it}$:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta^{\text{TWFE}}
  = \frac{\sum_{it} \tilde D_{it}\, Y_{it}}{\sum_{it} \tilde D_{it}^2},
  \qquad
  \tilde D_{it} = D_{it} - \bar D_i - \bar D_t + \bar D .
\end{align*}
Substitute $Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \tau_{it} D_{it} + \epsilon_{it}$:
the fixed effects are annihilated because $\tilde D$ has zero unit and period
sums, and $\sum_{it}\tilde D_{it}^2 = \sum_{(i,t):D_{it}=1}\tilde D_{it}$, so
\begin{align*}
  \plim\,\hat\beta^{\text{TWFE}}
  = \sum_{(i,t):\,D_{it}=1} w_{it}\,\tau_{it},
  \qquad
  w_{it} = \frac{\tilde D_{it}}{\sum_{(i,t):\,D_{it}=1}\tilde D_{it}},
  \qquad \sum w_{it} = 1 .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item For a \emph{treated} cell, $\tilde D_{it} = 1 - \bar D_i - \bar D_t + \bar D$,
        which is \alert{negative exactly when $\bar D_i + \bar D_t > 1 + \bar D$}:
        a unit treated for most of the panel, observed in a period when most of
        the sample is treated. That is the early adopter, late in the panel.
  \item Intuition: OLS rewards cells whose treatment status is
        \emph{surprising} given their unit and period averages. An early
        adopter's late-period treatment is completely unsurprising, so its
        effect is priced at zero, or below zero.
  \item The weights are a mechanical fact about OLS and are there even under
        constant effects, harmlessly. Heterogeneous $\tau_{it}$ is what turns
        strange weights into a wrong answer.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Negative Weights: Consequences and Diagnostics\adv}
\srct{de Chaisemartin and D'Haultf\oe uille (2020)} generalize the previous
frame's calculation, allowing covariates and non-binary treatments, and show
that negative weights are the rule in staggered designs, not a corner case.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Consequence: TWFE can have the \alert{wrong sign} even when every
        $\tau_{it} > 0$. This is not a small-sample problem; it survives
        $n\to\infty$, because the weights, not the noise, are at fault.
  \item By the formula, the negative weights fall on units treated \emph{early}
        and observed over a \emph{long} post-period, exactly the units carrying
        the most information about long-run effects. TWFE quietly discounts, and
        then inverts, the long run.
  \item \textbf{Diagnostic}: compute the weights
        (\texttt{TwoWayFEWeights::twowayfeweights}) and report how many are
        negative and what share of the total mass they carry. Small negative
        mass is reassuring about \emph{this particular} problem; it says nothing
        about parallel trends or about dynamic-effect contamination, so it is
        one diagnostic, not a validity test.
  \item \srct{Sun and Abraham (2021)} show the same contamination in event-study
        coefficients: with staggered timing, the TWFE $\hat\tau_k$ at lag $k$ is
        a weighted combination of \emph{every} cohort's effects at \emph{every}
        lag, with weights that need not be positive or sum to one lag by lag. So
        apparent pre-trends can be artefacts of post-treatment dynamics, and a
        clean pre-trend plot from raw TWFE proves nothing.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\alert{TWFE is fine when treatment timing is common, or when effects are
genuinely constant.} It is unreliable exactly where sociologists use it most.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Callaway--Sant'Anna: Build Clean Blocks, Then Aggregate}
Define the \alert{group-time average treatment effect} for cohort $g$ at time $t$:
\begin{align*}
  ATT(g,t) = \E\big[Y_t(g) - Y_t(\infty) \mid G_i = g\big] ,
\end{align*}
identified by comparing cohort $g$ to units \emph{not yet treated} at $t$ (or to
never-treated units), using only periods before $g$ as the baseline. Under
cohort-level parallel trends and no anticipation,
\begin{align*}
  ATT(g,t) = \E\big[Y_{t} - Y_{g-1} \mid G_i = g\big]
           - \E\big[Y_{t} - Y_{g-1} \mid \text{not yet treated at } t\big] :
\end{align*}
a clean $2\times2$. The baseline $g-1$ predates cohort $g$'s treatment, the
comparison group has not started treatment by $t$, and no already-treated unit
is ever used as a control.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Under \emph{conditional} parallel trends, each block can be estimated by
        IPW, outcome regression, or a doubly robust estimator, \alert{Week 6,
        reappearing inside a DID}.
  \item \textbf{Then choose an aggregation}, and the choice is an estimand choice:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
    \item \emph{simple}: average over all treated $(g,t)$;
    \item \emph{dynamic}: average by $t-g$, giving an event-study path;
    \item \emph{group}: average within cohort, showing who benefited;
    \item \emph{calendar}: average within period, showing when.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Sun--Abraham reaches the same place through an interaction-weighted
        regression; Borusyak--Jaravel--Spiess by imputing $Y(0)$ from untreated
        cells.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Imputation, Step by Step \srcp{Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess 2024}}
Use only untreated cells to learn the untreated model, then fill in:
\begin{align*}
  \text{1.}&\quad Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \epsilon_{it}\ \text{ on cells with } D_{it} = 0
   && \text{\footnotesize never-treated, and pre-periods of the treated} \\
  \text{2.}&\quad \hat Y_{it}(0) = \hat\alpha_i + \hat\lambda_t\ \text{ for cells with } D_{it} = 1
   && \text{\footnotesize impute the missing potential outcome} \\
  \text{3.}&\quad \hat\tau_{it} = Y_{it} - \hat Y_{it}(0),\quad
   \hat\tau = \textstyle\sum_{D_{it}=1} v_{it}\,\hat\tau_{it}
   && \text{\footnotesize average with weights you choose}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item No treated cell is ever a control; any aggregation ($v_{it}$) is
        allowed, so the estimand is chosen, not inherited from OLS.
  \item \textbf{Efficient} when errors are homoskedastic and serially
        uncorrelated: it uses all pre-periods, not just $g-1$.
  \item \textbf{Pre-trend tests are separate}: run on untreated cells only, so
        testing does not change the estimate.
  \item \srct{Gardner (2022)}'s two-stage DiD is the same idea as two regressions
        (\texttt{did2s}).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Extended TWFE: the Regression Was Not the Problem \srcp{Wooldridge 2025}}
TWFE failed because it forced one $\beta$ on all treated cells. Give every
cohort--period its own coefficient:
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t
   + \sum_{g}\sum_{t\geq g} \tau_{gt}\,\mathds 1\{G_i = g\}\,\mathds 1\{\text{period} = t\}
   + \epsilon_{it} .
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The unit and period effects are then fitted on untreated cells only (every
        treated cell has its own dummy), so $\hat\tau_{gt}$ equals the imputation
        estimate. \alert{Divorce data: both give $-4.85$}.
  \item Adding the pre-period interactions too, against never-treated states,
        reproduces Sun--Abraham and Callaway--Sant'Anna with a universal base:
        \alert{all three give $-10.56$}.
  \item Covariates enter as cohort-by-period interactions (the ``two-way
        Mundlak'' regression). Nonlinear versions (logit, Poisson) follow
        (\texttt{etwfe}).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stacked DiD and Its Weights}
\textbf{Stacking} \srcp{Cengiz, Dube, Lindner, and Zipperer 2019}: for each
adoption cohort, build a sub-experiment (the cohort plus clean controls, in a
fixed window around adoption), stack them, and run one regression with
sub-experiment-specific unit and time effects.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Never uses already-treated controls, and is easy to explain.
  \item \alert{Unweighted stacking does not estimate a clean average}: each
        sub-experiment's treated and control shares differ, so OLS weights
        their trends unequally \srcp{Wing, Freedman, and Hollingsworth 2024}.
        Corrective sample weights recover a ``trimmed aggregate ATT''.
  \item Used in sociology: Walmart Supercenters and unions
        \srcp{Choper, Lehner, and Parolin 2026}; welfare reform and children's
        home environments \srcp{Kalil et al.\ 2023}, both later.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Honest Inference About Pre-Trends}
Rather than testing pre-trends and proceeding as if they had passed,
\srct{Rambachan and Roth (2023)} propose bounding how badly parallel trends could
fail.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Their ``relative magnitudes'' restriction bounds how much the
        \emph{change} in the trend violation after treatment can exceed the
        largest change observed before it, by a factor $\bar M$, and the
        resulting confidence sets account for sampling uncertainty in the
        pre-period estimates rather than treating them as known.
  \item Report the \emph{breakdown value}: how large $\bar M$ would have to be
        before your conclusion reverses.
  \item This turns an untestable assumption into a quantitative claim, exactly
        the move sensitivity analysis made in Week 6.
  \item \texttt{HonestDiD}.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{Inference reminder}
\srct{Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan (2004)}: cluster on the unit at which
treatment varies. With few clusters, use CR2 or a wild cluster bootstrap
(Week 2).
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

over $k = 0,\dots,10$, estimated at $-6.53$ \srcp{figs/w10\_divorce.R, \texttt{HonestDiD}}.

\section[Beyond]{Beyond a Binary, Absorbing Treatment}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Doses: a Continuous Treatment}
\srct{Callaway, Goodman-Bacon, and Sant'Anna (2025)}.
Units receive dose $d$ (minimum-wage increase in dollars, hours of a program);
some get $d = 0$. Two different questions:
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATT}(d\mid d) &= \E[Y_t(d) - Y_t(0)\mid D = d]
   \qquad \text{\footnotesize effect of dose $d$ on those who got $d$} \\
  \text{ATT}(d\mid d) - \text{ATT}(d'\mid d') &= \underbrace{\text{ATT}(d\mid d) - \text{ATT}(d'\mid d)}_{\text{causal: more dose, same units}}
   + \underbrace{\text{ATT}(d'\mid d) - \text{ATT}(d'\mid d')}_{\text{selection: different units}}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Ordinary parallel trends (each dose group vs.\ $d=0$) identifies each
        $\text{ATT}(d\mid d)$, but \alert{not} how effects change with the dose.
  \item Comparing doses needs \textbf{strong parallel trends}: every dose group
        would have trended like any other under every dose. It rules out
        selection on gains.
  \item TWFE on a continuous $D$ mixes both terms with weights that do not
        follow the dose distribution; its weights on level effects sum to zero
        (\texttt{contdid}).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Treatments That Switch On and Off}
Union membership, unemployment, living with a partner: units enter and leave
treatment, and past treatment may still matter.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item TWFE with $D_{it}$ switching assumes no carryover (Imai--Kim) and
        constant effects, and uses switchers as each other's controls.
  \item \srct{de Chaisemartin and D'Haultf\oe uille (2026)}: compare units whose
        treatment first changes at $t$ with units that had the \emph{same}
        treatment history up to $t$ and have not yet changed. The effect of
        having been exposed for $\ell$ periods, $\text{DID}_\ell$, is an event
        study for non-absorbing, non-binary treatments (\texttt{DIDmultiplegtDYN}).
  \item Matching on treatment histories \srct{(Imai, Kim, and Wang 2023)} does
        the same with explicit lag length $L$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
Cost: only units with a matching history count, so fewer comparisons and
wider intervals. That is the price of not assuming carryover away.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Triple Differences}
A policy hits one group (say, mothers) in treated states. Difference the
state DiD for mothers against the state DiD for a group the policy should not
touch (women without children):
\begin{align*}
  \text{DDD} = \text{DiD}_{\text{mothers}} - \text{DiD}_{\text{non-mothers}} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Needs only \textbf{one} parallel-trends assumption: the bias in the two
        DiDs is the same. Neither DiD needs to be unbiased on its own
        \srcp{Olden and Møen 2022}.
  \item With covariates or staggered timing, ``the difference of two DiDs''
        and three-way fixed effects regressions are generally \alert{not}
        valid; pooling not-yet-treated units can add bias even without
        covariates. Use estimators built for DDD
        \srcp{Ortiz-Villavicencio and Sant'Anna 2025; \texttt{triplediff}}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Distributions, and a Design-Based View}
\textbf{Effects on the distribution} \srcp{Callaway and Li 2019}: with three
or more panel periods, distributional parallel trends plus copula stability
(the link between a unit's change in $Y(0)$ and its level stays the same over
time) identify quantile effects on the treated.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Where does uncertainty come from?} Standard errors here usually imagine
resampling units. In policy panels we observe every state.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \srct{Athey and Imbens (2022)}: treat the \emph{adoption date} as the random
        element; TWFE then has a design-based reading, and the usual variance
        is conservative.
  \item \srct{Rambachan and Roth (2026)}: design-based uncertainty for
        quasi-experiments; usual standard errors can remain valid, if
        conservative, for a well-defined estimand.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Factor models} (unit-specific responses to common shocks) relax
parallel trends further: synthetic DiD, generalized synthetic control,
counterfactual estimators (\texttt{fect}). Next week.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Harknett, Schneider, and Irwin (2021)}

  Y_{ict} = \beta_1\,\text{Seattle}_c\times\text{Year1}_t + \beta_2\,\text{Seattle}_c\times\text{Year2}_t
   + \text{city and wave effects} + X_{ict}'\delta + \epsilon_{ict} ,

linear probability models, weighted to the American Community Survey.

\section[Practice]{Practice}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What a Reanalysis of 49 Studies Found \srcp{Chiu, Lan, Liu, and Xu 2026}}
Political scientists reran 49 published panel studies with the
heterogeneity-robust estimators of this lecture.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The new estimators usually agree with TWFE \emph{qualitatively}, but the
        estimates vary a lot across estimators.
  \item Many studies lack evidence for parallel trends once pre-periods are
        examined properly.
  \item Many are underpowered: the robust estimators' intervals are wide.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
Read with the divorce data in mind: the estimator mattered (TWFE vs.\ robust),
and so did the window over which trends are assumed parallel. The practical
guide that collects these steps: \srct{Baker, Callaway, Cunningham,
Goodman-Bacon, and Sant'Anna (2026)}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A DID Checklist\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item State the estimand: ATT for whom, over which post-treatment window?
  \item Plot the raw group means over time. Before any regression.
  \item Is timing staggered? If so, \alert{do not use plain TWFE}. Use a modern
        estimator, and report the TWFE estimate only for comparison.
  \item Report an event-study plot with pre-period coefficients, using
        Sun--Abraham or Callaway--Sant'Anna rather than raw TWFE leads.
  \item Justify the scale (levels or logs) on substantive grounds.
  \item Cluster on the treatment-assignment unit; report the number of clusters.
  \item Report a sensitivity analysis for parallel trends.
  \item Placebo: a pre-treatment outcome, or an untreated outcome that the policy
        should not affect.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking Ahead}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Fixed effects difference away time-invariant confounders, a real gain,
        bought with precision and with a new assumption about trends.
  \item The staggered-adoption literature is a case study in how a method can be
        standard for decades and still be wrong. Read papers published before
        2021 with this in mind, including your own field's classics.
  \item \textbf{Next week}: what to do when there is only \emph{one} treated
        unit, and ``the control group'' has to be constructed.
  \item Notice that conditional parallel trends brought Week 6 back inside a
        DID: once trends are only parallel given covariates, you need a weighting
        or outcome model again, and all of Week 6's cautions return with it.
\end{itemize}
