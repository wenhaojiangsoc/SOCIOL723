\begin{frame}{Several Equations at Once}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.40\linewidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,
    pl/.style={draw=none,fill=white,rectangle,inner sep=1.5pt,minimum size=0pt,font=\normalsize}]
  \node (X) at (0cm,0cm) {$X$}; \node (M) at (1.8cm,1.9cm) {$M$}; \node (Y) at (3.6cm,0cm) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (X)--node[pl,above left=0pt]{$a$}(M);
  \draw[arr] (M)--node[pl,above right=0pt]{$b$}(Y);
  \draw[arr] (X)--node[pl,below=2pt]{$c$}(Y);
  \node[lab] at (0cm,-0.75cm) {father's status};
  \node[lab] at (1.8cm,2.65cm) {education};
  \node[lab] at (3.6cm,-0.75cm) {own status};
\end{tikzpicture}
\vskip4pt
$M_i = a X_i + e_{Mi}$\\[2pt]
$Y_i = c X_i + b M_i + e_{Yi}$
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.58\linewidth}
A \textbf{structural equation model} writes one regression equation for every variable an arrow
points into, and treats the equations as one system.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{OLS is the smallest one}: a single equation, a single outcome.
  \item \textbf{Mediation analysis is one too} (Week 5): the two equations on the left. Direct
        effect $c$, indirect effect $a \times b$, total $c + ab$.
  \item \textbf{More variables, more equations}: Blau and Duncan drew status attainment with five
        variables and three equations. Today's example.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip5pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Later today: variables that are not observed at all (latent variables).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Blau and Duncan (1967)}
\textbf{The question.} Blau and Duncan (1967), \textit{The American Occupational Structure}: how
does a father's position reach his son's? Directly, or through the son's schooling and first job?
\par\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} ``Occupational Changes in a Generation'', a supplement to the March 1962 Current
Population Survey: about 20,700 men aged 20 to 64. Five variables:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item $V$ father's education, $X$ father's occupational status;
  \item $U$ the man's own education, $W$ the status of his first job, $Y$ the status of his
        occupation in 1962.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Why it matters.} The founding study of status-attainment research, and the study that
brought path analysis into sociology. Its answer, that origins work mostly through schooling,
set the agenda of stratification research for decades.
\par\vskip3pt
\textbf{For us.} Everything can be recomputed from the ten correlations they published.
\end{frame}

\end{tikzpicture}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
All variables standardized (mean 0, variance 1):
\begin{align*}
  U_i &= a_1 V_i + a_2 X_i + e_{Ui} \\
  W_i &= b_1 U_i + b_2 X_i + e_{Wi} \\
  Y_i &= c_1 U_i + c_2 X_i + c_3 W_i + e_{Yi}
\end{align*}
\vskip-6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Each error is unrelated to the variables on the right of its equation and to the other
        errors. $V$ and $X$ are correlated; the model does not say why.
  \item \textbf{Two arrows are left out}: $V \to W$ and $V \to Y$. Father's education reaches the
        son's jobs only through his schooling.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Data Are Ten Correlations}
With standardized variables every variance is 1, and the data enter only through the correlations
\srcp{Blau and Duncan 1967, Table 5.1}:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcccc@{}}
\toprule
 & $V$ father's educ. & $X$ father's occ. & $U$ education & $W$ first job \\
\midrule
$X$ father's occupation & 0.516 \\
$U$ own education & 0.453 & 0.438 \\
$W$ first job & 0.332 & 0.417 & 0.538 \\
$Y$ occupation in 1962 & 0.322 & 0.405 & 0.596 & 0.541 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Five variables: $5 \times 4 / 2 = 10$ correlations.
  \item The model has \textbf{8 unknowns}: seven path coefficients
        ($a_1, a_2, b_1, b_2, c_1, c_2, c_3$) and the correlation of $V$ and $X$.
  \item Estimation $=$ find the 8 unknowns that reproduce the 10 correlations.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From an Equation to Correlations}
Take the education equation, $U = a_1 V + a_2 X + e_U$. Multiply both sides by $V$ and average
over men; then do the same with $X$:
\begin{align*}
  E[U V] &= a_1 E[V^2] + a_2 E[X V] + E[e_U V]
    && \text{\footnotesize multiply by $V$, average} \\
  r_{UV} &= a_1 + a_2\, r_{XV}
    && \text{\footnotesize variances are 1; $e_U$ unrelated to $V$} \\
  r_{UX} &= a_1\, r_{XV} + a_2
    && \text{\footnotesize the same with $X$}
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
Put in the observed correlations: two equations, two unknowns.
\begin{align*}
  0.453 &= a_1 + 0.516\, a_2, \qquad 0.438 = 0.516\, a_1 + a_2
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat a_1 = 0.309, \;\; \hat a_2 = 0.278 .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item These are Week 1's normal equations: the answer is the OLS regression of $U$ on $V$ and $X$.
  \item The same steps for the other two equations \srcp{figs/w14\_sem.R}:
        $\hat b_1 = 0.440$, $\hat b_2 = 0.224$; $\hat c_1 = 0.395$, $\hat c_2 = 0.115$,
        $\hat c_3 = 0.281$.
  \item 8 of the 10 correlations are used.
\end{itemize}
\vskip-2pt
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Correlations the Model Did Not Use}
The model also says what the two unused correlations, $r_{WV}$ and $r_{YV}$, must be. Multiply the
first-job equation $W = b_1 U + b_2 X + e_W$ by $V$ and average:
\begin{align*}
  r_{WV} &= b_1\, r_{UV} + b_2\, r_{XV}
    && \text{\footnotesize no arrow $V \to W$} \\
         &= 0.440 \times 0.453 + 0.224 \times 0.516 = 0.315
    && \text{\footnotesize the model's prediction}
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
 & implied by the model & observed & difference \\
\midrule
$r_{WV}$: father's education, first job & 0.315 & 0.332 & 0.017 \\
$r_{YV}$: father's education, occupation 1962 & 0.327 & 0.322 & $-0.005$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item 10 numbers, 8 unknowns: the model is \textbf{overidentified}. The 2 left over test the
        2 arrows left out.
  \item The question for a test of fit: is 0.017 more than sampling noise?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Test of Fit: Is the Gap More Than Chance?}
The model drew no arrow $V \to W$: it claims the coefficient of $V$ in the first-job equation is
exactly 0. The implied $r_{WV}$ equals the observed one only if that claim is right.
\vskip3pt
So test the claim directly. Put each left-out arrow back in and look at its $t$-statistic (Week 1)
\srcp{figs/w14\_sem.R}:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
arrow added & coefficient & s.e. & $t$ \\
\midrule
$V \to W$: father's education $\to$ first job & 0.025 & 0.007 & 3.7 \\
$V \to Y$: father's education $\to$ occupation in 1962 & $-0.014$ & 0.006 & $-2.2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Both $|t| > 1.96$: the gaps are not sampling noise. The model is \textbf{rejected}.
  \item Software reports the same test as one number for all left-out arrows at once, the
        \textbf{chi-square test of fit}: 18.1 with 2 degrees of freedom (one per left-out arrow),
        $p < 0.001$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What a Paper Reports About Fit}
The chi-square grows with $n$ for the same gaps. Papers add two measures of how \emph{large} the
misfit is \srcp{figs/w14\_sem.R; rules of thumb: Hu and Bentler 1999}.
\vskip4pt
\textbf{RMSEA}: the chi-square beyond what chance alone gives (its degrees of freedom), per
degree of freedom, with the sample size taken out.
\vskip-4pt
\[ \text{RMSEA} = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2 - \text{df}}{\text{df} \times n}}
   = \sqrt{\frac{18.1 - 2}{2 \times 20{,}700}} = 0.020
   \qquad \text{\footnotesize (below 0.06 is read as a close fit)} \]
\vskip-4pt
\textbf{SRMR}: the typical gap between an observed and an implied correlation. Five variables
give 15 variances and correlations; the model misses two, by 0.017 and $-0.005$.
\vskip-4pt
\[ \text{SRMR} = \sqrt{\frac{\text{sum of squared gaps}}{15}}
   = \sqrt{\frac{0.017^2 + 0.005^2}{15}} = 0.005
   \qquad \text{\footnotesize (below 0.08)} \]
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The test rejects; both measures say the misfit is negligible.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Rejected, but by How Much?}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item The rejected arrows are tiny. One standard deviation more of father's education moves the
        first job by 0.025 standard deviations, holding the son's education and the father's
        occupation fixed. The arrows the model kept run from 0.115 to 0.440.
  \item With 20,700 men almost any gap is detected (Week 3: a large $n$ rejects everything). The
        same correlations in a sample of 500:
\end{itemize}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
 & $t$ for $V \to W$ & $t$ for $V \to Y$ & chi-square test of fit \\
\midrule
$n = 20{,}700$ & 3.7 & $-2.2$ & 18.1, $p < 0.001$ \\
$n = 500$ & 0.6 & $-0.3$ & 0.4, $p = 0.80$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item The substantive reading stands: father's education reaches the son's jobs almost entirely
        through the son's schooling.
  \item Report the size of the gaps (0.017 and $-0.005$) next to the test, not the $p$-value alone.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}
An unmeasured trait (ability) raises both schooling and the first job: $e_U$ and $e_W$ are
correlated, and OLS of $W$ on $U$ and $X$ is biased.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{In the figure}: a two-headed arrow between the two errors. Its correlation is
        one more unknown.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip5pt
\textbf{Estimation.} Week 7's IV: $V$ is the instrument for $U$, with $X$ as a control. $V$ moves
$U$ and has no arrow into $W$.
\vskip-8pt
\[ \hat b_1 = \frac{\text{reduced form: coefficient of $V$ in $W$ on $V$, $X$}}
                   {\text{first stage: coefficient of $V$ in $U$ on $V$, $X$}}
   = \frac{0.159}{0.309} = 0.515 \]
\vskip-6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item OLS gave 0.440. Software returns the same 0.515 (s.e.\ 0.022) \srcp{figs/w14\_sem.R}.
  \item The price: ``no arrow $V \to W$'' now identifies $b_1$ and is no longer tested. With no
        left-out arrow to use, the model cannot be estimated.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Direct, Indirect, and Total Effects}
An effect along a chain of arrows is the product of its path coefficients; a total effect is the
sum over chains (Week 5). On occupation in 1962 \srcp{figs/w14\_sem.R}:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{own education} & direct, $c_1$ & 0.395 \\
 & through the first job, $b_1 c_3 = 0.440 \times 0.281$ & 0.123 \\
 & total & 0.518 \\
\midrule
\textbf{father's occupation} & direct, $c_2$ & 0.115 \\
 & through education, $a_2 \times 0.518$ & 0.144 \\
 & through the first job alone, $b_2 c_3$ & 0.063 \\
 & total & 0.322 \\
\midrule

\begin{frame}{What Fit Does Not Show}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep8pt
  \item \textbf{Fit is not direction.} Reverse one arrow, occupation in 1962 $\to$ first job. The
        reversed model reproduces the ten correlations exactly as well: same gaps, same test
        (18.1). The correlations cannot tell the two apart. Time order does.
  \item \textbf{Fit is not identification.} ``Errors unrelated to each other'' means no unmeasured
        cause of both education and occupation. Ability is one (Week 5). If it exists, $c_1$ is
        biased, and the fit does not change.
  \item \textbf{The test checks only the arrows left out.} A model with every arrow drawn fits
        perfectly and tests nothing.
  \item So far every variable in the model was observed. Next: a variable that is not.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

One more equation per rating, as in Blau and Duncan. The only difference: $T$ has no data.
\vskip-4pt
\[ A_i = \lambda_A T_i + e_{Ai}, \quad B_i = \lambda_B T_i + e_{Bi}, \quad
   C_i = \lambda_C T_i + e_{Ci}, \quad D_i = \lambda_D T_i + e_{Di} \]
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $T$ is a \textbf{latent variable}, the ratings its \textbf{indicators}, each $\lambda$ a
        \textbf{loading}: how strongly a rating follows $T$.
  \item Each error is unrelated to $T$ and to the other errors. $T$ has no units: its
        variance is set to 1.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the Loadings Come From}
Slide 7's step: multiply the equations for $A$ and $B$, and average over countries.
\begin{align*}
  E[A B] &= \lambda_A \lambda_B E[T^2] + \lambda_A E[T e_B] + \lambda_B E[T e_A] + E[e_A e_B] \\
  r_{AB} &= \lambda_A \lambda_B \qquad \text{\footnotesize (variance of $T$ is 1; errors unrelated)}
\end{align*}
\vskip-6pt
The same for every pair: six observed correlations, four unknown loadings. Take three of them:
\[ \frac{r_{AB}\, r_{AC}}{r_{BC}} = \frac{\lambda_A \lambda_B \cdot \lambda_A \lambda_C}{\lambda_B \lambda_C}
   = \lambda_A^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   \hat\lambda_A = \sqrt{\frac{0.604 \times 0.679}{0.451}} = 0.95 . \]
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Other triples give 0.82 (with $B$, $D$) and 0.92 (with $C$, $D$)
        \srcp{figs/w14\_factor.R}: overidentified, as with Blau and Duncan.
  \item Software uses all six correlations at once: 0.89, 0.69, 0.73, 0.76 for $A$ to $D$. Check:
        $0.89 \times 0.69 = 0.62$, observed 0.60.
  \item \textbf{Test of fit}: do the three answers disagree by more than chance? $6 - 4 = 2$
        degrees of freedom, chi-square 4.0, $p = 0.14$: \textbf{not rejected}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

  \item \textbf{A latent variable} assumes that every correlation among $A$ to $D$ comes from $T$.

\begin{frame}{Why Use a Latent Variable, Then?}
For an index or a control variable, PCA is enough, and simpler (Week 13). A latent variable is
used for two things PCA does not do.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep10pt
  \item \textbf{It can be wrong, so it can be checked.} PCA always returns a first component, even
        from four unrelated columns. The latent variable model has a test of fit: here one
        latent variable for the four ratings was not rejected.
  \item \textbf{$T$ does not have to be a number.} A component is always a scale, from less to more.
        A latent variable can be a set of \emph{types} that differ in kind and cannot be lined
        up on one scale. PCA has no counterpart. This is latent class analysis, next.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Democracy as a Type: Latent Classes}
So far $T$ was a number: more or less democracy. Another view: countries come in \emph{kinds}.
Then $T$ is a type, and the model is \textbf{latent class analysis} (Goodman 1974).
\vskip3pt
The same 75 countries and four ratings, each now split at its median: 1 if a country is rated
high, 0 if low \srcp{figs/w14\_lca.R}.
\begin{center}\small

\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Rated high: 49\%, 41\%, 39\%, and 40\% of countries on $A$ to $D$.
  \item If the four ratings were unrelated, 2 countries would be high on all four. 16 are.
  \item A hypothesis: \textbf{two types} of countries. Nobody recorded which country is which.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Latent Class Model}
Two ingredients:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{How common each type is}: a share $\pi$ of type 1, $1 - \pi$ of type 2.
  \item \textbf{How each type is rated}: $p_{A1}$ is the probability that a type-1 country is rated
        high on $A$; likewise $p_{B1}, \dots, p_{D2}$. Eight numbers.
\end{itemize}
The path-diagram assumption again: given the type, the ratings are unrelated, so their
probabilities multiply. A country is one type or the other, so the two cases add:
\[ \Pr(A = 1, B = 1, C = 1, D = 1) = \pi\, p_{A1}\, p_{B1}\, p_{C1}\, p_{D1}
     + (1 - \pi)\, p_{A2}\, p_{B2}\, p_{C2}\, p_{D2} . \]
\vskip-4pt
For a rating of 0, put $1 - p$ in place of $p$.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Nine unknowns} ($\pi$ and eight $p$'s) imply a probability for each of the 16
        patterns of ratings. The data are the 16 observed counts.
  \item The steps of a real analysis: choose the indicators; fit the model with 1, 2, 3, \dots\
        types; choose the number of types; read and name them; relate them to other variables.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimating the Nine Numbers}
\textbf{Maximum likelihood} (Week 3): choose the nine numbers that make the observed ratings most
likely. Country $i$'s contribution is the probability of its own four ratings:
\[ \log L = \sum_{i=1}^{75} \log \Big[ \pi \Pr(\text{ratings}_i \mid \text{type 1})
     + (1 - \pi) \Pr(\text{ratings}_i \mid \text{type 2}) \Big] . \]
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The logarithm of a \emph{sum}: setting derivatives to zero gives no formula, unlike OLS.
        The maximum has to be found by a search.
  \item \textbf{A general search}, as Newton's method for the logit (Week 4). It works: from our
        starting guess R's \texttt{optim} arrives at $\log L = -157.72$ \srcp{figs/w14\_lca.R}.
  \item A second search, the \textbf{EM algorithm}, reaches the same nine numbers and is what
        latent class software runs. First the result, then EM.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which Country Is Which Type?}
Bayes' rule, with the nine numbers. A country rated high on $A$, $B$, $D$ and low on $C$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{type 1 and these ratings:} \quad & 0.42 \times 0.92 \times 0.78 \times (1 - 0.79) \times 0.89 = 0.0563 \\
  \text{type 2 and these ratings:} \quad & 0.58 \times 0.18 \times 0.15 \times (1 - 0.10) \times 0.05 = 0.0007 \\
  \Pr(\text{type 1} \mid \text{ratings}) &= \frac{0.0563}{0.0563 + 0.0007} = 0.99
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Every country gets a probability, not a label: above 0.95 with three or four high
        ratings, below 0.1 with one or none.
  \item \textbf{To classify}, assign each country to its more likely type: 31 countries to type 1,
        44 to type 2. Report how sharp the assignment is: here 69 of 75 countries have a
        probability above 0.9 for their type.
  \item The two views of $T$ agree: the probability of type 1 correlates 0.85 with the numeric
        score of the factor model.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Second Search: Two Easy Problems}
The EM algorithm reaches the same nine numbers without a general optimizer. It starts from the
observation that the hard problem splits into two easy ones.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{If we knew the nine numbers}, Bayes' rule gives each country's probability of being
        type 1: the last slide. It works for any nine numbers, not only the final ones. Say
        $\pi = 0.5$, type 1 is rated high 60\% of the time, type 2 40\%. For a country with four
        high ratings:
        \[ \Pr(\text{type 1} \mid 1,1,1,1) =
           \frac{0.5 \times 0.6^4}{0.5 \times 0.6^4 + 0.5 \times 0.4^4} = 0.84 . \]
  \item \textbf{If we knew each country's type}, the nine numbers are counting. $\pi$ is the share
        of countries in type 1. The probability for rating $A$ in type 1 is the share of type-1
        countries rated high on $A$.
  \item We know neither. Each problem is easy given the answer to the other.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The EM Algorithm: Guess, Then Alternate}
Only the nine numbers are guessed, once, at the start. Then two steps repeat, each using the
other's output (Dempster, Laird, and Rubin 1977).
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{E step}. \emph{Compute} each country's probability of being type 1, $g_i$, from the
        current nine numbers by Bayes' rule. Nothing is guessed here.
  \item \textbf{M step}. Recompute the nine numbers by counting. With known types a country
        would count 1 toward its own type, 0 toward the other. Now it counts $g_i$ toward type 1
        and $1 - g_i$ toward type 2.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
The M step on three hypothetical countries:

\midrule

3 & 0.1 & 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.68\linewidth}
\small
Countries in type 1: $\sum_i g_i = 0.9 + 0.5 + 0.1 = 1.5$ of 3, so $\pi = 0.5$.\\[4pt]
Of these, rated high on $A$: $\sum_i g_i A_i = 0.9 + 0.5 = 1.4$, so\\
$\Pr(A = 1 \mid \text{type 1}) = \sum_i g_i A_i / \sum_i g_i = 1.4 / 1.5 = 0.93$.\\[4pt]
Type 2, with $1 - g_i$ in place of $g_i$: $(0.1 + 0.5) / 1.5 = 0.40$.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{EM: Where It Is Used, and What to Watch}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item \textbf{The same two steps work whenever a piece of the data is missing}: fill it in by
        its expected value given the current estimates, re-estimate as if the data were complete,
        repeat. Latent classes, factor models and structural equation models with latent
        variables, mixtures, item nonresponse, the random effects of Week 4.
  \item \textbf{Starting values.} EM climbs to the nearest peak of the likelihood, which need not
        be the highest. Run it from several random starts and keep the best. Here ten random
        starts end at the same answer.
  \item \textbf{The labels are arbitrary}: ``type 1'' and ``type 2'' can swap. The number of types
        is chosen by fit and BIC (Week 3).
  \item \textbf{Fit is not existence.} Two types reproduce the counts. So could a continuous
        trait. The types summarize how answers go together.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Treatments That Unfold Over Time}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.44\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\msmdag{}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}
A child can live in a poor neighborhood in childhood ($D_1$), in adolescence ($D_2$), both, or
neither. In between, family income $L$ changes. $Y$: the child's test score at the end.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $L$ is a \textbf{confounder} of $D_2$: income decides where the family lives next, and
        affects the test score.
  \item $L$ is also a \textbf{consequence} of $D_1$: a poor neighborhood lowers parents' earnings.
  \item $U$: family traits nobody measured. They affect income and the test score.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The same structure: poverty spells and health, marriage and wages (Week 10).
  \item Week 5: control for confounders; do not control for what the treatment causes. Here $L$ is both.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Target: the Effect of a Whole History}
$Y_i(d_1, d_2)$: child $i$'s test score if the neighborhood history were set to $(d_1, d_2)$.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{Effect of $D_1$}: $E[Y(1, d_2) - Y(0, d_2)]$, everything a poor neighborhood in
        childhood does, including what it does by lowering the family's later income.
  \item \textbf{Effect of $D_2$}: $E[Y(d_1, 1) - Y(d_1, 0)]$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
To see what each method recovers, a simulation where the truth is known \srcp{figs/w14\_msm.R}:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item $D_1$ is a coin flip. It lowers the score by 2 directly. It also raises the share of
        low-income families from 30\% to 50\%, and low income costs 5 points: $5 \times 0.2 = 1$
        more. \textbf{True effect of $D_1$: $-3$.}
  \item Low-income families are far more likely to live in a poor neighborhood in adolescence.
        \textbf{True effect of $D_2$: $-2$.}
  \item $U$ lowers income and lowers the score. It is not in the data.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Leaving Income Out: Confounding}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.44\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\msmdag{\draw[hot] (L) -- (A2); \draw[hot] (L) to[bend left=34] (Y);
        \draw[hot] (U) -- (L); \draw[hot] (U) -- (Y);}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}
Regress $Y$ on $D_1$ and $D_2$ only.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Two back-door paths into $D_2$ are open (orange):
        $D_2 \leftarrow L \to Y$ and $D_2 \leftarrow L \leftarrow U \to Y$.
  \item Low-income families end up in poor neighborhoods, and their children would score lower
        wherever they lived.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The coefficient of $D_2$ mixes the neighborhood's effect with the effect of income.
  \item Week 5's answer: close the back door by controlling for $L$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Controlling for Income, Problem 1: Over-Control}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.44\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\msmdag{\draw[hot] (A1) -- (L); \draw[hot] (L) to[bend left=34] (Y);}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}
Regress $Y$ on $D_1$, $D_2$, and $L$.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item $D_2$ is repaired: with $L$ held fixed, both of its back doors are closed.
  \item But $L$ sits on a causal path of $D_1$ (orange): $D_1 \to L \to Y$.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Holding income fixed compares children whose families ended up with the \emph{same} income.
        The part of $D_1$'s effect that works by lowering income is removed.
  \item What is left is the direct effect of $D_1$, not the effect of $D_1$ (Week 5: do not control
        for a mediator).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Controlling for Income, Problem 2: A Collider}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.44\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\msmdag{\draw[hot] (A1) -- (L); \draw[hot] (U) -- (L); \draw[hot] (U) -- (Y);
        \draw[<->,accent,dashed,line width=1.2pt] (A1) to[bend left=30] (U);}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}
Two arrows point into $L$: from $D_1$ and from $U$. $L$ is a \textbf{collider} (Week 5).
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Holding a collider fixed makes its two causes related (dashed).
  \item A back door opens that did not exist: $D_1 \to L \leftarrow U \to Y$.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Take two low-income families. One lived in a poor neighborhood: that explains its low
        income. The other did not: something else must explain it, more often the unmeasured
        disadvantage $U$.
  \item So among families with the same income, those from poor neighborhoods have \emph{less}
        unmeasured disadvantage, and $D_1$ looks less harmful than it is.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the Two Regressions Give}
The simulation, in a very large sample \srcp{figs/w14\_msm.R}:

  \item With $L$: $D_2$ is right. $D_1$ is wrong twice: over-control takes it from $-3$ to

        fixed.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

Week 6's inverse probability weight, applied to the second treatment
\srcp{Robins, Hern\'an, and Brumback 2000}:
\[ w_i = \frac{1}{\Pr(D_2 = d_{2i} \mid D_{1i}, L_i)} \]
One over the probability of the neighborhood the child had, given the past (a logit).

\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item 80\% of low-income families live in a poor neighborhood. The 20\% who do not count
        $1 / 0.2 = 5$ times each, the others $1.25$ times: after weighting, half and half.
  \item Nothing in the past predicts $D_2$ any more: the arrows $L \to D_2$ and $D_1 \to D_2$
        are gone, and both back doors are closed.
  \item $L$ is never held fixed: $D_1 \to L \to Y$ stays in $D_1$'s effect, the collider stays closed.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Marginal Structural Model}
A model for the potential-outcome means, fitted by \emph{weighted} regression of $Y$ on the
treatments, without $L$:
\[ E[Y(d_1, d_2)] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 d_1 + \beta_2 d_2 . \]
\vskip-8pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
 & coefficient of $D_1$ & coefficient of $D_2$ \\
\midrule
truth & $-3.0$ & $-2.0$ \\
regression of $Y$ on $D_1$, $D_2$ & $-2.2$ & $-5.6$ \\
regression of $Y$ on $D_1$, $D_2$, $L$ & $-1.7$ & $-2.0$ \\
\textbf{weighted} regression of $Y$ on $D_1$, $D_2$ & $-3.0$ & $-2.0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Structural}: a model for potential outcomes. \textbf{Marginal}: for the whole
        population, not within levels of $L$.
  \item $L$ is used only to build the weights. It never enters the outcome regression.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Is This Only Week 6's Weighting Under a New Name?}
\textbf{With one period, yes.} $E[Y(d)] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 d$, fitted with weights
$1 / \Pr(D = d \mid X)$, is Week 6's weighting estimator of the ATE.
\par\vskip3pt
With a treatment over time, the name covers two separate things:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{The weights} are the estimator: Week 6's weight, once per period, multiplied.
        New: the product, the stabilizing, an assumption that must hold in every period.
  \item \textbf{The marginal structural model} is the target: a model for how the mean potential
        outcome depends on the \emph{whole history}. Week 6 needed none: a 0/1 treatment has two
        means. Sixteen yearly quintiles have $5^{16}$ histories. Nobody can estimate a mean for
        each, so histories are summarized: a coefficient per period, or the average exposure.
\end{itemize}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item That summary is a modeling choice. It can be wrong even when the weights are right.
  \item Weighting is one way to fit the model. Week 6's outcome modeling, period by
        period (the g-formula), is another \srcp{Hern\'an and Robins 2020}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Many Periods: One Weight per Period, Multiplied}
Periods $k = 1, \dots, K$. Write $\bar D_{k-1}$ for the treatments before period $k$ and $\bar L_k$
for the covariates up to period $k$. The weight is the product of the period weights:
\[ w_i = \prod_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{\Pr(D_k = d_{ki} \mid \bar D_{k-1}, \bar L_k)} . \]
\vskip-4pt
What must hold, period by period:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Sequential ignorability}: given the measured past, each period's treatment is as
        good as random. $U$ may affect $L$ and $Y$, but not any $D_k$ directly.
  \item \textbf{Overlap}: every history has some chance of each treatment (Week 6).
  \item \textbf{A correct model for the treatment} in each period.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
The practical problem: a product of many numbers like 5 is enormous. A handful of children with
unusual histories carry the whole estimate. The remedy is on the next two slides.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stabilized Weights}
Replace the 1 in the numerator by the probability of the same treatment given the
\emph{treatment history only}:
\[ sw_i = \prod_{k=1}^{K}
   \frac{\Pr(D_k = d_{ki} \mid \bar D_{k-1})}{\Pr(D_k = d_{ki} \mid \bar D_{k-1}, \bar L_k)} . \]
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{Numerator}: how common the child's neighborhood is among all children with the
        same neighborhood history.
  \item \textbf{Denominator}: how common it is among those who also share the family's income
        history.
  \item $sw_i > 1$: the family lives where families \emph{of its income} rarely live.
        $sw_i < 1$: where they usually live.
  \item If income did not affect where families live, numerator $=$ denominator, and every weight
        is 1: the unweighted regression.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{column}{0.56\linewidth}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item The \textbf{denominator} removes $L \to D_2$, as before.
  \item The \textbf{numerator}, a function of $D_1$ only, puts $D_1 \to D_2$ back (orange).
  \item \textbf{No harm}: $D_1$ is in the outcome regression, which closes
        $D_2 \leftarrow D_1 \to Y$.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip8pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item \textbf{The gain}: the weight keeps only what $L$ adds. If 95\% of families stay put
        whatever their income, a family that moves has plain weight $1 / 0.05 = 20$ and
        stabilized weight $0.05 / 0.05 = 1$.
  \item \textbf{The rule}: the numerator may use only what is in the outcome regression. $L$ is
        not, and must not be.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Wodtke, Harding, and Elwert (2011)}
\textbf{The question.} Does growing up in a disadvantaged neighborhood lower high school
graduation? Earlier studies measured the neighborhood once, and controlled for family income and
employment.
\par\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} Panel Study of Income Dynamics: 834 Black and 1,259 non-Black children, followed
every year from age 1 to 17. Outcome: high school graduation by age 20.
\par\vskip2pt
\textbf{The treatment} $D_k$, every year from age 2 to 17: the census tract's disadvantage, in
national quintiles, 5 the most disadvantaged.
\par\vskip3pt
\textbf{Time-varying confounders} $L_k$, every year: the household head's marital status,
employment, and work hours; family income, family size, homeownership, welfare receipt.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Families move when a job is lost or a marriage ends: $L_k \to D_k$. The neighborhood
        shapes the parents' jobs and income in turn: $D_{k-1} \to L_k$.
  \item The two-period DAG, sixteen times over.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Wodtke et al.: the Method}
\textbf{Weights.} For each child, sixteen yearly ratios multiplied, with the covariates fixed at
birth $L_0$ in both numerator and denominator:
\[ sw_i = \prod_{k=1}^{16}
   \frac{\Pr(D_k = d_{ki} \mid \bar D_{k-1}, L_0)}{\Pr(D_k = d_{ki} \mid \bar D_{k-1}, \bar L_k)} . \]
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Each probability: an ordered logit (Week 4) of this year's quintile.
  \item The weights average 1.04 and 1.00, with 99th percentiles of 4.8 and 3.0
        \srcp{their Table 4}.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{The marginal structural model.} $5^{16}$ possible histories
$\bar d = (d_1, \dots, d_{16})$; a history matters through its average:
\[ \text{logit} \Pr\big(Y(\bar d) = 1\big) = \theta_0 + \theta_1 \cdot \frac{1}{16} \sum_{k=1}^{16} d_k . \]
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Lowercase $d_k$: a history that could be set, lived or not. Fitted as a weighted logit of
        graduation on each child's observed average quintile, by race.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Starting From a Question: Control Over Childbearing}
\textbf{The question.} What happens to women's lives when they gain, or lose, control over
whether and when to have a child? Women who used the pill or had an abortion differ from other
women in much else, and so do places with easy and with difficult access. Quantitative
scholars seeking causal inference looked for shocks to access:
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.02}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.0cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.7cm}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{5.8cm}@{}}
\toprule
study & the shock & finding \\
\midrule
Goldin and Katz (2002) & states lowered the age for the pill without parental consent, at different
  dates, 1969--74 & college women married later; the timing matches women's entry into law and
  medicine \\
Myers (2017) & the same laws, recoded, and access to legal abortion & abortion access, not
  the pill, delayed marriage and first births \\
Fischer, Royer, White (2018) & Texas laws of 2011--13 closed clinics & counties left with no
  provider within 50 miles: abortions $-20.5$\%, births $+2.6$\% \\
Dench et al.\ (2024) & state bans after \textit{Dobbs} & births $+2.3$\% in ban
  states, first half of 2023 (synthetic DiD, Week 11) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip-2pt
{\small The question came first. Each shock was found because someone was looking for it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Starting From a Puzzle in Reading}
A question often arrives as a doubt while reading: a control that looks like a mediator, a
result at odds with another paper.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Myers (2017)} reread Goldin and Katz. Studies had coded the state pill laws
        differently in 35 of 51 jurisdictions. Recoded, the pill's effect on early marriage
        disappears; legal abortion is what delayed marriage and births.
  \item \textbf{Ludwig and Br\"uderl (2018)}, Week 10: does marriage raise men's wages, or do men on
        steeper wage paths marry? With each man's own wage path, almost no premium is left.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{This has never been easier.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item With public data and code, an AI assistant can reproduce a paper's main table and change
        the one choice you doubt in an afternoon.
  \item What stays scarce is the hunch: reading closely enough to notice what is wrong.
  \item A failed replication is not yet a paper. The contribution is what is true instead.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Question Can Be Descriptive}
A natural experiment answers a narrow ``what if''. Someone has to establish what is there, and
for whom.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Chetty, Hendren, Kline, and Saez (2014).} Tax records of 40 million children. A
        child from the bottom fifth reaches the top fifth 4.4\% of the time in Charlotte and
        12.9\% in San Jose.
  \item \textbf{Chetty, Grusky, et al.\ (2017).} 90\% of children born in 1940 earned more than
        their parents; of those born in the 1980s, 50\%.
  \item \textbf{Nilforoshan et al.\ (2023)}, \textit{Nature}. Mobile phones of 9.6 million people:
        whom people of different incomes cross paths with. Large cities are more segregated than
        small ones, not less.
  \item \textbf{Pettit and Western (2004).} Of men born 1965--69, 3\% of white and 20\% of Black men
        had been to prison by their early thirties.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
Description makes the causal questions: Pettit and Western's numbers are why Pager's (2003)
audit of criminal records and hiring had to be done.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Starting From a Method: Dependency Parsing}
\textbf{The method, from linguistics.} A dependency parser attaches every word of a sentence to
the word it depends on, and labels the link.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1.9cm, every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node (w1) at (0,0) {The};      \node (w2) at (1,0) {officer};
  \node (w3) at (2,0) {arrested}; \node (w4) at (3,0) {the}; \node (w5) at (4,0) {man};
  \draw[-{Stealth}, thick, dukeblue] (w3.north) to[out=150, in=30]
        node[above, font=\scriptsize] {subject} (w2.north);
  \draw[-{Stealth}, thick, dukeblue] (w3.north) to[out=30, in=150]
        node[above, font=\scriptsize] {object} (w5.north);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-8pt
Word counts read ``the man arrested the officer'' as the same text.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Stuhler (2022)}, \textit{Sociological Methods \& Research}: rules that turn a
        parser's output into what an actor does, what is done to it, and how it is described.
  \item \textbf{Stuhler (2024)}, \textit{PNAS}: 87,531 works of fiction, 1850--2010. In
        relations between a woman and a man, the woman is more often the one acted upon. The
        gap narrowed after the 19th century but remains, and is wider for male authors.
  \item The parser came first. It mattered because it was tied to an old question: who is
        shown as acting.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Starting From New Data: Credit Records}
Credit bureaus record the loans, cards, and missed payments of most American adults. Surveys never
measured debt this well.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Rhodes et al.\ (2026)}, \textit{ASR}. A large national sample of credit records,
        linked to state safety-net policy (two-way fixed effects, Week 10). A more generous
        safety net matters less for \emph{whether} low-income households borrow than for
        \emph{what} they borrow: fewer payday loans, more mainstream credit cards.
  \item \textbf{Miller, Wherry, and Foster (2023)} linked the women of the Turnaway Study, some
        of whom were just past a clinic's gestational limit and turned away, to their credit
        reports. Being denied an abortion raised past-due debt, bankruptcies, and evictions.
        The abortion question again, answered with data nobody had used for it.
  \item The risk of starting from data: a paper about what the records happen to contain. Both
        papers work because a question was put to the data.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When the Method or the Data Come First}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item \textbf{It works when the tool unlocks a question people already had.} Who acts and who
        is acted upon in fiction was argued for decades from close readings of a few novels; a
        parser could count it in 87,531. What a lost safety net does to household debt could not be seen in
        surveys; credit records show it.
  \item \textbf{It fails when the question is chosen because the tool fits.} The paper answers
        something nobody asked, and its interest ends when the tool stops being new.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Only the Question Defines You}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item \textbf{Methods do not last.} A new method is a default option in software within a few
        years. Whoever is known for a tool competes with every cohort trained on the next one,
        and starts over when the tool becomes ordinary.
  \item \textbf{Data do not last.} Exclusive data become public, or are replaced by better data.
        Access is not a contribution.
  \item \textbf{A question lasts.} Pettit and Western kept working on incarceration, Desmond on
        eviction, Myers on access to abortion. The methods changed from paper to paper; the
        question did not.
  \item \textbf{The question is also where designs and data come from.} People find a shock, or
        think of linking two files, because they have lived with one question long enough to
        know its institutions.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Keeping One Identity}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{A test.} Say what you study in one sentence that names no method and no dataset.
        ``I use machine learning on administrative data'' fails. ``I study why families lose
        their housing'' passes.
  \item \textbf{New data and new methods keep arriving.} Choose among them: take up the ones
        that fit your identity.
  \item \textbf{Choose a question large enough for ten years} and specific enough for a first
        paper. ``Inequality'' is a field. ``How eviction shapes where poor families live'' is a
        question.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
\begin{alertblock}{For the years ahead}
This course gave you methods. They are what you use. The question is who you are as a
researcher.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}
