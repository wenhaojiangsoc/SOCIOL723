\section[Why Likelihood]{Why We Need More Than Least Squares}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Ways to Justify an Estimator}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Design / agnostic}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep3pt
  \item Start with the CEF. $\beta$ is defined as the best linear approximation
        to it, whatever the world looks like.
  \item Assumes almost nothing about how $Y$ was generated.
  \item This was Weeks 1--2.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{Model-based}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep3pt
  \item Write down a full \emph{probability model} for how $Y$ was generated,
        with unknown parameters.
  \item Estimate the parameters that make the observed data most probable.
  \item This is maximum likelihood, and it is Weeks 3--4.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip8pt
\begin{block}{Neither is ``the right one''}
The design-based view is safer for causal estimands. The model-based view buys
you things the agnostic view cannot deliver: predictions on the right support,
structural parameters, latent variables, and outcomes that are not real numbers.
Be pragmatic, and be explicit about which you are using.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{OLS Is Already a Maximum Likelihood Estimator\core}
Suppose we assume the full model
\begin{align*}
  Y_i = X_i'\bm\beta + \epsilon_i , \qquad
  \epsilon_i \overset{iid}{\sim} \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  Y_i \sim \mathcal N(X_i'\bm\beta,\ \sigma^2) .
\end{align*}
Then the probability of the observed sample, as a function of the parameters, is
\begin{align*}
  \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}
  \exp\!\left(-\frac{(Y_i - X_i'\bm\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) .
\end{align*}
\pause
Taking logs, everything except $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i(Y_i - X_i'\bm\beta)^2$
is free of $\bm\beta$. \alert{Maximizing over $\bm\beta$ is exactly minimizing the
sum of squared residuals.}
\vskip4pt
So OLS under normality \emph{is} the MLE. What we do today is take the same idea
and apply it when the outcome is not normal.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why We Need the Generalization}
The normal linear model assumes $Y_i \in \R$ with constant variance. Sociological
outcomes are often not like that.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Binary}: employed or not, voted or not, married or not. A linear
        model can predict probabilities below 0 and above 1.
  \item \textbf{Counts}: number of children, arrests, publications. Non-negative
        integers, right-skewed, variance tied to the mean.
  \item \textbf{Categorical}: occupation, party, religious affiliation. There is
        no meaningful numeric coding of $\{$professional, service, manual$\}$.
  \item \textbf{Ordered}: strongly agree $\dots$ strongly disagree. The gaps
        between categories are not known.
  \item \textbf{Durations, censored, truncated}: time to first birth; income
        top-coded at \$$200{,}000$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
Maximum likelihood handles all of these, and is the engine behind almost every
model in the sociological literature.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where We Are Going}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{The likelihood}: what it is, and the crucial distinction between
        probability and likelihood.
  \item \textbf{Finding the maximum}: the score function, the first-order
        condition, and worked examples you can do on paper.
  \item \textbf{Information}: how the \emph{curvature} of the log-likelihood tells
        you how precise your estimate is.
  \item \textbf{Properties}: consistency, asymptotic normality, efficiency,
        invariance, and what can go wrong.
  \item \textbf{Testing}: the likelihood ratio, Wald, and score tests, which are
        three ways of measuring the same distance.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip6pt
As in Weeks 1--2, everything examinable is done in the \alert{one-parameter
scalar case}. Frames marked \core{} are the essential core; frames marked
\adv{} generalize.
\end{frame}

\section[The Likelihood]{The Likelihood Function}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Probability Versus Likelihood\core}
Both start from the same object, the density (or mass function) $f(y\mid\theta)$.
What differs is \emph{what you hold fixed}.
\vskip2pt
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Probability}
\begin{align*}
  f(y\mid\theta)
\end{align*}
$\theta$ is \emph{fixed and known}; $y$ varies.\\[4pt]
``If the coin is fair, how likely am I to see 7 heads in 10 tosses?''
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{Likelihood}
\begin{align*}
  \lik(\theta;y) \equiv f(y\mid\theta)
\end{align*}
$y$ is \emph{fixed} (it is your data); $\theta$ varies.\\[4pt]
``Given that I saw 7 heads in 10 tosses, which values of $\theta$ make that
outcome most probable?''
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{The single most important sentence today}
The likelihood is \emph{the same formula read backwards}: a function of the
parameter, with the data nailed down. It is \alert{not} a probability
distribution over $\theta$; it does not integrate to one, and
$\lik(\theta)$ is not ``the probability that $\theta$ is true.''
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A First Example: Marbles in a Bag}
An opaque bag contains blue and white marbles. You want the proportion $\theta$
that are blue. You draw 10 marbles with replacement and get 7 blue.
\vskip4pt
The number of blue draws is Binomial$(10,\theta)$, so
\begin{align*}
  \lik(\theta) = \Pr(X = 7\mid\theta) = \binom{10}{7}\theta^{7}(1-\theta)^{3} .
\end{align*}
\pause
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Try $\theta = 0.3$: $\lik = 120(0.3)^7(0.7)^3 \approx 0.0090$.
  \item Try $\theta = 0.5$: $\lik = 120(0.5)^{10} \approx 0.117$.
  \item Try $\theta = 0.7$: $\lik = 120(0.7)^7(0.3)^3 \approx 0.267$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
Among these, $\theta = 0.7$ makes the data we actually saw most probable. The MLE
is the value that does best among \emph{all} $\theta\in[0,1]$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Likelihood, Plotted}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1]
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (9.3,0) node[right]{\small $\theta$};
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,3.4) node[above]{\small $\lik(\theta)$};
  \foreach \t/\lab in {0/0, 2/0.25, 4/0.5, 6/0.75, 8/1}
    \draw (\t,0) -- (\t,-0.08) node[below]{\scriptsize \lab};
  % binomial(10,7) likelihood, scaled: peak 0.2668 at 0.7 -> y=3
  \draw[dukeblue,very thick,smooth,samples=100,domain=0.02:0.98,variable=\x]
    plot ({8*\x},{3*120*(\x)^7*(1-\x)^3/0.26683});
  \draw[accent,dashed,thick] (5.6,0) -- (5.6,3);
  \node[accent] at (6.5,3.2) {\small $\hat\theta = 0.7$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The height at any $\theta$ is the probability of \emph{these} data if that
        $\theta$ were true.
  \item The peak is the MLE. The \alert{width} of the peak will turn out to be our
        measure of uncertainty; that is the whole of Section 4.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From One Observation to Many\core}
With $n$ observations we need the \emph{joint} density
$f(y_1,\dots,y_n\mid\theta)$. If the observations are independent given $\theta$,
it factors:
\begin{align*}
  \lik(\theta; y_1,\dots,y_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(y_i \mid \theta) .
\end{align*}
\pause
Products of many small numbers underflow and are painful to differentiate, so we
take logs:
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;\loglik(\theta) \equiv \log \lik(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(y_i\mid\theta)\;}
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\log$ is strictly increasing, so it \alert{does not move the maximum}.
  \item A product becomes a sum, so the derivative is a sum, which is why every
        MLE formula looks like a sample average.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Example 1: Bernoulli\core}
Let $Y_i \in \{0,1\}$ with $\Pr(Y_i=1)=\theta$. The mass function written as a
single formula:
\begin{align*}
  f(y_i\mid\theta) = \theta^{y_i}(1-\theta)^{1-y_i} .
\end{align*}
(Check: at $y_i = 1$ it gives $\theta$; at $y_i = 0$ it gives $1-\theta$.)
\vskip4pt
\begin{align*}
  \lik(\theta)
  &= \prod_{i=1}^n \theta^{y_i}(1-\theta)^{1-y_i}
   = \theta^{\sum_i y_i}(1-\theta)^{n - \sum_i y_i} , \\[3pt]
  \loglik(\theta)
  &= \Big(\textstyle\sum_i y_i\Big)\log\theta
   + \Big(n - \textstyle\sum_i y_i\Big)\log(1-\theta) .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
Notice that the data enter only through $\sum_i y_i$: it is a
\emph{sufficient statistic}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Example 2: Poisson}
Let $Y_i$ be a count with $\Pr(Y_i = y\mid\theta) = \dfrac{\theta^{y}e^{-\theta}}{y!}$.
\begin{align*}
  \loglik(\theta)
  &= \sum_{i=1}^n \log\!\left(\frac{\theta^{y_i}e^{-\theta}}{y_i!}\right)
   = \sum_{i=1}^n \Big( y_i\log\theta - \theta - \log(y_i!)\Big) \\[3pt]
  &= \log(\theta)\sum_{i} y_i \;-\; n\theta \;-\; \sum_i \log(y_i!) .
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The last term does not involve $\theta$ at all. It is an
        \emph{additive constant} and can be dropped for maximization, but not
        if you want to compare log-likelihood \emph{values} across models.
  \item Again the data enter only through $\sum_i y_i$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked Example 3: Normal}
Let $Y_i \sim \mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, two unknown parameters.
\begin{align*}
  f(y_i\mid\mu,\sigma^2)
  = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
\end{align*}
so
\begin{align*}
  \loglik(\mu,\sigma^2)
  = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi) - \frac{n}{2}\log\sigma^2
    - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i-\mu)^2 .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}
  \item Look at the last term: for fixed $\sigma^2$, maximizing over $\mu$ means
        \alert{minimizing $\sum_i(y_i-\mu)^2$}. Least squares falls out of the
        normal likelihood, not the other way round.
  \item Replacing $\mu$ with $X_i'\bm\beta$ gives the linear regression model.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Finding the MLE]{Finding the Maximum}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Score Function\core}
\begin{block}{Definition}
The \alert{score} is the derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to the
parameter:
\begin{align*}
  S(\theta) \;\equiv\; \frac{\partial \loglik(\theta)}{\partial\theta}
  \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{\partial \log f(y_i\mid\theta)}{\partial \theta} .
\end{align*}
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The MLE $\hat\theta$ solves $S(\hat\theta) = 0$, the
        \alert{first-order condition}, exactly as the normal equations were the
        FOC for least squares.
  \item Second-order condition: $\partial^2\loglik/\partial\theta^2 < 0$ at
        $\hat\theta$, so that we have a maximum rather than a minimum.
  \item A key population fact: $\E[S(\theta_0)] = 0$ at the true parameter. The
        score is a \emph{mean-zero} random variable, which is why the CLT will
        apply to it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bernoulli: Deriving $\hat\theta$\core}
\begin{align*}
  \loglik(\theta) &= \Big(\textstyle\sum_i y_i\Big)\log\theta
   + \Big(n - \textstyle\sum_i y_i\Big)\log(1-\theta) \\[4pt]
  S(\theta) &= \frac{\sum_i y_i}{\theta} - \frac{n - \sum_i y_i}{1-\theta}
  \ \overset{!}{=}\ 0 .
\end{align*}
\pause
Multiply through by $\theta(1-\theta)$:
\begin{align*}
  (1-\theta)\sum_i y_i - \theta\Big(n - \sum_i y_i\Big) &= 0 \\
  \sum_i y_i - \theta\sum_i y_i - n\theta + \theta\sum_i y_i &= 0 \\
  \boxed{\;\hat\theta = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i = \bar y\;}
\end{align*}
The MLE of a probability is the sample proportion, derived, not assumed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Poisson: Deriving $\hat\theta$}
\begin{align*}
  \loglik(\theta) &= \log(\theta)\sum_i y_i - n\theta - \sum_i\log(y_i!) \\[4pt]
  S(\theta) &= \frac{\sum_i y_i}{\theta} - n \ \overset{!}{=}\ 0
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \boxed{\;\hat\theta = \bar y\;}
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
Check the second-order condition:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial^2 \loglik}{\partial\theta^2} = -\frac{\sum_i y_i}{\theta^2} < 0 ,
\end{align*}
so we really have a maximum. \\[4pt]
Both examples give $\bar y$, which is not a coincidence: for these distributions
the mean \emph{is} the parameter.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Normal: Deriving $\hat\mu$ and $\hat\sigma^2$}
Two parameters, so two first-order conditions.
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial\loglik}{\partial\mu}
  &= \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - \mu) \overset{!}{=} 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat\mu = \bar y . \\[6pt]
  \frac{\partial\loglik}{\partial\sigma^2}
  &= -\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i (y_i-\mu)^2 \overset{!}{=} 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \hat\sigma^2 = \frac1n\sum_i (y_i - \hat\mu)^2 .
\end{align*}
\pause
\begin{alertblock}{The MLE of $\sigma^2$ is biased}
It divides by $n$, not $n-1$. So $\E[\hat\sigma^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2 < \sigma^2$.
\end{alertblock}
This is our first warning: \alert{maximum likelihood does not promise
unbiasedness}. What it promises is good behaviour as $n$ grows.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When There Is No Closed Form}
Most likelihoods you will meet, logit, probit, negative binomial, anything
with covariates, have no algebraic solution to $S(\theta)=0$. We search
numerically.
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{Newton--Raphson}
Start at $\theta^{(0)}$ and iterate
\begin{align*}
  \theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)} - \left[\frac{\partial^2\loglik}{\partial\theta^2}
  \bigg|_{\theta^{(t)}}\right]^{-1} S(\theta^{(t)}) ,
\end{align*}
i.e.\ take a step whose size is the score divided by the curvature. Steep
curvature $\Rightarrow$ small confident steps; flat $\Rightarrow$ large steps.
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
Variants you will meet: \textbf{BFGS} (approximates the second derivative),
\textbf{Fisher scoring} (uses the expected rather than observed second
derivative; this is what \texttt{glm()} does, as iteratively reweighted least
squares).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Practical Matters in \texttt{R}}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \texttt{optim()} \alert{minimizes}. MLE is a maximization problem, so you
        pass it the \emph{negative} log-likelihood.
  \item Supply \texttt{par}, the starting values. Poor starting values are the
        most common cause of failed convergence.
  \item Always check the convergence code (\texttt{\$convergence == 0}), a
        returned number is not the same as a converged number.
  \item Ask for \texttt{hessian = TRUE}; you need it for standard errors.
        Remember the sign: if you minimized $-\loglik$, then the returned Hessian
        is $-\partial^2\loglik/\partial\theta\partial\theta'$, which is already
        the observed information.
  \item Try several starting values. If they land in different places, your
        likelihood is multimodal or your model is not identified.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
All of this is Thursday's lab.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification}
\begin{block}{Definition}
$\theta$ is \alert{identified} if distinct parameter values imply distinct
distributions of the data: $\theta^{*}\neq\theta \Rightarrow
f(\,\cdot \mid \theta^{*}) \neq f(\,\cdot\mid\theta)$.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
Non-identification means the likelihood is \emph{flat} along some direction: the
data cannot distinguish among a set of parameter values.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Classic example}: $Y_i \sim \mathcal N(\theta_1 - \theta_2,\, 1)$.
        Only the \emph{difference} is identified. Optimizers started at different
        points will return different $(\hat\theta_1,\hat\theta_2)$ lying along a
        ridge, all with the same log-likelihood.
  \item This is the likelihood analogue of \alert{perfect collinearity} in OLS, 
        and it has the same symptom: a singular Hessian, so no standard errors.
  \item Diagnosis: multiple start values, and inspecting the Hessian's rank.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

    \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,2.9) node[above]{\scriptsize $\loglik(\theta)$};
    \draw[dukeblue,very thick,smooth,samples=60,domain=1.05:3.55,variable=\x]
      plot ({\x},{2.4-1.5*(\x-2.3)^2});
    \draw[accent,dashed] (2.3,0) -- (2.3,2.4);
    \node at (2.3,-0.45) {\footnotesize peaked: much information};
  \end{scope}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
Both log-likelihoods peak at the same $\hat\theta$. But on the left, many nearby
values of $\theta$ fit almost as well; we are uncertain. On the right, moving
away from $\hat\theta$ costs a lot of fit; we are confident.
\vskip3pt
\alert{The second derivative measures exactly this.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fisher Information\core}
\begin{block}{Definition (one parameter)}
\begin{align*}
  \Info(\theta) \;\equiv\; -\E\!\left[\frac{\partial^2 \loglik(\theta)}{\partial\theta^2}\right]
  \;=\; \E\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial \loglik(\theta)}{\partial\theta}\right)^{\!2}\right]
  \;=\; \Var\big(S(\theta)\big) .
\end{align*}
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
The second equality is the \alert{information equality}, it holds when the
model is correctly specified, and it will matter a great deal in a moment.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The minus sign is there because the log-likelihood is concave at a
        maximum, so the second derivative is negative.
  \item Information is \emph{additive over independent observations}: with $n$
        i.i.d.\ observations, $\Info_n(\theta) = n\,\Info_1(\theta)$. This is the
        source of the familiar $1/n$ in variances.
  \item Two estimators are available: the \emph{expected} information
        $\Info(\hat\theta)$ and the \emph{observed} information
        $-\partial^2\loglik/\partial\theta^2$ evaluated at $\hat\theta$. They are
        asymptotically equivalent under correct specification, and software
        typically reports the observed version.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Variance of the MLE\core}
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;\hat{V}(\hat\theta) = \Info(\hat\theta)^{-1}
  \qquad\text{or}\qquad
  \left[-\frac{\partial^2\loglik}{\partial\theta^2}\bigg|_{\hat\theta}\right]^{-1}\;}
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
Read it as a sentence: \emph{the variance of the estimate is the reciprocal of
the curvature of the log-likelihood at its peak}.
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize These are two \emph{different} estimators, expected and observed
information, equal only asymptotically and under correct specification. Pick one
and say which.}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Sharper peak $\Rightarrow$ larger information $\Rightarrow$ smaller
        variance.
  \item With several parameters\adv, $\Info$ is a matrix and
        $\hat{V}(\hat{\bm\theta}) = \Info(\hat{\bm\theta})^{-1}$; the
        standard errors are the square roots of its diagonal.
  \item This is why \texttt{optim(..., hessian = TRUE)} is not optional.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked: Bernoulli Information}
\begin{align*}
  S(\theta) &= \frac{\sum_i y_i}{\theta} - \frac{n-\sum_i y_i}{1-\theta}, \\[3pt]
  \frac{\partial^2\loglik}{\partial\theta^2}
  &= -\frac{\sum_i y_i}{\theta^2} - \frac{n - \sum_i y_i}{(1-\theta)^2} .
\end{align*}
Taking expectations, using $\E[\sum_i y_i] = n\theta$:
\begin{align*}
  \Info(\theta)
  = \frac{n\theta}{\theta^2} + \frac{n(1-\theta)}{(1-\theta)^2}
  = \frac{n}{\theta} + \frac{n}{1-\theta}
  = \frac{n}{\theta(1-\theta)} .
\end{align*}
\pause
\begin{align*}
  \Var(\hat\theta) = \Info(\theta)^{-1} = \frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{n} . \quad\checkmark
\end{align*}
Exactly the variance of a sample proportion you already know, now \emph{derived}
from the shape of the likelihood.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Worked: Poisson Information}
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial^2\loglik}{\partial\theta^2} = -\frac{\sum_i y_i}{\theta^2}
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \Info(\theta) = \frac{\E[\sum_i y_i]}{\theta^2} = \frac{n\theta}{\theta^2}
  = \frac{n}{\theta} ,
\end{align*}
so
\begin{align*}
  \Var(\hat\theta) = \frac{\theta}{n},
  \qquad
  \hat{V}(\hat\theta) = \frac{\bar y}{n} .
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
Sanity check by direct calculation: $\hat\theta = \bar y$ and
$\Var(\bar Y) = \Var(Y)/n = \theta/n$ because the Poisson mean equals its
variance. \checkmark
\vskip4pt
Two routes, same answer. When you can compute the variance directly, do so; 
it is a free check on your information calculation.
\end{frame}

\section[Properties]{Properties of the MLE}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Four Properties\core}
Under regularity conditions, as $n\to\infty$:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{Consistency}: $\hat\theta \pto \theta_0$.
  \item \textbf{Asymptotic normality}:
        $\hat\theta \overset{a}{\sim} \mathcal N\big(\theta_0, \Info(\theta_0)^{-1}\big)$.
  \item \textbf{Asymptotic efficiency}: among \emph{regular} estimators, and when
        the model is correctly specified, none has a smaller asymptotic variance
, the MLE attains the \alert{Cramér--Rao lower bound}. This is much
        stronger than Gauss--Markov, which only ranked \emph{linear} unbiased
        estimators.
  \item \textbf{Invariance}: if $\hat\theta$ is the MLE of $\theta$, then
        $c(\hat\theta)$ is the MLE of $c(\theta)$ for any continuous $c(\cdot)$.
        Wonderfully convenient: the MLE of the odds is the odds of the MLE.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip6pt
\alert{The first three are asymptotic}, none of them says anything about
$n = 40$. Invariance, by contrast, holds exactly in any sample.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Consistency and Normality Hold\adv}
The argument is a first-order Taylor expansion of the score around $\theta_0$:
\begin{align*}
  0 = S(\hat\theta) \approx S(\theta_0)
  + \frac{\partial S(\theta_0)}{\partial\theta}(\hat\theta - \theta_0)
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  \sqrt n(\hat\theta-\theta_0)
  \approx \frac{\tfrac{1}{\sqrt n}S(\theta_0)}
               {-\tfrac1n \partial S(\theta_0)/\partial\theta} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The \textbf{denominator} is a sample average of second derivatives; by the
        LLN it converges to $\Info_1(\theta_0)$.
  \item The \textbf{numerator} is a normalized sum of the i.i.d.\ mean-zero
        scores; by the CLT it is asymptotically
        $\mathcal N\big(0,\Info_1(\theta_0)\big)$.
  \item Combining: $\sqrt n(\hat\theta-\theta_0)\dto
        \mathcal N\big(0,\Info_1(\theta_0)^{-1}\big)$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\alert{This is structurally identical to the OLS argument in Week 2}: a sample
average in the numerator, a sample average in the denominator, LLN below and CLT
above.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Regularity Conditions, and When They Fail}
The theory needs: a correctly specified and identified model; a parameter space
in which $\theta_0$ is an \emph{interior} point; a support for $y$ that does not
depend on $\theta$; and enough smoothness to differentiate twice under the
integral.
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{Failures you will actually meet}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Boundary}: testing whether a variance component is zero. The
        usual $\chi^2$ reference distribution is wrong.
  \item \textbf{Support depends on $\theta$}: $Y_i\sim U[0,\theta]$ gives
        $\hat\theta = \max_i Y_i$, which is \emph{always} below $\theta$, badly
        biased and not asymptotically normal.
  \item \textbf{Separation} in logit: the MLE runs off to infinity. Week 4.
  \item \textbf{Too many parameters relative to $n$}: incidental parameters.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Maximum Likelihood Is Not Unbiased\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item We already saw it: $\hat\sigma^2_{\text{MLE}} = \frac1n\sum_i(y_i-\bar y)^2$
        has $\E[\hat\sigma^2] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$.
  \item $Y_i \sim U[0,\theta]$: the MLE is $\max_i Y_i$, which is below $\theta$
        with probability one.
  \item And unbiasedness does not survive transformation: even if $\hat\theta$ is
        unbiased for $\theta$, $c(\hat\theta)$ is generally biased for
        $c(\theta)$. (Invariance holds for the \emph{MLE property}, not for
        unbiasedness.)
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{The trade we are making}
Give up exact unbiasedness in finite samples; get consistency, asymptotic
efficiency, and a completely general recipe that works for any probability model
you can write down.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When the Model Is Wrong: the Sandwich Returns\adv}
The information equality
$-\E[\partial^2\loglik/\partial\theta^2] = \E[S(\theta)^2]$ holds only if the
model is correctly specified. When it is not, the two sides differ and
\begin{align*}
  \Var(\hat\theta) \approx
  \underbrace{\big[-\E \partial^2\loglik\big]^{-1}}_{\text{bread}}
  \underbrace{\E\big[S(\theta)S(\theta)'\big]}_{\text{meat}}
  \underbrace{\big[-\E \partial^2\loglik\big]^{-1}}_{\text{bread}} .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Exactly the sandwich from Week 2, with the score in place of
        $X_i\epsilon_i$. It is called the \emph{quasi-} or \emph{pseudo-MLE}
        variance, or the Huber--White variance for likelihood models.
  \item In \texttt{R}: \texttt{sandwich::vcovHC()} works on \texttt{glm} objects
        too, and \texttt{vcovCL()} clusters them.
  \item The estimator $\hat\theta$ then converges to the parameter of the
        \emph{best-approximating} model in the Kullback--Leibler sense, the
        likelihood analogue of ``best linear approximation to the CEF.''
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Testing]{Likelihood-Based Tests}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Ways to Measure the Same Distance}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.05]
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (8.6,0) node[right]{\small $\theta$};
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,3.5) node[above]{\small $\loglik(\theta)$};
  \draw[dukeblue,very thick,smooth,samples=80,domain=0.5:8.1,variable=\x]
    plot ({\x},{3.0-0.14*(\x-5.2)^2});
  % MLE
  \draw[dashed,gray] (5.2,0) -- (5.2,3.0);
  \node at (5.2,-0.35) {\scriptsize $\hat\theta$};
  % null
  \draw[dashed,gray] (2.2,0) -- (2.2,{3.0-0.14*(2.2-5.2)^2});
  \node at (2.2,-0.35) {\scriptsize $\theta_0$};
  % LR: vertical distance
  \draw[accent,thick,<->] (2.2,{3.0-0.14*9}) -- (2.2,3.0);
  \node[accent,right] at (2.3,2.35) {\scriptsize LR: drop in $\loglik$};
  % Wald: horizontal distance
  \draw[accent,thick,<->] (2.2,0.28) -- (5.2,0.28);
  \node[accent] at (3.7,0.6) {\scriptsize Wald: distance in $\theta$};
  % score: slope at theta0
  \draw[accent,very thick] (1.5,{3.0-0.14*(1.5-5.2)^2-0.7*(1.5-2.2)})
                        -- (3.1,{3.0-0.14*(3.1-5.2)^2*0+1.08-0.7*(3.1-2.2)});
  \node[accent] at (1.5,1.75) {\scriptsize score: slope at $\theta_0$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-6pt
All three ask: \emph{is $\theta_0$ far from $\hat\theta$?} They are
asymptotically equivalent but can differ in finite samples.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Likelihood Ratio Test\core}
Compare the maximized log-likelihood with and without the restriction:
\begin{align*}
  LR = -2\big[\loglik(\hat\theta_{\text{restricted}})
            - \loglik(\hat\theta_{\text{unrestricted}})\big]
  \ \dto\ \chi^2_{q} ,
\end{align*}
where $q$ is the number of restrictions.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Logic: if the restriction is true, imposing it should not cost much fit.
  \item The restricted likelihood is always weakly \emph{lower}, so $LR \geq 0$.
  \item \textbf{Requires estimating both models.} Usually easy, and generally the
        best-behaved of the three in finite samples, prefer it when you can.
  \item \texttt{R}: \texttt{lmtest::lrtest(m0, m1)}, or \texttt{anova(m0, m1,
        test = "Chisq")} for \texttt{glm}.
  \item \alert{The models must be nested and fitted on the same observations.}
        Silently dropping different rows to missingness invalidates the test.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Wald Test\core}
Measure the horizontal distance, scaled by the estimated standard error:
\begin{align*}
  W = \frac{(\hat\theta - \theta_0)^2}{\hat{V}(\hat\theta)} \ \dto\ \chi^2_1 ,
  \qquad\text{or }\ z = \frac{\hat\theta-\theta_0}{\SE(\hat\theta)} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item This is the $t$-ratio you already read off every regression table.
  \item \textbf{Requires only the unrestricted model}, convenient when the
        restricted model is hard to fit.
  \item Multi-parameter version\adv:
        $W = (\bm R\hat{\bm\theta}-\bm q)'[\bm R\hat{V}(\hat{\bm\theta})\bm R']^{-1}
        (\bm R\hat{\bm\theta}-\bm q)$, identical in form to Week 2.
  \item \alert{Caution}: the Wald test is not invariant to how you parameterize
        the restriction. Testing $\theta = 1$ and $\log\theta = 0$ can give
        different answers. The LR test does not have this problem.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Score (Lagrange Multiplier) Test}
Measure the slope of the log-likelihood \emph{at the null}:
\begin{align*}
  LM = \frac{S(\theta_0)^2}{\Info(\theta_0)} \ \dto\ \chi^2_q .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Logic: if $\theta_0$ were the maximum, the slope there would be zero. A
        steep slope is evidence against it.
  \item \textbf{Requires only the restricted model}, useful when the
        unrestricted model is expensive to fit, e.g.\ testing for overdispersion
        without fitting the negative binomial.
  \item Many familiar specification tests are score tests in disguise, including
        Breusch--Pagan for heteroskedasticity.
  \item \textbf{Which to use?} Asymptotically the three are identical. In finite
        samples: LR if you can fit both models, Wald if only the unrestricted is
        available, score if only the restricted is.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Model Comparison Beyond Nested Tests\core}
LR, Wald and score all require \emph{nested} models. For non-nested comparison:
\begin{align*}
  AIC = -2\loglik(\hat\theta) + 2k ,
  \qquad
  BIC = -2\loglik(\hat\theta) + k\log n .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Both penalize fit by complexity; \alert{lower is better}.
  \item BIC penalizes more heavily once $n > 7$, and increasingly so with $n$.
        BIC targets the ``true'' model if it is in the candidate set; AIC targets
        predictive accuracy.
  \item Neither is a hypothesis test; there is no $p$-value and no threshold.
  \item Comparable only across models fitted on \emph{exactly the same} data with
        the same outcome scale.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
We return to model selection properly in Week 12, where cross-validation replaces
these analytic penalties.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where This Leaves Us}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item A likelihood is the data's probability read as a function of the
        parameter. The MLE maximizes it; the score is the derivative you set to
        zero.
  \item The \emph{curvature} of the log-likelihood is information, and its
        inverse is the variance. Everything about precision follows from the shape
        of the peak.
  \item The MLE is consistent, asymptotically normal, and efficient, but not
        unbiased, and all of the guarantees are asymptotic.
  \item LR, Wald, and score are three views of the same question.
\end{itemize}
