\section[Why ML]{Why Machine Learning?}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four Reasons to Use Machine Learning}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Prediction is the goal.} Where are the poor? Wealth in Rwanda predicted from
        mobile-phone records \srcp{Blumenstock, Cadamuro, and On 2015}.
  \item \textbf{Measurement.} Turn text and images into variables: how populist a speech
        is \srcp{Bonikowski, Luo, and Stuhler 2022}; how rich a neighbourhood is, read from
        its cars \srcp{Gebru et al.\ 2017}.
  \item \textbf{Ingredients of causal estimators.} Weighting needs each person's chance of
        treatment given $X$; regression adjustment, their expected outcome (Week 6). With
        hundreds of covariates, or text and images as $X$, ML predicts them, and double
        machine learning keeps their errors out of the causal estimate
        \srcp{Chernozhukov et al.\ 2018; Imai and Nakamura 2026}.
  \item \textbf{Who benefits?} A causal forest splits on $X$ to find where the treatment
        effect $\tau(x)$ is larger or smaller \srcp{Wager and Athey 2018}.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip2pt
{\small Reasons 3 and 4 are next week. \alert{Common thread}: every job predicts something
from $X$: the outcome $Y$, the treatment $D$, or the effect $\tau(x)$.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Kinds of Machine Learning\core}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.16\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.25\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.27\linewidth}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.22\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
kind & the data & what is learned & examples today \\
\midrule
\textbf{supervised} & inputs $X$ and a recorded outcome $Y$ & a rule that predicts $Y$ for new cases &
  log earnings: lasso, trees \\
\textbf{unsupervised} & inputs $X$ only, no outcome & a few dimensions that summarize $X$ &
  PCA; a status index \\
\textbf{self-supervised} & inputs $X$; hide a part and treat it as $Y$ & a code that predicts the hidden part &
  word2vec, BERT; the hidden word \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt\small
  \item \textbf{Supervised}: someone recorded the answer; judged by error on new cases.
  \item \textbf{Unsupervised}: no answer to check; judged by how much of $X$ it keeps.
  \item \textbf{Self-supervised}: labels come free from the data, so it trains on billions of words.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Linear Regression Is Already Machine Learning\core}
OLS learns a rule from data, $\hat Y = a + b\,x$, and applies it to anyone: a supervised
learner with a shape fixed in advance. Blue dots: average log earnings at each value (size =
number of people); red: the OLS rule \srcp{figs/w12\_intro.R, A}.
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.74\linewidth]{figs/cef_gss.pdf}
\end{center}
\vskip-5pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item Schooling: the line (slope 0.119) is close. Age: earnings rise, then flatten, and the
        line misses the young (arrow). A fixed shape cannot learn a pattern it does not allow.
  \item Next: rules whose shape is learned, and ways to stop them bending too much.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Goal From Here: Predict People We Have Not Seen\core}
\begin{block}{From now on}
Learn a rule from \textbf{training} data, then predict $Y$ (log earnings, or any outcome)
for \textbf{new} people. Every method is judged by its error on them, not by how well it
fits the data it learned from.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\textbf{Test $R^2$}: compare the rule with the simplest one, ``predict everyone with the
average'' $\bar Y$ of the training data:
\[
  R^2_{\text{test}} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{\text{new } i}\big(Y_i - \hat Y_i\big)^2}{\sum_{\text{new } i}\big(Y_i - \bar Y\big)^2}
\]
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item 1: perfect. 0: no better than the average. \alert{Negative: worse than the average.}
  \item Why it can be negative: in the training data, OLS with an intercept can always
        match the average (set every slope to 0), so its $R^2 \geq 0$. On new people nothing
        stops a rule from doing worse than the average. Test $R^2$ has no floor.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Making OLS Flexible Backfires\core}
The obvious fix: let OLS bend by adding squares and interactions. The 23 GSS columns

\end{center}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Training Error Flatters; New Cases Tell the Truth\core}
OLS on the first $p$ of the 243 GSS columns, 600 training cases, 1,053 new cases
\srcp{figs/w12\_gss.R}:
\vskip-2pt
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{figs/optimism.pdf}
\end{center}
\vskip-6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item Training error falls with every term added (0.98 with none, 0.40 with 200): a
        coefficient can always fit a little more noise.
  \item Error on new cases falls, then rises: 0.89 with none, \alert{0.68 with 12}, 1.16 with 200.
  \item So we cannot judge or tune a method on the data it was fitted to.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measuring Error Honestly: Hold Out a Test Set\core}
Training error is optimistic (previous slides). To measure error on \emph{new} cases,
keep some cases new: hide them from the fit.
\vskip6pt
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm]
  \fill[dukeblue!20] (0,0) rectangle (7,0.8);
  \fill[gray!35] (7,0) rectangle (10,0.8);

\vskip2pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item Split the sample \emph{at random}, before looking at anything.
  \item Fit only on the training set.
  \item Predict the test cases and compute the test MSE: an honest estimate of error on

\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt

1,053 test cases (the previous slide used 600 of the training cases).}

\section[Regularization]{Regularization}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Regularization Is Machine Learning\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{The problem.} With 243 terms and 300 cases, OLS uses every coefficient to chase
        the training cases, noise included, and predicts new people worse than the average.
  \item \textbf{The idea.} Keep the linear regression, but make every coefficient pay for being
        nonzero, and more for being large. A predictor gets a sizeable coefficient only if it improves the fit by enough
        to cover the charge; weak ones are pulled toward zero, or set to zero.
  \item \textbf{The trade.} The pulled-in coefficients are a little off on average, but they
        barely move from sample to sample, so predictions for new people improve.
  \item \textbf{What makes it machine learning.} How much to charge is not chosen by the
        researcher or by theory. It is a tuning parameter chosen by the data: we try many
        charges and keep the one that predicts held-out cases best.
  \item The lasso and ridge are two ways to set the charge (next slides).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Would $p$ Be Large?}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep8pt
  \item \textbf{Constructed features.} Flexible relationships need squares and interactions,
        and they multiply fast.
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item Civil war onset: the literature offers 88 country-level predictors
          \srcp{Hegre and Sambanis 2006}. All pairwise interactions add 3,828 terms; three-way
          interactions add 109,736, far more than the 7,140 country-years, with only 115 onsets
          to learn from \srcp{Muchlinski et al.\ 2016}.
    \item The GSS: 23 main-effect columns become 243 with pairs and squares.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Many available features.} Text, images, and administrative records give
        thousands of columns. Most are noise; some proxy traits we want to model.
  \item When terms outnumber cases, or come close, the data are \textbf{high-dimensional}: OLS
        cannot be fitted, or fits the noise.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Lasso}

\begin{frame}{Lasso\core}
\textbf{L}east \textbf{A}bsolute \textbf{S}hrinkage and \textbf{S}election
\textbf{O}perator: $\hat\beta$ solves a \textit{penalized} least squares problem
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta = \argmin_{b\in\R^{p}}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i - \textstyle\sum_{j} X_{ij} b_j\big)^2
  + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p}\big|b_j\big|
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Fit in the sample, plus $\lambda$ times the total absolute size of the coefficients.
  \item $\lambda = 0$ is OLS. As $\lambda$ grows, more coefficients are set
        \emph{exactly} to zero: lasso selects variables and estimates them at once.
  \item \textbf{Standardize} the regressors first (software does), so the penalty does
        not depend on whether income is in dollars or thousands.
\end{itemize}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{center}\small
GSS, 243 terms, 2,456 training cases \srcp{figs/w12\_690s.R, C}:\quad
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
$\lambda$ (glmnet scale) & 0.35 & 0.024 & 0.0014 \\
terms kept & 0 & 42 & 179 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Lasso: Fixing All But One Coefficient\core}
The lasso minimizes, over $b_1, \dots, b_p$,
\[
  L(b) = \sum_{i=1}^n \Big(Y_i - \sum_{k=1}^p X_{ik} b_k\Big)^2 + \lambda \sum_{k=1}^p |b_k| ,
\]
with regressors standardized: mean 0, variance 1, so $\sum_i X_{ij}^2 = n$.
\vskip4pt
\textbf{One coefficient at a time.} Fix every $b_k$ with $k \neq j$ at its current value (the
algorithm starts from all zeros). Let $R_i = Y_i - \sum_{k\neq j} X_{ik} b_k$, the part of $Y_i$
the other regressors leave unexplained. As a function of $b_j$ alone,
\[
  L(b_j) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big(R_i - X_{ij} b_j\big)^2 + \lambda\,|b_j| + \text{terms without } b_j .
\]
{\small Solve for $b_j$, update it, move to the next $j$ using the updated values, and cycle
until nothing changes (\textit{coordinate descent}, \texttt{glmnet}). The loss is convex, so the
starting values do not change the answer.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Lasso: the First-Order Condition\core}
For $b_j \neq 0$, $|b_j|$ has derivative $\text{sign}(b_j)$ ($+1$ or $-1$). Set $\partial L/\partial b_j = 0$:
\begin{align*}
  -2\sum_i X_{ij}\big(R_i - X_{ij} b_j\big) + \lambda\,\text{sign}(b_j) &= 0
   && \text{\footnotesize chain rule} \\
  -2\sum_i X_{ij} R_i + 2 b_j \sum_i X_{ij}^2 + \lambda\,\text{sign}(b_j) &= 0
   && \text{\footnotesize multiply out} \\
  -2n\,\hat b_j + 2n\, b_j + \lambda\,\text{sign}(b_j) &= 0
   && \text{\footnotesize $\textstyle\sum_i X_{ij}^2 = n$; $\hat b_j = \frac1n \sum_i X_{ij} R_i$} \\
  b_j &= \hat b_j - \frac{\lambda}{2n}\,\text{sign}(b_j)
   && \text{\footnotesize solve for $b_j$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $\hat b_j$ is the least-squares answer: set $\lambda = 0$ and the condition gives $b_j = \hat b_j$.
  \item The lasso moves it toward zero by $\lambda/(2n)$: down if $b_j > 0$, up if $b_j < 0$.
  \item The formula assumes $b_j \neq 0$. Next: when is $b_j = 0$?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Kink Decides: When Is $b_j$ Zero?\core}
At $b_j = 0$, $|b_j|$ has a kink: slope $-1$ to the left, $+1$ to the right, so any value in
$[-1, 1]$ is a valid slope there (the \textit{subgradient}). Collect the three cases:
\begin{align*}
  b_j > 0:&\quad b_j = \hat b_j - \tfrac{\lambda}{2n}, \text{ which is positive only if } \hat b_j > \tfrac{\lambda}{2n} \\
  b_j < 0:&\quad b_j = \hat b_j + \tfrac{\lambda}{2n}, \text{ which is negative only if } \hat b_j < -\tfrac{\lambda}{2n} \\
  b_j = 0:&\quad 0 \in -2n\hat b_j + \lambda\,[-1, 1] \;\Longleftrightarrow\; |\hat b_j| \leq \tfrac{\lambda}{2n}
\end{align*}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.58\linewidth}
\textbf{In one line} (\textit{soft thresholding}):
\[
  b_j = \text{sign}(\hat b_j)\,\Big(|\hat b_j| - \tfrac{\lambda}{2n}\Big)_+
\]
{\small Move the least-squares answer toward zero by $\lambda/(2n)$; if that would cross zero,
stop at zero. A weak regressor is dropped.}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.4\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.4]
  \draw[->] (-3.2,0) -- (3.4,0) node[right]{\scriptsize $\hat b_j$};
  \draw[->] (0,-3.0) -- (0,3.2) node[above]{\scriptsize lasso $b_j$};
  \draw[gray,dashed] (-3,-3) -- (3,3);
  \draw[accent,very thick] (-3,-2.2) -- (-0.8,0) -- (0.8,0) -- (3,2.2);
  \draw[gray] (0.8,0.1) -- (0.8,-0.1) node[below]{\tiny $\frac{\lambda}{2n}$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

(7 and 1.75); the other 398 are tiny \srcp{figs/w12\_sparsity.R}. Lasso with $\lambda = 0.4$:

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Approximate Sparsity\adv}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Lasso works best when the true coefficients are approximately sparse:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
    \item a small group of regressors with relatively large coefficients suffices
          to approximate the regression $\sum_j \beta_j X_{ij}$;
    \item the rest have small coefficients and add little.
  \end{itemize}
  \item A classic example: $\beta_j \propto 1/j^2$, $j = 1, \dots, p$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip-10pt
\begin{center}

\end{center}
\vskip-8pt
{\footnotesize $\beta_j = 7/j^2$: 7 at $j = 1$, 1.75 at 2, 0.28 at 5, 0.07 at 10; beyond that they barely matter.}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Ridge and Elastic Net}

\begin{frame}{Ridge Regression for Dense Coefficients\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Lasso does best when a few coefficients are large; ridge when many are small:
  \begin{align*}
    \hat\beta^{\text{ridge}} = \argmin_{b\in\R^{p}}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i - \textstyle\sum_{j} X_{ij} b_j\big)^2
    + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} b_j^2
  \end{align*}
  \item The penalty is the squared ($\ell_2$) size instead of the absolute ($\ell_1$) size.
  \item Ridge penalizes \emph{large} coefficients much more aggressively and
        \emph{small} ones much less. The marginal cost of moving $b_j$:
  \begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
  \toprule
  & $b_j = 0.1$ & $b_j = 1$ & $b_j = 2$ \\
  \midrule
  lasso, $\partial(\lambda|b_j|)/\partial b_j = \lambda$ & $\lambda$ & $\lambda$ & $\lambda$ \\
  ridge, $\partial(\lambda b_j^2)/\partial b_j = 2\lambda b_j$ & $0.2\lambda$ & $2\lambda$ & $4\lambda$ \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \item Near zero ridge's cost vanishes, so no coefficient is ever pushed exactly to
        0: ridge keeps every regressor, each a little, which suits a dense truth.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ridge, One Coefficient at a Time\core}
Same steps with $\lambda b_j^2$ in place of $\lambda |b_j|$. No kink, so one first-order condition:
\begin{align*}
  -2\sum_i X_{ij}\big(R_i - X_{ij} b_j\big) + 2\lambda b_j = -2n\hat b_j + 2n b_j + 2\lambda b_j = 0
  \;\Rightarrow\; b_j = \hat b_j\cdot\frac{n}{n + \lambda}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
{\small Example: $n = 100$, ridge $\lambda = 25$ (factor $100/125 = 0.8$), lasso $\lambda = 40$ (threshold 0.20)
\srcp{figs/w12\_690s.R, B}:}
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
& OLS & ridge & lasso \\
\midrule
strong regressor & 0.30 & $0.30\times 0.8 = 0.24$ & 0.10 \\
weak regressor & 0.15 & $0.15\times 0.8 = 0.12$ & 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item Ridge \emph{multiplies} every coefficient by $n/(n + \lambda)$; lasso \emph{subtracts}
        $\lambda/(2n)$ and stops at 0. Ridge never reaches zero, so it never selects.
  \item $\lambda = 0$ is OLS; $\lambda \to \infty$ sends every slope to 0.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where Ridge Wins: Many Small Effects\core}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.84\linewidth]{figs/dense_beta.pdf}
\end{center}
\vskip-6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item \textbf{Dense} (left): every coefficient small, none dominant. Ridge does best: it keeps
        every regressor and shrinks each a little, where the lasso would drop real small effects.
  \item Ridge does \emph{not} select: all $p$ regressors stay in, so it does not make the model smaller.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Elastic Net for Sparse \emph{or} Dense Coefficients\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Ridge and lasso can be combined to do well in either \textit{sparse} or
        \textit{dense} settings. The elastic net, with tuned penalty levels:
  \begin{align*}
    \hat\beta^{\text{EN}} = \argmin_{b\in\R^{p}}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i - \textstyle\sum_{j} X_{ij} b_j\big)^2
    + \lambda_1 \sum_{j=1}^{p} b_j^2 + \lambda_2 \sum_{j=1}^{p}|b_j|
  \end{align*}
  \item Choosing $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ gives more flexibility for a good
        prediction rule than ridge or lasso alone.
  \item It still selects variables unless we shut the lasso penalty off with
        $\lambda_2 = 0$.
  \item It handles groups of correlated predictors better than pure lasso: the ridge
        part spreads weight across the group instead of picking one.
  \item In \texttt{glmnet}: one $\lambda$ and a mixing weight $\alpha$,
        $\lambda\big(\alpha\sum|b_j| + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\sum b_j^2\big)$; $\alpha = 1$ is
        lasso, $\alpha = 0$ ridge.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Poverty From Phone Records}
\textbf{The question.} Blumenstock, Cadamuro, and On (2015): Rwanda has few reliable, recent measures of who is poor and where.
Can mobile phone records stand in for a household survey?
\vskip1pt
\textbf{The data.} Phone surveys of 856 randomly sampled subscribers (assets, housing)
give a wealth index; each respondent's call records give \emph{several thousand}
metrics: call volume, timing, contacts' network, mobility.
\vskip1pt
\textbf{The method.} $p \gg n$: thousands of candidate regressors for 856 people. Exactly
our setting. \alert{Elastic net} drops the irrelevant metrics; $\lambda$ chosen by
cross-validation.
\vskip1pt
\textbf{The results.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item Cross-validated correlation between predicted and surveyed wealth: $r = 0.68$.
  \item Averaged over Rwanda's 30 districts, the predictions match the government's
        in-person DHS survey of 12,792 households: $r = 0.92$.
  \item Tree ensembles gave similar results: the gain came from the regularized
        search over many features, not from one special method.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cross-Validation: Two Rules\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item $K = 5$ or $10$ is standard: leave-one-out ($K = n$) has low bias but high
        variance and is expensive.
  \item \alert{Everything data-dependent happens inside each round}, on the $K - 1$ training folds
        only, not once on the whole training set: variable selection, standardization,
        imputation. So the selected variables may differ slightly from fold to fold; that is
        correct. Selecting on all the data first is a serious leak (Lab 12 shows it).
  \item With dependence (panels, clusters, time) \alert{split on the independent
        unit}, not on rows. Same rule as the bootstrap in Week 2.
  \item If $\lambda$ is chosen by CV, the CV error at that $\lambda$ is optimistic:
        report error on a test set never used for tuning.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Why It Works: Bias and Variance}

\begin{frame}{Why the U-Shape? Bias and Variance\core}
The CV error fell, then rose, as $\lambda$ shrank. Why? Let $f(x)$ be the average of $Y$ among
everyone with $X = x$, the best possible prediction. Our $\hat f(x)$ changes from sample to sample:
\begin{align*}
  \text{Bias}\big(\hat f(x)\big) &= \E\big[\hat f(x)\big] - f(x)
   && \text{\footnotesize average prediction minus the truth} \\
  \Var\big(\hat f(x)\big) &= \E\Big[\big(\hat f(x) - \E[\hat f(x)]\big)^2\Big]
   && \text{\footnotesize scatter around that average} \\
  \text{MSE}(x) &= \E\Big[\big(\hat f(x) - f(x)\big)^2\Big]
   && \text{\footnotesize average squared distance from the truth}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Bias}: wrong on average. Rigid rules (large $\lambda$, few terms) have high bias.
  \item \textbf{Variance}: unstable. Flexible rules (small $\lambda$, many terms) have high variance.
  \item Expectations are over repeated \emph{samples}: they describe a method, not one fit.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Decomposing the MSE\core}
Same idea as the bandwidth in Week 9:
\begin{align*}
  \text{MSE}(x)
  &= \E\Big[\big((\hat f(x) - \E[\hat f(x)]) + (\E[\hat f(x)] - f(x))\big)^2\Big]
   && \text{\footnotesize add and subtract $\E[\hat f(x)]$} \\
  &= \E\Big[\big(\hat f(x) - \E[\hat f(x)]\big)^2\Big] + \big(\E[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\big)^2
   && \text{\footnotesize expand the square;} \\
  &\quad + 2\,\big(\E[\hat f(x)] - f(x)\big)\,\underbrace{\E\big[\hat f(x) - \E[\hat f(x)]\big]}_{=\,0}
   && \text{\footnotesize the bias is a constant} \\
  &= \Var\big(\hat f(x)\big) + \text{Bias}\big(\hat f(x)\big)^2
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Average squared error = spread around the average prediction + distance of
        that average from the truth.
  \item For any approximation fitted on a sample, there is a trade-off between the two.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Tradeoff in One Picture\core}
Lasso on simulated data with 400 regressors and 100 cases, many samples; error averaged over
new people \srcp{figs/w12\_lasso\_bv.R}:
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.43\linewidth]{figs/lasso_bv_curve.pdf}
\end{center}
\vskip-6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item Large $\lambda$ (left): few terms, predictions steady but wrong on average. Bias dominates.
  \item Small $\lambda$ (right): many terms, predictions jump from sample to sample. Variance dominates.
  \item Error on new people = noise + bias$^2$ + variance is lowest in between. Training error
        keeps falling, so it cannot find that point.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{So Accept a Little Bias\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item Weeks 1--2 prized unbiasedness: Gauss--Markov ranks estimators \emph{within} the
        unbiased class. For prediction the target is total error, bias$^2$ + variance.
  \item OLS with 243 terms and 300 cases keeps bias low and lets variance explode
        (test $R^2$ $-11.8$). Shrinking gives up a little bias for a large cut in variance.
  \item On the GSS, CV error 0.865 with $\lambda$ large (bias dominates), 0.699 with $\lambda$
        small (variance dominates), 0.652 in between.
  \item Every method after this has the same bargain and its own tuning parameter (depth for trees, size
        and training time for networks), chosen the same way: CV on the training set, the
        test set once, at the end.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\subsection{After Tuning}

\begin{frame}{Lasso Caveats\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The estimates shrink toward zero relative to the unpenalized regression:
        \textit{shrinkage bias} or \textit{regularization bias}. Lasso is biased
        by design.
  \item Lasso will not generally select the ``right'' set of variables.
  \item It tends to exclude variables with small but nonzero true
        coefficients (those with $|\hat b_j| \leq \lambda/(2n)$).
  \item It tends to fail to select the right variables when the $X_i$ are
        \textit{correlated} (next slide).
  \item \alert{So: do not interpret the selected set as ``the variables that
        matter'', and do not report $p$-values from a naive refit on the
        selected set.} The selection invalidates them. Valid inference after
        selection is Week 13's job.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Lasso Caveats: Correlated Regressors}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Suppose $X_1$ has coefficient $\beta_1 = 0$ but is highly correlated with
        $X_2, \dots, X_k$, whose coefficients are nonzero.
  \item The marginal predictive benefit of $X_1$ can be very high when
        $X_2, \dots, X_k$ are \emph{not} in the model, because $X_1$ stands in for
        all of them; the marginal benefit of any single one of $X_2, \dots, X_k$
        is then low.
  \item $X_1$ may enter with a nonzero coefficient while all of $X_2, \dots, X_k$
        are excluded.
  \item This inability to select \emph{exactly} the right regressors is not special
        to lasso; every variable-selection procedure shares it.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
{\small Next slide: what this means on the GSS.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Post-Lasso\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item One way to undo the \textit{shrinkage bias}: refit OLS using only the
        regressors whose lasso coefficients are nonzero.
  \item This is ``least squares post lasso'', or \textit{post-lasso}:
  \begin{align*}
    \hat\beta^{\text{post}} = \argmin_{b\in\R^p}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i - \textstyle\sum_{j} X_{ij} b_j\big)^2
    \quad\text{such that } b_j = 0 \text{ if } \hat\beta_j^{\text{lasso}} = 0
  \end{align*}
  \item Lasso chooses \emph{which} variables; OLS chooses \emph{how large}.
  \item Removing the shrinkage of the nonzero coefficients sometimes improves
        prediction, and sometimes does not.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From Linear to Nonlinear Models\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item We want to predict $Y_i$ from $p$ regressors $X_i$.
  \item So far the prediction rule was \textit{linear}: OLS, lasso, ridge. You choose
        the variables, and an interaction exists only if you type it.
  \item In reality the pattern is rarely linear; it has thresholds, curves, and
        interactions.
  \item Two classes of flexible methods find them on their own: \textbf{tree-based
        methods} and \textbf{neural networks}.
  \item Both are commonly used as the \textit{nuisance} learners inside double
        machine learning (Week 13).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Regression Trees\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Partition the regressor space (where $X_i$ takes values) into a set of
        \textit{rectangles}, and fit a simple model within each.
  \item The splitting rules can be drawn as a tree, hence \textit{decision trees}.
  \item The most common choice fits a \emph{constant} in each rectangle, so the
        unknown function is approximated by a \textit{step function}. With $J$
        regions $R_1, \dots, R_J$:
  \[
    f(X_i) = \sum_{j=1}^J \beta_j\,\mathds 1(X_i \in R_j),
  \]
        where $\beta_j$ is a constant for region $j$ and $\mathds 1(\cdot)$ the indicator.
  \item It is a regression on $J$ dummies, one per leaf, whose definition the data choose.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Each Leaf Predicts Its Average\core}
Given a partition, choose the constants to minimize the in-sample sum of squares:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta &= \argmin_{b_1,\dots,b_J}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\Big(Y_i - \sum_{j=1}^J b_j\,\mathds 1(X_i\in R_j)\Big)^2 \\
  &= \argmin_{b_1,\dots,b_J}\ \sum_{j=1}^J\ \sum_{i\in R_j}\big(Y_i - b_j\big)^2
   && \text{\footnotesize each $i$ is in exactly one region}
\end{align*}
The problem separates by region. For region $j$:
\begin{align*}
  -2\sum_{i\in R_j}(Y_i - b_j) = 0
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  \hat\beta_j = \frac{1}{n_j}\sum_{i\in R_j} Y_i = \bar Y_{R_j}
   && \text{\footnotesize $n_j$ cases in $R_j$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The prediction in a leaf is the average outcome of the training cases in it,
        exactly the dummy-regression result of Week 1.
  \item The hard part is choosing the rectangles.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Growing a Regression Tree\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The cut points are chosen from the data, not fixed in advance.
  \item To keep computation tractable we split recursively, two children at a time.
  \item At each node, consider every predictor $X_1, \dots, X_p$. For predictor $X_j$
        and threshold $s$, form the binary split
  \[
    R_L = \{i: X_{ij} \leq s\}, \qquad R_R = \{i: X_{ij} > s\}
  \]
  \item Compute the sum of squared errors after the split,
  \[
    \text{SSE}(j, s) = \sum_{i\in R_L}\big(Y_i - \bar Y_L\big)^2 + \sum_{i\in R_R}\big(Y_i - \bar Y_R\big)^2,
  \]
        with $\bar Y_L, \bar Y_R$ the mean outcomes in the two children.
  \item Choose the $(j, s)$ with the smallest $\text{SSE}(j, s)$: the largest
        reduction in MSE.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Growing a Regression Tree: Depth\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Repeat the search inside each child, recursively.
  \item Splits at level 2 or deeper may use different variables and cut points in
        different branches; that is how trees create interactions and
        nonlinearities.
  \item Stop when a prespecified depth is reached, or (more commonly) when a node
        would have fewer than a minimum number of observations.
  \item \textbf{Why depth matters}: the deeper the tree, the smaller the bias, but
        the noisier the prediction, since fewer observations sit in each leaf to
        estimate its mean. The bias--variance tradeoff again.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Pruning a Tree\adv}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Trying every possible tree structure and cross-validating each is not
        feasible.
  \item Better: grow a large tree $T_0$ (stop only when leaves are small), then
        \textit{prune} it back to a \textit{subtree} $T \subset T_0$.
  \item \textit{Cost-complexity pruning}: for a penalty $\alpha$, choose the subtree
        that minimizes
  \begin{align*}
    C_\alpha(T) = \sum_{m=1}^{|T|}\ \sum_{i\in R_m}\big(Y_i - \bar Y_{R_m}\big)^2 + \alpha\,|T|,
  \end{align*}
        where $|T|$ is the number of leaves.
  \item As in lasso, the penalty trades complexity against in-sample fit: each extra
        leaf must lower the SSE by more than $\alpha$.
  \item $\alpha$, and with it the tree, is chosen by a validation set or CV.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize GSS: the CV-pruned tree reaches test $R^2$ 0.215, below OLS (0.251). One tree is
too rough a step function for a mostly smooth relationship.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bagging\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Even tuned, one tree has \textit{high variance}, especially in small samples.
  \item \textit{Bootstrap aggregation}, or \textit{bagging}, is a general way to
        reduce the variance of a learning method.
  \item Recall: for independent $Z_1, \dots, Z_B$, each with variance $\sigma^2$,
        $\Var(\bar Z) = \sigma^2/B$. Averaging cuts variance.
  \item Draw $B$ bootstrap samples; grow a tree on each, \emph{deep} and
        \emph{unpruned}: low bias, high variance. Average:
  \[
    \hat f_{\text{bag}}(x) = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B \hat f^{\,b}(x)
  \]
  \item The average keeps the low bias and sheds much of the variance. Hundreds or
        thousands of trees are typical.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
{\footnotesize GSS: one deep tree, test $R^2$ $-0.264$; 500 bagged deep trees, $0.268$
\srcp{figs/w12\_690s.R, G}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Random Forests\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Random forests improve on bagging by \textit{decorrelating} the trees.
  \item As in bagging, grow trees on bootstrap samples. But at each split, consider
        only \textit{a random sample of $m$ predictors} out of the $p$, a fresh
        sample at every split; typically $m \approx \sqrt p$ (GSS: $p = 23$, $m = 5$).
  \item \textbf{Why}: if one strong predictor dominates the first split, every
        bagged tree splits on it first, and the trees look alike. Averaging
        near-copies removes little variance, and the other predictors' information
        goes unused.
  \item On the GSS, education is that predictor: it is the root split
        (slide on the first GSS split).
  \item Forcing splits to consider different subsets gives different trees, and their
        average varies less.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Decorrelating Trees Works\adv}
$B$ trees, each with variance $\sigma^2$ at a point, pairwise correlation $\rho$:
\begin{align*}
  \Var\Big(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B T_b\Big)
  &= \frac{1}{B^2}\Big(\sum_b \Var(T_b) + \sum_{b\neq b'}\Cov(T_b, T_{b'})\Big)
   && \text{\footnotesize variance of a sum} \\
  &= \frac{1}{B^2}\big(B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2\big)
   && \text{\footnotesize $B(B-1)$ ordered pairs} \\
  &= \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2
   && \text{\footnotesize rearrange}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt\small
  \item More trees kill only the second term. The first, $\rho\sigma^2$, is a floor.
        With $\sigma^2 = 1$, $B = 500$: $\rho = 0.6$ gives 0.601; $\rho = 0.3$ gives 0.301.
  \item GSS: two trees' test predictions correlate 0.34 when bagging, 0.29 in the
        forest; test $R^2$ 0.268 vs.\ 0.274: little gain here \srcp{figs/w12\_690s.R, G}.
  \item Out-of-bag error: each tree skips about a third of the data
        ($(1-1/n)^n \approx e^{-1}$). GSS: OOB MSE 0.626, test MSE 0.645.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which Variables Does the Forest Use? Two Measures\core}
A forest averages 1,000 trees: no single tree to read, no coefficient. Instead ask
\emph{how much the predictions depend on each variable}.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{1. Permutation importance.} Shuffle one column, which breaks its link with $Y$ and
leaves its distribution unchanged. Predict again and record how much the error rises.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt\small
  \item GSS forest, 1,053 test cases: test MSE 0.645. Shuffle education: 0.809. Its importance $= 0.164$.
  \item \texttt{ranger} does this tree by tree, on each tree's out-of-bag cases
        (\texttt{importance = "permutation"}).
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{2. Impurity importance.} Every split cuts the SSE, as in the earlier split by hand
(the root split on schooling cut it by 259.0). Credit each cut to the variable that was split on,
then add the cuts up within a tree and average over the trees.
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize Impurity: \texttt{importance = "impurity"}, free while growing the trees. Same forest as the benchmark table: 2,456 training cases, 23 columns
\srcp{figs/w12\_importance.R}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boosted Trees\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Bagging and random forests reduce \emph{variance} from low-bias estimates
        (deep trees) by repeated sampling. \textit{Boosting} reduces \emph{bias}
        from low-variance estimates (shallow trees) by repeatedly fitting
        residuals.
  \item Fit a simple rule (a shallow tree), take the \textit{residuals}, fit
        another shallow tree to those residuals, and so on.
  \item Each step fits what the previous steps missed, so each step reduces the
        remaining approximation error.
  \item Stop at a stopping criterion. The overall prediction is the sum of the
        rules from all steps.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boosted Trees: the Algorithm\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item Initialize the residuals: $R_i := Y_i$, $i = 1, \dots, n$.
  \item For $j = 1, \dots, J$:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item fit a tree-based rule $\hat f_j(X_i)$ to the data $(X_i, R_i)_{i=1}^n$;
    \item update the residuals, $R_i := R_i - \lambda\,\hat f_j(X_i)$, where
          $\lambda$ is the \textit{learning rate}.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Output the boosted rule
  \[
    \hat f(X_i) := \sum_{j=1}^J \lambda\,\hat f_j(X_i).
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
{\small Choices: the tree depth, the number of steps $J$, and the learning rate
(a common default is $\lambda = 0.1$), usually by CV. In practice one starts from the
mean rather than from 0.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boosted Trees: How Many Steps}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The number of steps $J$ matters.
  \item Each step fits the part of the outcome the previous steps left
        unexplained, so the in-sample error can only fall (weakly) with each step:
        1.573, 0.593, 0.233, 0.133, 0.069 by hand.
  \item Too many steps and the model overfits: eventually it fits the noise.
  \item Too few steps leave substantial bias.
  \item In practice, stop once CV-MSE no longer improves.
  \item A small learning rate with many steps usually beats a large rate with few:
        many cautious steps rather than a few bold ones.
  \item A popular implementation is \texttt{xgboost} \srcp{Chen and Guestrin 2016}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bagging, Random Forests, Boosting\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Bagging}: fit trees on bootstrap resamples and average. Averaging
        $B$ nearly independent estimates cuts variance by roughly $1/B$ without
        adding bias. But bootstrap trees are \emph{correlated}, they all split
        on the same dominant predictor first, which caps the gain.
  \item \textbf{Random forests}: bag, but at each split consider only a random
        subset of $m$ predictors. Decorrelating the trees is what makes the
        averaging work. $m$ is the main tuning parameter, alongside node size.
        Out-of-bag error is a free cross-validation estimate.
  \item \textbf{Boosting}: fit trees \emph{sequentially}, each on the residuals of
        the current ensemble, adding a shrunken version:
        $\hat f \leftarrow \hat f + \lambda\,\hat t$. Attacks \emph{bias} rather than
        variance, and with a small learning rate $\lambda$ and many trees is usually the strongest
        tabular predictor available.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\alert{Bagging and random forests reduce variance; boosting reduces bias.} That
is the cleanest way to keep them straight.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Combining Learners: Stacking\adv}
No method wins everywhere: lasso won the small-sample GSS race among linear
models, the forest won overall. \textbf{Stacking} lets the data choose a mix
\srcp{van der Laan, Polley, and Hubbard 2007}:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Fit each candidate $\hat f_1,\dots,\hat f_M$ with $K$-fold
        cross-validation; keep the out-of-fold predictions $\hat f_m^{(-k)}(X_i)$.
  \item Regress $Y_i$ on the out-of-fold predictions with non-negative weights
        summing to one: $\min_{a}\sum_i\big(Y_i - \sum_m a_m\hat f_m^{(-k)}(X_i)\big)^2$.
  \item Predict with $\sum_m \hat a_m\hat f_m$, each refit on all data.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Out-of-fold predictions keep a learner that overfits from getting weight
        it did not earn.
  \item Asymptotically at least as good as the best single candidate
        (the ``super learner'' oracle result). Next week it supplies the nuisance
        functions inside DML.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Single Layer Neural Network\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item A neural network takes the $p$ inputs $X_i = (X_{1i}, \dots, X_{pi})$ and
        builds a nonlinear function $f(X_i)$ to predict $Y_i$.
  \item The $p$ features make up the units of the \textit{input layer}.
  \item Every input feeds into each of $K$ hidden units, the \textit{hidden layer}.
  \item A single-layer network has the form
  \begin{align*}
    f(X_i) &= \beta_0 + \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_k A_k = \beta_0 + \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_k\,h_k(X_i) \\
           &= \beta_0 + \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_k\,\sigma\Big(w_{k0} + \sum_{j=1}^p w_{kj}X_{ji}\Big)
  \end{align*}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Steps: Hidden Units, Then Output\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Each of the $K$ \textbf{hidden units} (\textit{neurons}) takes a weighted sum of the inputs
        and bends it. The number it outputs is its \textit{activation} $A_k$:
  \[
    A_k = h_k(X_i) = \sigma\Big(w_{k0} + \sum_{j=1}^p w_{kj}X_{ji}\Big),
  \]
        where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the \textit{activation function}, a fixed bend chosen in advance.
        Neuron = the node; activation = its output. (Software sometimes lists $\sigma$ as its own
        ``layer''; it is part of each neuron.)
  \item The $K$ activations feed into the output layer, a linear regression on the
        learned features:
  \[
    f(X_i) = \beta_0 + \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_k A_k
  \]
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
{\small $\beta_k$ and $w_{kj}$ are called \textit{weights}; $\beta_0$ and $w_{k0}$
are called \textit{biases} (intercepts, not the bias of the bias--variance tradeoff).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Activation Functions\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The \textit{sigmoid} function
  \[
    \sigma(z) = \frac{e^z}{1+e^z} = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},
  \]
        the same function the logit uses to turn a linear index into a probability
        (Week 4).
  \item The modern default is the \textit{ReLU} (\textit{rectified linear unit}):
        $\sigma(z) = (z)_+ = \max(0, z)$.
  \begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
  \toprule
  $z$ & $-1$ & $0.5$ & $2$ \\
  \midrule
  sigmoid & 0.27 & 0.62 & 0.88 \\
  ReLU & 0 & 0.5 & 2 \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \item ReLU is cheap to compute and store, and its derivative is simple (0 or 1).
        It thresholds at 0, but the intercept $w_{k0}$ moves the threshold, like a
        neuron that fires only above a threshold.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the Activation Must Be Nonlinear\core}
Suppose $\sigma(z) = z$ instead:
\begin{align*}
  f(X) &= \beta_0 + \textstyle\sum_k \beta_k\big(w_{k0} + \sum_j w_{kj}X_j\big)
   && \text{\footnotesize substitute the identity} \\
  &= \underbrace{\big(\beta_0 + \textstyle\sum_k\beta_k w_{k0}\big)}_{\text{intercept}}
   + \textstyle\sum_j \underbrace{\big(\textstyle\sum_k \beta_k w_{kj}\big)}_{\text{slope on } X_j} X_j
   && \text{\footnotesize regroup}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \alert{A linear network of any width or depth is one linear regression.}
        All the flexibility comes from $\sigma$.
  \item With ReLU and the intercept $w_{k0}$, each unit is a hinge at its own
        location: ReLU networks are piecewise-linear functions.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the Bend Makes a Network Flexible\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item Each hidden unit takes a weighted sum of the inputs and \textbf{bends} it (S-curve, ReLU).
        The output adds the bent pieces with weights.
  \item \textbf{Without the bend}, a weighted sum of weighted sums is still a weighted sum: the
        network is just a linear regression, however many units it has.
  \item \textbf{With the bend}, adding pieces that switch on at different places draws curves,
        thresholds, and interactions nobody typed in. Example with $\sigma(z) = z^2$ and two units:
        $\tfrac14(X_1 + X_2)^2 - \tfrac14(X_1 - X_2)^2 = X_1X_2$, an interaction.
  \item \textbf{More units, finer shapes}: with enough hidden units a network can approximate any
        smooth function \srcp{Hornik, Stinchcombe, and White 1989}. That says it is possible, not
        how many units or how to find the weights (training, next).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Number of Parameters in a Single Layer Network}
With $p$ inputs and $K$ neurons:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item input to hidden layer: $(p+1)\times K$, that is $p\times K$ weights and $K$
        biases;
  \item hidden to output layer: $K + 1$, that is $K$ weights and 1 bias.
\end{itemize}
To fit, minimize the loss (continuous $Y_i$) over all of them:
\[
  \argmin_{W\in\R^{(p+1)\times K};\ \beta\in\R^{K+1}}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i - f(X_i)\big)^2
\]
\vskip3pt
{\small GSS: 23 inputs, 2 hidden units: $(23+1)\times 2 + (2+1) = 48 + 3 = 51$ parameters.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two-Layer Neural Network}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item MNIST: the input layer has $p = 784$ units; hidden layer $L_1$ has $K_1 = 256$
        units, $L_2$ has $K_2 = 128$; the output layer has 10 units, the
        probabilities of the digits 0--9 from a \textit{softmax} (the multinomial
        logit of Week 4).
  \item Each neuron $k = 1, \dots, K_1$ in the first hidden layer:
  \begin{align*}
    A_k^{(1)} = h_k^{(1)}(X_i) = \sigma\Big(w_{k0}^{(1)} + \sum_{j=1}^{p} w_{kj}^{(1)} X_{ji}\Big)
  \end{align*}
  \item The second hidden layer treats the neurons of $L_1$ as its inputs:
  \begin{align*}
    A_\ell^{(2)} = h_\ell^{(2)}(X_i) = \sigma\Big(w_{\ell 0}^{(2)} + \sum_{k=1}^{K_1} w_{\ell k}^{(2)} A_k^{(1)}\Big),
    \qquad \ell = 1, \dots, K_2
  \end{align*}
  \item Depth builds features of features (edges, then shapes, then digits).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fitting a Neural Network\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Take the single-layer network; the multilayer case works the same way.
  \item We minimize the squared loss over $(p+1)K$ input weights and biases and $K+1$
        output ones.
  \item That is hard, for two reasons:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item With a nonlinear activation, the loss is \textit{non-convex}. Gradient
          $= 0$ is necessary but no longer sufficient for the global minimum; we can
          land in a local minimum or at a saddle point.
    \item The sheer number of parameters: no closed form, no single calculation
          solves the gradient equations.
  \end{itemize}
  \item Collect all parameters in one long vector $\theta$, of length $(p+1)K + (K+1)$,
        and minimize
  \[
    R(\theta) = \frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i - f_\theta(X_i)\big)^2
  \]
        by \textit{gradient descent}, with \textit{regularization} against overfitting.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Gradient Descent: Picture\core}
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.66\linewidth]{figs/gradient_descent.pdf}
\end{center}
\vskip-6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item Two minima, one local, one global: where you start decides where you end.
  \item Is a local minimum good enough? For prediction, usually yes: the global minimum of
        training error would fit the noise (early stopping avoids it on purpose), and in large
        networks different local minima give similar error.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Gradient Descent\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item Start with an initial guess $\theta^0$ for all parameters (small random
        numbers); set $t = 0$.
  \item Find a small change $\delta$ such that $\theta^{t+1} = \theta^t + \delta$
        \textit{reduces} the loss: $R(\theta^{t+1}) < R(\theta^t)$.
  \item Stop when the improvement is negligible, e.g.\
        $|R(\theta^{t+1}) - R(\theta^t)| < \varepsilon$.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
The step comes from the \textit{gradient} of $R$ at $\theta^t$, with a small
positive \textit{learning rate} $\rho$:
\begin{align*}
  \delta = -\rho\,\nabla R(\theta^t) = -\rho\,\frac{\partial R(\theta)}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_{\theta = \theta^t}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The gradient points uphill; we step the other way.
  \item Too large a $\rho$ overshoots; too small crawls.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Gradient Descent by Hand}
One parameter, three outcomes $Y = (1, 2, 6)$, the model predicts $\theta$ for everyone:
\begin{align*}
  R(\theta) = \tfrac12\sum_i (Y_i - \theta)^2, \qquad
  \frac{dR}{d\theta} = -\sum_i (Y_i - \theta), \qquad
  \theta^{t+1} = \theta^t + 0.1\sum_i (Y_i - \theta^t)
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Gradient for One Observation\adv}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Computing the gradient is feasible. Take the loss for one observation $i$;
        the total loss is the sum over the $n$ observations:
  \begin{align*}
    R_i(\theta) &= \tfrac12\big(Y_i - f_\theta(X_i)\big)^2 \\
    &= \tfrac12\Big(Y_i - \beta_0 - \sum_{k=1}^K \beta_k\,\sigma\Big(w_{k0} + \sum_{j=1}^p w_{kj}X_{ji}\Big)\Big)^2
  \end{align*}
  \item For short, write the linear index of hidden unit $k$ as
  \[
    z_{ik} = w_{k0} + \sum_{j=1}^p w_{kj}X_{ji}, \qquad A_{ik} = \sigma(z_{ik}).
  \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backpropagation: the Chain Rule\adv}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item For an output weight $\beta_k$:
  \begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial R_i}{\partial\beta_k}
    = \frac{\partial R_i}{\partial f_\theta(X_i)}\cdot\frac{\partial f_\theta(X_i)}{\partial\beta_k}
    = -\big(Y_i - f_\theta(X_i)\big)\cdot\sigma(z_{ik})
  \end{align*}
  \item For an input weight $w_{kj}$:
  \begin{align*}
    \frac{\partial R_i}{\partial w_{kj}}
    &= \frac{\partial R_i}{\partial f_\theta(X_i)}\cdot\frac{\partial f_\theta(X_i)}{\partial\sigma(z_{ik})}\cdot
       \frac{\partial\sigma(z_{ik})}{\partial z_{ik}}\cdot\frac{\partial z_{ik}}{\partial w_{kj}} \\
    &= -\big(Y_i - f_\theta(X_i)\big)\cdot\beta_k\cdot\sigma'(z_{ik})\cdot X_{ji}
  \end{align*}
  \item Both contain the residual $Y_i - f_\theta(X_i)$: differentiation hands a share of
        the residual to each parameter through the chain rule. Compute $f$ forward,
        pass the residual backward: \textit{backpropagation}.
  \item Deep networks repeat the second line once per layer. If $\sigma'$ is small
        (sigmoid in its flat tails), products of many small numbers vanish: one reason
        ReLU replaced sigmoid.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stochastic Gradient Descent}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item The gradient of the loss $R(\theta) = \sum_i R_i(\theta)$ is the sum of the $n$ gradients
        $\nabla R_i$. Dividing by $n$ (the \emph{average} loss) only rescales the step $\rho$, but keeps
        the step from growing with $n$; software uses the average:
  \[
    \theta^{t+1} = \theta^t - \rho\,\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \nabla R_i(\theta^t)
  \]
        Every update uses all $n$ observations: expensive.
  \item \textit{Stochastic gradient descent} (SGD) uses only a random subsample
        $B_t$, a \textit{mini-batch}, at each update:
  \[
    \theta^{t+1} = \theta^t - \rho\,\frac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{i\in B_t} \nabla R_i(\theta^t)
  \]
  \item One full pass through all observations is an \textit{epoch}. At the start of
        each epoch the data are shuffled and cut into non-overlapping mini-batches.
  \item With $n = 1{,}000$ and batches of 10: 100 updates per epoch instead of one.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dealing With Overfitting: Weight Decay\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item As in high-dimensional regression, a massive number of parameters risks
        overfitting.
  \item A common fix is the ridge ($\ell_2$) penalty on all weights and
        biases:\footnote{$\sum_j\theta_j^2$ sums the squares of every entry of $\theta$.}
  \begin{align*}
    R_i^{\text{ridge}}(\theta) = R_i(\theta) + \lambda\sum_j\theta_j^2
  \end{align*}
  \item SGD with a one-observation mini-batch then updates
  \begin{align*}
    \theta^{t+1} = \theta^t - \rho\big(\nabla R_i(\theta^t) + 2\lambda\,\theta^t\big)
    = (1 - 2\rho\lambda)\,\theta^t - \rho\,\nabla R_i(\theta^t)
  \end{align*}
  \item Each step first shrinks every weight by the factor $1 - 2\rho\lambda$ (it
        ``decays''), then steps downhill.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dealing With Overfitting: Dropout\adv}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Lasso-like penalties that zero out weights are less common in networks.
        Instead, \textit{dropout learning} keeps units from becoming over-specialized.
\end{itemize}

\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Tuning a Neural Network}
Three classes of hyperparameters must be tuned for a network to perform well:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textit{Architecture}: number of hidden layers and units per layer.
  \item \textit{Regularization}: the weight penalty $\lambda$ and the dropout rate
        $\phi$, possibly separately for each layer.
  \item \textit{Optimization}: the SGD details, learning rate $\rho$, batch size,
        and number of epochs.
\end{itemize}
\vskip5pt
For large networks CV is often too expensive. Instead, hold out one validation set and
monitor performance on it while tuning.
\end{frame}

One hidden layer (\texttt{nnet}), inputs standardized; hidden units
$\{2, 5, 10, 20\}$ and weight decay $\{0.01, 0.1, 1\}$ by 5-fold CV; average of

  \item Test $R^2$ 0.259: about lasso's (0.261), below random forests (0.272)

\end{itemize}

This matches the benchmark literature: on tables of data, tree ensembles still
beat deep networks \srcp{Grinsztajn, Oyallon, and Varoquaux 2022}. Networks win
when the input is unstructured: text, images, audio.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Turn Text and Images Into Numbers?}
Sociologists increasingly study behaviour through text, images, and audio: speeches,
posts, court records, street photographs \srcp{Lazer et al.\ 2009}. A regression needs
numbers. Three uses:
\vskip3pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{As the object of study}: the numbers for words carry cultural meaning.
        How did the meaning of ``class'' change over a century of books?
        \srcp{Kozlowski, Taddy, and Evans 2019}
  \item \textbf{As predictors or measurements}: score each presidential speech for
        populism; read a neighbourhood's income from its cars
        \srcp{Bonikowski, Luo, and Stuhler 2022; Gebru et al.\ 2017}.
  \item \textbf{As control variables}: the effect of nearby gunshots on children's test
        scores must hold neighbourhood ``quality'' fixed; surveys miss it, street images
        may record it. The effect of price on demand must hold product quality fixed; it
        is written in the product description \srcp{Bajari et al.\ 2025}.
\end{enumerate}
{\small Use 3 is next week. Today: how the numbers are made.}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Principal Components}

\begin{frame}{Feature Extraction: Principal Components\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item We want to turn complex objects, such as text or images, into a relatively small set of
        numerical features.
  \item Principal components are an early, classic example of such features (\emph{embeddings}).
  \item Principal components are weighted sums of the original variables, chosen to be the
        ``best'' at reproducing the data: each new direction is orthogonal to the previous ones.
  \item A small number of principal components often captures most of the variation in the
        original variables, and so gives a useful low-dimensional summary.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Principal Components: the Setup\core}
A sample of $n$ observations $W_1, \dots, W_n$ of a centred vector with $d$ entries,
$W_i = (W_{i1}, \dots, W_{id})'$, each entry with mean 0. Their covariance matrix:
\[
  \Sigma_n = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n W_i W_i' \qquad (d \times d).
\]
\textbf{With $d = 2$, written out:}
\begin{align*}
  \Sigma_n &= \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
  \begin{pmatrix} W_{i1} \\ W_{i2} \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix} W_{i1} & W_{i2} \end{pmatrix}
  = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \begin{pmatrix} W_{i1}^2 & W_{i1}W_{i2} \\ W_{i2}W_{i1} & W_{i2}^2 \end{pmatrix}
   && \text{\footnotesize multiply} \\
  &= \begin{pmatrix} \Var(W_1) & \Cov(W_1, W_2) \\ \Cov(W_1, W_2) & \Var(W_2) \end{pmatrix}
   && \text{\footnotesize means are 0}
\end{align*}
{\small Diagonal: each variable's variance. Off the diagonal: how the variables move together.
For standardized SEI and prestige: 1, 1, and their correlation 0.82.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Principal Directions and Components\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The \textbf{eigenvectors} $c_k$ (each of length 1) and \textbf{eigenvalues} $\lambda_k$ of
        $\Sigma_n$ solve
  \[
    \Sigma_n c_k = \lambda_k c_k, \qquad k = 1, \dots, d .
  \]
        Software (\texttt{prcomp}) finds them. $c_1$ is the direction in which the data vary
        most; $\lambda_k$ is the variance along $c_k$; the $\lambda_k$ add up to the total variance.
  \item Keep the $K$ largest ($K \ll d$): $C_K = [c_1, \dots, c_K]$, a $d \times K$ matrix.
  \item \textbf{Project} $W_i$ on each principal direction. With $d = 2$, written out:
  \[
    X_{ki} = c_k' W_i = c_{k1} W_{i1} + c_{k2} W_{i2} :
  \]
        a weighted sum of the variables, the $k$-th \textbf{principal component}.
  \item Person $i$'s compressed features: $X_i^K = (X_{1i}, \dots, X_{Ki})' = C_K' W_i$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reconstruction\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item $X_i^K$ is the compressed feature vector of the original $W_i$.
  \item Approximate $W_i$ by projecting $X_i^K$ back to the $d$-dimensional space:
  \[
    \hat W_i = C_K X_i^K .
  \]
  \item With $d = 2$ and $K = 1$, written out: $\hat W_i = c_1 X_{1i}$, i.e.\
        $\hat W_{i1} = c_{11} X_{1i}$ and $\hat W_{i2} = c_{12} X_{1i}$.
        Green person: $\hat W_i = (0.707 \times 1.24,\ 0.707 \times 1.24)' = (0.87, 0.87)'$.
  \item The choice of $K$ sets how much of the original information (variance) is kept:
        the share $\sum_{k \leq K} \lambda_k \big/ \sum_k \lambda_k$.
  \item A common use: compressing images (next).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\[
  \underset{d\times 1}{W_i} \;\mapsto\; C_K'W_i := \underset{K\times 1}{E_i}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Step 1 \textbf{encodes} the input into $K$ numbers; step 2 \textbf{decodes} them
        back to the original space: an encoder--decoder network, an \textbf{autoencoder}.
  \item For principal components, $A = C_K'$, so $\hat W_i = C_K E_i = C_K X_i^K$.
  \item The weights into each hidden unit are a principal direction $c_k$; the hidden units'
        outputs are the principal components; the decoder rebuilds the input.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Change What the Network Predicts\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item With encoder $e(\cdot)$ and decoder $d(\cdot)$, an autoencoder minimizes the
        rebuilding loss $\E\big[\text{loss}\big(W,\ d(e(W))\big)\big]$. It must rebuild
        \emph{everything}: font, word order, typos, lighting.
  \item Keep the encoder, but predict another target $A$:
        $\E\big[\text{loss}\big(A,\ f(e(W))\big)\big]$. The code keeps what predicts $A$:
  \begin{center}\small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.0}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  model & trained to predict & the code ends up holding \\
  \midrule
  autoencoder & the input itself & whatever varies most \\
  word2vec & the words near each word & what a word's company says \\
  BERT & words hidden from a sentence & meaning in context \\
  Gebru et al.\ CNN & a car's make and model & visual traits of cars \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \item The task is a choice: for price effects, a description's code should hold product
        type and quality, not its wording.
  \item All but the last are \textbf{self-supervised} (slide on the three kinds): the
        target comes free from the data, so they train on billions of words.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{You Know a Word by Its Neighbours\core}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.52\linewidth}
What is \textcolor{dukeblue}{tesg\"uino}?
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item A bottle of \textcolor{dukeblue}{tesg\"uino} is on the table.
  \item Everyone likes \textcolor{dukeblue}{tesg\"uino}.
  \item \textcolor{dukeblue}{Tesg\"uino} makes you drunk.
  \item We make \textcolor{dukeblue}{tesg\"uino} out of corn.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize\color{gray}(Jurafsky and Martin, Ch.\ 6)}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.45\linewidth}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item You never saw the word, yet you know: an alcoholic drink, made from corn.
  \item \textbf{Distributional hypothesis}: words used in similar contexts have similar
        meanings.
  \item So describe each word by the company it keeps, then compress that description.
  \item Similar words get similar lists of numbers, measured by the \textit{cosine}
        $\frac{\sum_k a_k b_k}{\sqrt{\sum_k a_k^2}\sqrt{\sum_k b_k^2}}$: 1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{column}{0.58\linewidth}
\small
\textbf{Skip-gram} \srcp{Mikolov et al.\ 2013}: from each word $w$ in a text,
predict the words $c$ within a few positions of it.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Input: a 0/1 row with a single 1 for $w$. The weights from that one input
        are the word's vector $u_{w1}, \dots, u_{wK}$; nothing else reaches the
        middle layer.
  \item Output: a probability for every word in the vocabulary,
  \[
    \Pr(c \mid w) = \tfrac{\exp(\sum_k u_{wk} v_{ck})}{\sum_{c'}\exp(\sum_k u_{wk} v_{c'k})},
  \]
        the multinomial logit of Week 4 with $V$ categories; $v_{ck}$ are the
        output weights.
  \item Words with the same neighbours need the same predictions, so they get similar
        vectors $u_w$.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Negative Sampling: a Logistic Regression\adv}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The softmax denominator sums over all $V = 100{,}000$ words, for every word of
        billions. Too slow.
  \item Instead, a yes/no question: is this pair a real neighbour pair, or a fake one
        made by drawing a random word?
  \[
    \Pr(\text{real} \mid w, c) = \sigma\Big(\sum_k u_{wk} v_{ck}\Big),
    \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
  \]
        the logit of Week 4, with the product of two word vectors as the linear index.
  \item Each real pair is fed with a few fake ones \srcp{figs/w12\_repr.R, E}:
  \begin{center}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
  \toprule
  pair & kind & $\sum_k u_{wk} v_{ck}$ & $\Pr(\text{real})$ \\
  \midrule
  (tesg\"uino, drunk) & real & 2.0 & 0.88 \\
  (tesg\"uino, carburetor) & fake & $-1.5$ & 0.18 \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \item Gradient descent raises real pairs' products, lowers fake ones'; words sharing
        neighbours end up with aligned $u_w$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Word Vectors Capture, and What They Miss}
\textbf{Capture}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Similarity: words used alike (\emph{teacher}, \emph{instructor}) get a high cosine.
  \item Directions: vector(\emph{king}) $-$ vector(\emph{man}) $+$ vector(\emph{woman})
        lands closest to vector(\emph{queen}) \srcp{Mikolov et al.\ 2013}. The difference
        \emph{man} to \emph{woman} is roughly one direction, shared across pairs.
  \item The same directions carry the corpus's stereotypes: occupations sit closer to
        \emph{he} or \emph{she} as they do in the text, and track census occupation
        shares over a century \srcp{Garg et al.\ 2018}. For sociology that is data.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Miss}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{One vector per word}: \emph{bank} gets the same numbers in ``river
        bank'' and ``bank loan''.
  \item \textbf{No order}: averaging word vectors gives ``dog bites man'' and ``man
        bites dog'' the same sentence vector. Next: read the whole sentence.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: the Geometry of Culture}
\textbf{The question.} Kozlowski, Taddy, and Evans (2019): what does ``class'' mean, and how did its meaning change over the
twentieth century?
\vskip2pt
\textbf{The method.} Word vectors trained on a century of published books.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Build a dimension}: average the differences over many antonym pairs,
        vector(\emph{rich}) $-$ vector(\emph{poor}), vector(\emph{affluent}) $-$
        vector(\emph{impoverished}), \dots: an \emph{affluence} direction.
  \item \textbf{Place words on it}: the cosine of a word's vector with the direction.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip2pt
Toy three-number vectors \srcp{figs/w12\_repr.R, F; hypothetical}: rich $=(2.0, 1.0, 0.5)$,
poor $=(-1.6, 0.8, 0.4)$, so the direction is $(3.6, 0.2, 0.1)$;
golf $=(1.2, 0.3, 1.0)$ gives cosine 0.78, camping $=(-0.6, 0.2, 1.1)$ gives $-0.44$.
\vskip2pt
\textbf{The results.} Dimensions of affluence, education, cultivation, status, and more;
words' positions were checked against survey ratings of the same words. Tracking the
dimensions decade by decade shows how the meanings of class moved.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Attention: Look at Every Word at Once\core}
\begin{quote}\small
The survey asked parents about the new school. \textbf{It} was badly underfunded.
\end{quote}
\vskip-2pt
A reader links \emph{It} to \emph{school}, eight words back; a recurrent network must
carry that link through every word between. \textbf{Self-attention}
\srcp{Vaswani et al.\ 2017} lets each word look at all others directly. Each word gets
three lists of $D$ numbers, weighted sums of its word vector with learned weights:
a \textbf{query} $q$ (what it looks for), a \textbf{key} $k$ (what it offers), a
\textbf{value} $v$ (what it passes on). For word $i$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{score}_{ij} &= \frac{\sum_{d} q_{id} k_{jd}}{\sqrt D}
   && \text{\footnotesize how well word $j$ answers word $i$} \\
  \text{weight}_{ij} &= \frac{\exp(\text{score}_{ij})}{\sum_{j'}\exp(\text{score}_{ij'})}
   && \text{\footnotesize softmax: positive, sums to 1 over $j$} \\
  \text{new vector}_{i} &= \textstyle\sum_j \text{weight}_{ij}\, v_{j}
   && \text{\footnotesize weighted average of the values}
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Using a Pretrained Model on Your Data\core}
You have 2,000 hand-coded speeches and a BERT trained by someone else on billions of words.
\vskip3pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{Linear probing}: freeze BERT. Turn each text into its 768 numbers and
        fit a ridge or logistic regression on them: today's first section, with
        $p = 768$. Cheap, and hard to overfit.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning}: keep training \emph{all} of BERT's weights, plus the new
        output layer, on your labelled data; small learning rate, a few epochs, early
        stopping on a validation set. Better when the task differs from ordinary text.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \srct{Bonikowski, Luo, and Stuhler (2022)} fine-tune a pretrained language model
        to score populism, nationalism, and authoritarianism in U.S.\ presidential
        campaign speeches, 1952--2020.
  \item The cross-validation rules still hold: tune on training folds, report error on
        texts the model never saw, and split by speaker or document, not by sentence.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Text as a Control Variable: a Preview}
\textbf{The question}: how much does demand fall when price rises? Expensive products
differ in quality, and quality is in the description, not in a column.
\[
  Y_i = \beta D_i + g(W_i) + \epsilon_i
  \qquad
  \begin{array}{l}
    \text{\footnotesize $Y$: log sales, $D$: log price} \\
    \text{\footnotesize $W$: the description's BERT vector}
  \end{array}
\]
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item $g(W_i)$ is an unknown function of 768 numbers: exactly the setting of this
        week's methods.
  \item What matters is how well $W$ predicts sales and price, so fine-tune BERT to
        predict \emph{those}, not to rebuild the text \srcp{Bajari et al.\ 2025}.
  \item Sociology has the same shape: neighbourhood images as controls for
        ``quality'' when studying exposure to violence.
  \item But $\hat g$ is deliberately biased, so plugging it in and reading $\hat\beta$ is not
        valid. \textbf{Next week}: how to use such predictions in a causal estimate.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predictions as Measurements\core}
Sociologists increasingly use model outputs as \emph{variables}: an LLM codes
the topic of 100,000 speeches; a classifier labels images. Errors in those
labels bias every downstream estimate.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Prediction-powered inference} \srcp{Angelopoulos et al.\ 2023}: hand-code a
random subsample of $n$ cases; the model labels all $N$. For a mean,
\begin{align*}
  \hat\theta_{PP}
  = \underbrace{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat Y_i}_{\text{model, everyone}}
  - \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\hat Y_i - Y_i\big)}_{\text{model's bias, estimated on the coded cases}}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Unbiased whatever the model's quality; the better the model, the smaller
        the variance relative to using the $n$ coded cases alone.
  \item The same correction extends to regressions: \srct{Egami, Hinck, Stewart, and
        Wei (2023)}. The coded subsample must be random.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking Inside a Black Box\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Permutation importance}: shuffle one variable in the test data and
        record how much error rises. Correlated variables share credit
        unpredictably, and impurity-based importance favours variables with many
        cut points \srcp{Strobl et al.\ 2007}.
  \item \textbf{Partial dependence}: average prediction as one variable is set to
        each value for everyone, $\bar f(x_j) = \frac1n\sum_i \hat f(x_j, X_{i,-j})$.
        It evaluates the model at combinations (a 22-year-old with 30 years of
        experience) that never occur.
  \item \textbf{SHAP values}: split each prediction into additive contributions
        using Shapley values from game theory \srcp{Lundberg and Lee 2017}.
        Same caveat: the attribution describes the model, not the world.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{None of these is a causal effect}
They answer ``what does this fitted function do when I move this input?''
Turning that into ``what happens when a person's input changes'' needs Weeks 5--11.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Fairness]{Prediction and Fairness}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Biased Algorithm, and Why}
Obermeyer et al.\ (2019): a commercial algorithm ranks patients for a care-management program
(6,079 Black and 43,539 White patients). Above the 97th percentile of the score,
patients are enrolled automatically.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item At the same score, Black patients are sicker: at the 97th percentile they
        have 26.3\% more chronic conditions (4.8 vs.\ 3.8).
  \item \textbf{Cause: the label.} The algorithm predicts \emph{cost}. At the same
        health, Black patients generate \$1,801 less cost a year, through
        unequal access to care. The prediction is accurate; the target is wrong.
  \item Equalizing health at the threshold would raise the Black share of
        auto-enrolled patients from 17.7\% to 46.5\%.
  \item Retraining on a label combining health and cost cut the excess
        chronic conditions by 84\%.
\end{itemize}
{\footnotesize Lesson: what is measured, and for whom, is built into the outcome before any model is fit.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fairness Criteria Conflict}
A risk score $S$, groups $a$, outcome $Y$. Three reasonable requirements:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Calibration} within groups: $\Pr(Y = 1\mid S = s, a) = s$.
  \item \textbf{Balance for the negative class}: equal mean $S$ among those with $Y = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Balance for the positive class}: equal mean $S$ among those with $Y = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\srct{Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan (2017)}: all three hold only with
perfect prediction or equal base rates. For a yes/no rule
\srcp{Chouldechova 2017},
\begin{align*}
  \text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1 - \text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot(1 - \text{FNR}),
\end{align*}
with $p$ the base rate: equal PPV across groups with different $p$ forces
different error rates. COMPAS: recidivism 51\% among Black and 39\% among White
defendants, so the two sides of that debate were each right about a different
criterion.
\end{frame}

\section[Practice]{Practice}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{What ML Buys, and What It Does Not\core}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Buys}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item Prediction with many predictors, including $p > n$.
  \item Automatic nonlinearity and interactions, no functional form to guess.
  \item Honest out-of-sample performance estimates.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{Does not buy}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item \alert{Causal interpretation.} A variable importance score is not an
        effect.
  \item Valid standard errors for the coefficients it selects.
  \item Extrapolation: trees do return a prediction for out-of-range inputs, but
        only the boundary leaf's constant, so they cannot extend a trend.
  \item Freedom from the identification problems of Weeks 5--11.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip5pt
\begin{block}{The genuine sociological uses}
\emph{Prediction as the goal} (screening, triage, forecasting);
\emph{measurement} (constructing variables from text or images);
\emph{describing heterogeneity}; and \alert{estimating nuisance functions inside a
causal estimator}, which is next week.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking Ahead}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Every method today trades bias for variance, tuned by cross-validation.
  \item That trade is exactly why we cannot drop these estimates into a regression
        and read off a coefficient: they are \emph{deliberately biased} and
        converge slowly.
  \item \textbf{Next week}: what has to change before an estimate this biased can
        be used as an ingredient in a causal estimator, and what it buys us when
        it can.
\end{itemize}
