\begin{frame}{A Different Goal\core}
Weeks 5--11 asked: \emph{what is the causal effect, and what must be true to
recover it?} Today we set that aside entirely.
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{Prediction versus inference}
\textbf{Inference}: we care about a parameter $\beta$, its interpretation, and
its standard error. \\
\textbf{Prediction}: we care only about $\hat Y$ for units we have not seen. The
parameters are means, not ends, and may not even be identified.
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item These goals conflict. The model that predicts best is usually not the
        model whose coefficients you would want to interpret, and vice versa.
  \item \srct{Molina and Garip (2019)}: sociology has plenty of genuine
        \emph{prediction} problems (who drops out, which neighbourhoods gentrify,
        which cases to audit), and separate ML tools for describing heterogeneity.
  \item Next week we put the two back together. Today, keep them apart.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\textbf{Goal}: predict $Y$ for units we have not seen, and know how well we did.

\section[Bias-Variance]{The Bias--Variance Tradeoff}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Decomposing Prediction Error\core}
Let $Y = f(X) + \epsilon$ with $\E[\epsilon\mid X]=0$ and
$\Var(\epsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2$, and let $\hat f$ be estimated from a training
sample drawn independently of the new observation. At a new point $x_0$, taking
expectations over \emph{both} the training sample and the new outcome:
\begin{align*}
  \E\Big[\big(Y_0 - \hat f(x_0)\big)^2\Big]
  = \underbrace{\Big(\E[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\Big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2}
  + \underbrace{\Var\big(\hat f(x_0)\big)}_{\text{variance}}
  + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}} .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Bias}: how far the method is wrong on average, across training
        samples. Rigid models (a straight line) have high bias.
  \item \textbf{Variance}: how much $\hat f$ moves if you redraw the training
        data. Flexible models (a deep tree) have high variance.
  \item \textbf{Irreducible error}: noise. No method beats it.
  \item \alert{Every tuning parameter in this course is a dial between the first
        two terms.} Penalty size, tree depth, number of neighbours, bandwidth
        (Week 9!), all the same dial.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Decomposition, Proved}
At a fixed $x_0$, write $f = f(x_0)$ and $\hat f = \hat f(x_0)$; the new outcome is
$Y_0 = f + \epsilon_0$ with $\epsilon_0$ independent of the training data:
\begin{align*}
  \E\big[(Y_0 - \hat f)^2\big]
  &= \E\big[(\epsilon_0 + f - \hat f)^2\big]
   && \text{\footnotesize substitute $Y_0$} \\
  &= \E[\epsilon_0^2] + 2\,\E[\epsilon_0]\,\E[f - \hat f] + \E\big[(f - \hat f)^2\big]
   && \text{\footnotesize $\epsilon_0$ independent of $\hat f$} \\
  &= \sigma^2 + \E\big[(f - \E\hat f + \E\hat f - \hat f)^2\big]
   && \text{\footnotesize $\E[\epsilon_0] = 0$; add and subtract $\E\hat f$} \\
  &= \sigma^2 + (f - \E\hat f)^2 + \E\big[(\hat f - \E\hat f)^2\big]
   && \text{\footnotesize cross term has mean zero}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Expectations are over \emph{training samples}: bias and variance describe
        a method, not one fitted model.
  \item The same algebra gave the MSE of the RD estimator (Week 9) and the
        bandwidth choice; here the dial is model complexity.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why Unbiasedness Stops Being the Goal\core}
Weeks 1--2 prized unbiasedness: Gauss--Markov ranked estimators \emph{within} the
unbiased class.
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{For prediction, that is the wrong criterion}
We care about total error. Accepting a little bias to remove a lot of variance
lowers MSE. Every method today is deliberately biased.
\end{alertblock}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item OLS with $k$ regressors has variance growing in $k$: with $p/n$ not small,
        the variance term swamps everything and OLS predicts badly even though it
        is unbiased.
  \item When $p > n$, OLS is not even defined, $\X'\X$ is singular.
  \item \textbf{In-sample fit is not evidence.} $R^2$ never falls when you add
        regressors, so training error is a systematically optimistic estimate of
        test error. The gap is the \emph{optimism}, and it grows with model
        complexity.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How Optimistic Is Training Error?}
OLS with $p$ regressors (with intercept), fixed $\X$, $\Var(\epsilon) = \sigma^2\Ix$,
hat matrix $\bm H = \X(\X'\X)^{-1}\X'$, $\trace(\bm H) = p$:
\begin{align*}
  \E\Big[\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i (Y_i - \hat Y_i)^2\Big]
  &= \tfrac1n\E\big[\bm\epsilon'(\Ix - \bm H)\bm\epsilon\big] = \sigma^2\,\tfrac{n - p}{n}
   && \text{\footnotesize residual $= (\Ix - \bm H)\bm\epsilon$; trace} \\
  \E\Big[\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i (Y_i^{\text{new}} - \hat Y_i)^2\Big]
  &= \sigma^2 + \tfrac1n\E\big[\bm\epsilon'\bm H\bm\epsilon\big] = \sigma^2\,\tfrac{n + p}{n}
   && \text{\footnotesize new noise at the same $\X$} \\
  \text{optimism} &= \tfrac{2\,p\,\sigma^2}{n}
   && \text{\footnotesize subtract}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Training error is too low by $2p\sigma^2/n$: Mallows' $C_p$ adds it back,
        and AIC (Week 3) is the likelihood version of the same correction.
  \item The penalty grows with $p/n$, not with $p$: 50 predictors are harmless with
        $n = 100{,}000$ and fatal with $n = 100$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Estimating Test Error: Cross-Validation\core}
Split the data, fit on part, evaluate on the rest.
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{$K$-fold cross-validation}
Partition the sample into $K$ folds. For $k=1,\dots,K$: fit on all folds but
$k$, predict fold $k$, record the error. Average:
\begin{align*}
  CV_K = \frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{1}{|\mathcal I_k|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_k}
  \Big(Y_i - \hat f^{(-k)}(X_i)\Big)^2 .
\end{align*}
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $K = 5$ or $10$ is standard: leave-one-out ($K=n$) has low bias but high
        variance and is expensive.
  \item \alert{Everything data-dependent must happen inside the fold}, 
        variable selection, standardization, imputation. Selecting predictors on
        the full sample and then cross-validating is a very common and very
        serious leak.
  \item With dependence (panels, clusters, time) \alert{split on the
        independent unit}, not on rows. Same rule as the bootstrap in Week 2.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Cross-Validation Estimates \srcp{Bates, Hastie, and Tibshirani 2024}}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item CV refits the model $K$ times on different training sets, so it estimates
        the \alert{average} test error of the \emph{procedure} over training sets
        of size $n(K-1)/K$, not the error of the one model you finally fit.
  \item The usual interval $\widehat{CV} \pm 2\,\widehat{\text{se}}$, treating the $n$
        held-out errors as independent, undercovers: the folds share training
        data, so the errors are correlated. \textbf{Nested CV} fixes the interval.
  \item \textbf{Tuning and evaluating are two jobs}. If $\lambda$ is chosen by CV,
        the CV error at the chosen $\lambda$ is optimistic; report error on a
        held-out test set never used for tuning, or nest the tuning inside each fold.
  \item \textbf{One-standard-error rule}: among $\lambda$ within one se of the minimum,
        take the simplest. GSS: lasso keeps 48 of 294 terms at the minimum, 12
        under the 1-se rule; test $R^2$ 0.344 vs.\ 0.333.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Regularization]{Regularization}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Ridge Regression\core}
Add a penalty on the squared size of the coefficients:
\begin{align*}
  \hat{\bm\beta}^{\text{ridge}}
  = \argmin_{\bm\beta}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i - X_i'\bm\beta\big)^2
  + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_j^2 ,
  \qquad
  \hat{\bm\beta}^{\text{ridge}} = (\X'\X + \lambda \Ix)^{-1}\X'\Y ,
\end{align*}
where, as always with a penalty, $\X$ and $\Y$ are centred and the
intercept is left out of both the penalty and the displayed matrix.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Adding $\lambda\Ix$ makes the matrix invertible \alert{even when
        $p > n$}, which is why ridge works where OLS does not exist.
  \item Coefficients shrink toward zero but never reach it: ridge does not select.
  \item \textbf{Always standardize the predictors first}; the penalty is not
        scale-invariant, and the intercept is not penalized.
  \item Ridge shines under \alert{dense} signals: many small effects, and
        correlated predictors whose coefficients OLS cannot separate.
  \item As $\lambda\to0$ we recover OLS; as $\lambda\to\infty$ all slopes go to
        zero and the fit collapses to the sample mean of $Y$. The dial is the
        bias--variance dial.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ridge Through the Singular Value Decomposition}
Write $\X = \bm U\bm D\bm V'$ with singular values $d_1 \geq \dots \geq d_p$ and
columns $\bm u_j$ of $\bm U$:
\begin{align*}
  \X\hat{\bm\beta}^{\text{OLS}}
  &= \bm U\bm U'\Y = \textstyle\sum_j \bm u_j\,(\bm u_j'\Y)
   && \text{\footnotesize projection on the column space} \\
  \X\hat{\bm\beta}^{\text{ridge}}
  &= \X(\X'\X + \lambda\Ix)^{-1}\X'\Y
   = \textstyle\sum_j \bm u_j\,\dfrac{d_j^2}{d_j^2 + \lambda}\,(\bm u_j'\Y)
   && \text{\footnotesize $\X'\X = \bm V\bm D^2\bm V'$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Ridge shrinks each principal direction by $d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)$: directions
        in which the predictors barely vary ($d_j$ small, the collinear ones) are
        shrunk most. That is where OLS variance comes from.
  \item \textbf{Effective degrees of freedom}:
        $\text{df}(\lambda) = \trace\big[\X(\X'\X+\lambda\Ix)^{-1}\X'\big] = \sum_j d_j^2/(d_j^2+\lambda)$,
        from $p$ at $\lambda = 0$ down to 0. Plug it into the optimism formula.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Little Bias Always Helps \srcp{Hoerl and Kennard 1970}}
One centred regressor, $s = \sum_i x_i^2$; ridge gives $\hat\beta_\lambda = \hat\beta\,s/(s + \lambda)$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{bias}(\hat\beta_\lambda) &= -\frac{\lambda}{s+\lambda}\,\beta,
  \qquad
  \Var(\hat\beta_\lambda) = \Big(\frac{s}{s+\lambda}\Big)^2\frac{\sigma^2}{s}
   && \text{\footnotesize shrink a constant fraction} \\
  \text{MSE}(\lambda) &= \frac{\lambda^2\beta^2 + s\,\sigma^2}{(s+\lambda)^2}
   && \text{\footnotesize bias$^2$ + variance} \\
  \frac{d\,\text{MSE}}{d\lambda}\Big|_{\lambda = 0} &= -\frac{2\sigma^2}{s^2} < 0
   && \text{\footnotesize differentiate, evaluate at 0}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Moving away from OLS \emph{always} lowers MSE at first, whatever $\beta$ is:
        the variance falls at first order, the bias enters squared. The best
        $\lambda^* = \sigma^2/\beta^2$ (set the derivative to zero).
  \item The same is true with many regressors: some $\lambda > 0$ beats OLS.
        Unbiasedness is a choice, not an optimum.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Lasso\core}
Penalize the \emph{absolute} size instead:
\begin{align*}
  \hat{\bm\beta}^{\text{lasso}}
  = \argmin_{\bm\beta}\ \sum_{i=1}^n\big(Y_i - X_i'\bm\beta\big)^2
  + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p}\big|\beta_j\big| .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \alert{The $\ell_1$ penalty sets coefficients exactly to zero}, so lasso
        performs variable selection and estimation simultaneously.
  \item Geometrically: the $\ell_1$ constraint region is a diamond with corners on
        the axes, and the contours of the sum of squares typically first touch it
        at a corner. The $\ell_2$ region is a ball with no corners, hence no
        exact zeros.
  \item Lasso suits \alert{approximate sparsity}: a few predictors matter, the
        rest are approximately irrelevant.
  \item \textbf{Elastic net} mixes them,
        $\lambda\big(\alpha\sum|\beta_j| + \tfrac{1-\alpha}{2}\sum\beta_j^2\big)$,
        which handles groups of correlated predictors better than pure lasso.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Penalties, One Coefficient}
Orthonormal design ($\X'\X = \Ix$), OLS estimate $b_j$. Minimize
$(b_j - \beta_j)^2 + \text{penalty}$ coefficient by coefficient:
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.48\linewidth}
\begin{align*}
  \text{ridge, } \lambda\beta^2: &\quad \hat\beta_j = \frac{b_j}{1+\lambda} \\
  \text{lasso, } \lambda|\beta|: &\quad \hat\beta_j = \text{sign}(b_j)\big(|b_j| - \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\big)_+ \\
  \text{best subset}: &\quad \hat\beta_j = b_j\,\mathds 1\{|b_j| > \sqrt{\lambda}\}
\end{align*}
{\footnotesize Lasso: for $\beta_j > 0$ the derivative $-2(b_j - \beta_j) + \lambda = 0$
gives $b_j - \lambda/2$; if that is negative the minimum sits at 0.}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.5\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.45]
  \draw[->] (-3.2,0) -- (3.4,0) node[right]{\scriptsize $b_j$};
  \draw[->] (0,-3.0) -- (0,3.2) node[above]{\scriptsize $\hat\beta_j$};
  \draw[gray,dashed] (-3,-3) -- (3,3);
  \draw[dukeblue,very thick] (-3,-2) -- (3,2);
  \draw[accent,very thick] (-3,-2.2) -- (-0.8,0) -- (0.8,0) -- (3,2.2);
  \draw[black,thick] (-3,-3) -- (-1.2,-1.2); \draw[black,thick] (-1.2,0) -- (1.2,0); \draw[black,thick] (1.2,1.2) -- (3,3);
  \node[dukeblue] at (2.4,1.15) {\scriptsize ridge};
  \node[accent] at (1.65,2.25) {\scriptsize lasso};
  \node at (2.0,3.05) {\scriptsize subset};
  \node[gray] at (-2.3,-2.85) {\scriptsize OLS};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item Ridge scales everything down; lasso subtracts a constant and stops at
        zero (\textbf{soft thresholding}); best subset keeps or kills (hard).
  \item Lasso's degrees of freedom are, on average, the number of nonzero
        coefficients \srcp{Zou, Hastie, and Tibshirani 2007}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Choosing $\lambda$, and a Warning About Interpretation\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Cross-validation} over a grid of $\lambda$: pick the minimizer, or
        the ``one standard error'' rule, the largest $\lambda$ within one
        standard error of the minimum, which buys a simpler model at almost no
        predictive cost.
  \item There are also \textbf{theoretically derived} penalties that set
        $\lambda$ from the model rather than by cross-validation. They matter when
        the lasso is a \emph{step inside} an inference procedure rather than the
        final answer.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{Lasso selection is not a variable-importance ranking}
Among correlated predictors, lasso picks one essentially arbitrarily and zeroes
the rest. Re-run on a bootstrap sample and you may get a different set. \alert{Do
not interpret the selected set as ``the variables that matter'',} and do not
report post-lasso $p$-values from a naive refit, the selection invalidates
them. Valid post-selection inference is exactly what Week 13 is for.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Trees]{Trees and Ensembles}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Regression Trees\core}
Ridge and lasso control complexity, but both still start from an \emph{additive
linear} rule: you choose the variables, and an interaction exists only if you
type it. Trees are the first method we meet that finds nonlinearities and
interactions on its own.
\vskip3pt
Recursively split the covariate space to minimize within-node sum of squares; the
prediction in a leaf is the mean of the training outcomes there.
\vskip3pt
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.78,>=Stealth,thick,font=\scriptsize]
  \node[draw=dukeblue,rounded corners,inner sep=3pt] (r) at (0,2.4) {educ $< 13$?};
  \node[draw=dukeblue,rounded corners,inner sep=3pt] (l) at (-2.2,1.1) {exper $< 20$?};
  \node[draw=accent,rounded corners,inner sep=3pt] (rr) at (2.2,1.1) {$\hat Y = 10.6$};
  \node[draw=accent,rounded corners,inner sep=3pt] (ll) at (-3.6,-0.1) {$\hat Y = 9.2$};
  \node[draw=accent,rounded corners,inner sep=3pt] (lr) at (-1.0,-0.1) {$\hat Y = 9.8$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (r)--(l) node[midway,above left]{yes};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (r)--(rr) node[midway,above right]{no};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (l)--(ll); \draw[->,dukeblue] (l)--(lr);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Strengths}: automatic interactions and nonlinearity; invariant to
        monotone transformations; handles mixed variable types; readable.
  \item \alert{Weaknesses}: high variance, a small change in the data can
        change the first split and hence everything below it. A single deep tree
        overfits badly; a shallow one underfits.
  \item Pruning with a complexity penalty helps but does not solve it. The real
        fix is to average many trees.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Growing and Pruning a Tree}
\textbf{Grow}: at each node, over every variable $j$ and cut point $s$, split
into $R_1 = \{x_j < s\}$ and $R_2 = \{x_j \geq s\}$ to minimize
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{i \in R_1}(Y_i - \bar Y_{R_1})^2 + \sum_{i \in R_2}(Y_i - \bar Y_{R_2})^2 ;
\end{align*}
repeat in each child until nodes are small. Greedy: each split is best given the
splits above it, not best overall.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Prune}: among subtrees $T$ of the big tree, minimize
\begin{align*}
  C_\alpha(T) = \sum_{\text{leaves } m}\ \sum_{i\in R_m}(Y_i - \bar Y_{R_m})^2 + \alpha\,|T| ,
\end{align*}
with $|T|$ the number of leaves and $\alpha$ by cross-validation: the lasso idea,
penalizing size.
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize GSS: a CV-pruned tree reaches test $R^2$ 0.313, below OLS (0.338). One tree is
too rough a step function for a mostly smooth relationship.}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Bagging, Random Forests, Boosting\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Bagging}: fit trees on bootstrap resamples and average. Averaging
        $B$ nearly independent estimates cuts variance by roughly $1/B$ without
        adding bias. But bootstrap trees are \emph{correlated}, they all split
        on the same dominant predictor first, which caps the gain.
  \item \textbf{Random forests}: bag, but at each split consider only a random
        subset of $m$ predictors. Decorrelating the trees is what makes the
        averaging work. $m$ is the main tuning parameter, alongside node size.
        Out-of-bag error is a free cross-validation estimate.
  \item \textbf{Boosting}: fit trees \emph{sequentially}, each on the residuals of
        the current ensemble, adding a shrunken version:
        $\hat f \leftarrow \hat f + \eta\,\hat t$. Attacks \emph{bias} rather than
        variance, and with small $\eta$ and many trees is usually the strongest
        tabular predictor available.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\alert{Bagging and random forests reduce variance; boosting reduces bias.} That
is the cleanest way to keep them straight.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Decorrelating Trees Works}
$B$ trees, each with variance $\sigma^2$ at a point, pairwise correlation $\rho$:
\begin{align*}
  \Var\Big(\frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B T_b\Big)
  &= \frac{1}{B^2}\Big(\sum_b \Var(T_b) + \sum_{b\neq b'}\Cov(T_b, T_{b'})\Big)
   && \text{\footnotesize variance of a sum} \\
  &= \frac{1}{B^2}\big(B\sigma^2 + B(B-1)\rho\sigma^2\big)
   && \text{\footnotesize $B(B-1)$ ordered pairs} \\
  &= \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\,\sigma^2
   && \text{\footnotesize rearrange}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item More trees kill the second term only. The first, $\rho\sigma^2$, is a
        floor: bagged trees on the same data are correlated.
  \item Random forests lower $\rho$ by letting each split see only $m$ of the $p$
        variables; each tree gets worse, the average gets better.
  \item Out-of-bag error: each tree skips about a third of the data
        ($(1-1/n)^n \approx e^{-1}$). GSS: OOB MSE 0.547, test MSE 0.556.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boosting Is Gradient Descent}
Loss $L(\bm f) = \sum_i \tfrac12 (Y_i - f_i)^2$ over the vector of fitted values:
\begin{align*}
  -\frac{\partial L}{\partial f_i} &= Y_i - f_i
   && \text{\footnotesize the residual is the negative gradient} \\
  \hat f^{(m)} &= \hat f^{(m-1)} + \eta\,\hat t_m(x)
   && \text{\footnotesize $\hat t_m$: a tree fit to the residuals}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Each tree is small (depth 3 here) and shrunk by $\eta$ (0.05 here): many
        weak steps rather than a few big ones \srcp{Friedman 2001}.
  \item Any differentiable loss works: logistic for binary $Y$, Poisson for counts
        (Week 4); the residual becomes the gradient of that loss.
  \item The number of steps is the complexity dial; stop when CV error turns up.
        GSS: about a few hundred rounds.
  \item \texttt{xgboost} adds penalties on leaf values and is the usual default
        for tables of data \srcp{Chen and Guestrin 2016}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

expanded design; tuning by CV inside the training set \srcp{figs/w12\_gss.R}:

\midrule

\begin{frame}{Combining Learners: Stacking}
No method wins everywhere: lasso won the small-sample GSS race among linear
models, the forest won overall. \textbf{Stacking} lets the data choose a mix
\srcp{van der Laan, Polley, and Hubbard 2007}:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Fit each candidate $\hat f_1,\dots,\hat f_M$ with $K$-fold
        cross-validation; keep the out-of-fold predictions $\hat f_m^{(-k)}(X_i)$.
  \item Regress $Y_i$ on the out-of-fold predictions with non-negative weights
        summing to one: $\min_{a}\sum_i\big(Y_i - \sum_m a_m\hat f_m^{(-k)}(X_i)\big)^2$.
  \item Predict with $\sum_m \hat a_m\hat f_m$, each refit on all data.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Out-of-fold predictions keep a learner that overfits from getting weight
        it did not earn.
  \item Asymptotically at least as good as the best single candidate
        (the ``super learner'' oracle result). Next week it supplies the nuisance
        functions inside DML.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Binary Outcomes: Loss, Thresholds, Calibration}
For $Y \in \{0,1\}$ every method above predicts $\hat p(x) = \widehat{\Pr}(Y=1\mid x)$:
lasso and ridge on the logistic log-likelihood (Week 3), trees on the Gini
index or deviance, boosting on the logistic loss.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Classification is a second step}: predict 1 when $\hat p(x) > c$. The
        cutoff $c$ is a decision about costs (a missed dropout vs.\ an unneeded
        intervention), not a statistical quantity; 0.5 is rarely right.
  \item \textbf{Ranking}: the AUC is the chance a random positive case gets a
        higher $\hat p$ than a random negative one; it ignores the cutoff and
        the level of $\hat p$.
  \item \textbf{Calibration}: among cases with $\hat p \approx 0.3$, do 30\% have
        $Y = 1$? Flexible models often rank well and calibrate badly; check a
        calibration plot on held-out data.
  \item \textbf{Rare outcomes}: accuracy is useless (predict ``no'' for everyone);
        report AUC, calibration, and precision among the flagged.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Neural Nets]{Neural Networks}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Goal: Learn the Features Too}
Lasso chooses among features we built (interactions, squares). Trees build
features by splitting. A neural network \alert{learns its own features} as
smooth functions of the inputs, then regresses on them.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Plan}:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item One hidden layer: the model, activations, and why nonlinearity matters.
  \item What a network can represent: an interaction from two neurons;
        universal approximation; counting parameters.
  \item Fitting: gradient descent, backpropagation, stochastic gradients.
  \item Regularizing: weight decay, dropout, early stopping.
  \item A network on the GSS.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize Notation follows ISL Ch.\ 10: inputs $X_1,\dots,X_p$, $K$ hidden units,
activation $\sigma(\cdot)$, weights $w$, output coefficients $\beta$.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Single Hidden Layer}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.42\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85,>=Stealth,
  nd/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,thick,minimum size=6mm,inner sep=0pt,font=\scriptsize}]
  \foreach \i in {1,...,4} \node[nd] (x\i) at (0,3.6-1.0*\i) {$X_\i$};
  \foreach \k in {1,...,5} \node[nd,fill=dukeblue!10] (a\k) at (2,4.0-1.0*\k) {$A_\k$};
  \node[nd,draw=accent,fill=accent!12] (f) at (4,1.0) {$f$};
  \foreach \i in {1,...,4} \foreach \k in {1,...,5} \draw[->,gray!70] (x\i) -- (a\k);
  \foreach \k in {1,...,5} \draw[->,dukeblue] (a\k) -- (f);
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (0,-1.3) {input};
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (2,-1.3) {hidden};
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (4,-1.3) {output};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.56\linewidth}
\begin{align*}
  A_k &= \sigma\Big(w_{k0} + \textstyle\sum_{j=1}^p w_{kj}X_j\Big), \quad k = 1,\dots,K \\
  f(X) &= \beta_0 + \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^K \beta_k A_k
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Each hidden unit is a linear index of the inputs pushed through a fixed
        nonlinear $\sigma$: a logistic regression without an outcome.
  \item The output is a linear regression on the $K$ learned features.
  \item $w$ and $\beta$ are chosen together to minimize
        $\sum_i (Y_i - f(X_i))^2$.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Activations, and Why They Must Be Nonlinear}
\begin{align*}
  \text{sigmoid:}\quad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}
  \qquad\qquad
  \text{ReLU:}\quad \sigma(z) = \max(0, z)
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
Suppose $\sigma(z) = z$ instead:
\begin{align*}
  f(X) &= \beta_0 + \textstyle\sum_k \beta_k\big(w_{k0} + \sum_j w_{kj}X_j\big)
   && \text{\footnotesize substitute the identity} \\
  &= \underbrace{\big(\beta_0 + \textstyle\sum_k\beta_k w_{k0}\big)}_{\text{intercept}}
   + \textstyle\sum_j \underbrace{\big(\textstyle\sum_k \beta_k w_{kj}\big)}_{\text{slope on } X_j} X_j
   && \text{\footnotesize regroup}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \alert{A linear network of any width or depth is one linear regression.}
        All the flexibility comes from $\sigma$.
  \item ReLU is the modern default: cheap to compute, gradient 1 where active.
        With the intercept $w_{k0}$, each unit is a hinge at its own location:
        ReLU networks are piecewise-linear functions.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Neurons Make an Interaction}
Two inputs, two hidden units, $\sigma(z) = z^2$; weights $w_1 = (0, 1, 1)$,
$w_2 = (0, 1, -1)$, $\beta = (0, \tfrac14, -\tfrac14)$:
\begin{align*}
  A_1 &= (X_1 + X_2)^2, \qquad A_2 = (X_1 - X_2)^2
   && \text{\footnotesize the two hidden units} \\
  f(X) &= \tfrac14 (X_1 + X_2)^2 - \tfrac14 (X_1 - X_2)^2
   && \text{\footnotesize output layer} \\
  &= \tfrac14\big[(X_1^2 + 2X_1X_2 + X_2^2) - (X_1^2 - 2X_1X_2 + X_2^2)\big] = X_1X_2
   && \text{\footnotesize expand}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The interaction was never typed in. The network built it from linear
        indices and a nonlinearity.
  \item \textbf{Universal approximation}: with a non-polynomial activation and
        enough hidden units, one hidden layer approximates any continuous
        function on a bounded set \srcp{Hornik, Stinchcombe, and White 1989}.
        Existence, not a recipe: it says nothing about how many units, or how to
        find the weights.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Deeper Networks, and Counting Parameters}
Stack layers: layer $\ell$ treats layer $\ell-1$'s activations as inputs,
\begin{align*}
  A^{(\ell)}_m = \sigma\Big(w^{(\ell)}_{m0} + \textstyle\sum_k w^{(\ell)}_{mk} A^{(\ell-1)}_k\Big).
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
\toprule
network & count & parameters \\
\midrule
GSS, 25 inputs, 2 hidden units & $(25+1)\cdot 2 + (2+1)$ & 55 \\
MNIST digits: $784 \to 256 \to 128 \to 10$ & $785\cdot256 + 257\cdot128 + 129\cdot10$ & 235,146 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Depth builds features of features (edges, then shapes, then digits), often
        with far fewer units than one wide layer would need.
  \item 235,146 parameters for 60,000 training images: more parameters than cases,
        and still it predicts well. Regularization and the fitting algorithm, not
        the parameter count, control the effective complexity.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fitting: Gradient Descent}
Collect all weights in $\theta$; minimize $R(\theta) = \tfrac12\sum_i (Y_i - f_\theta(X_i))^2$.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Not convex: many local minima and saddle points; no closed form.
  \item Start from small random $\theta^0$ and step downhill:
\end{itemize}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{align*}
  \theta^{t+1} = \theta^{t} - \rho\,\nabla R(\theta^{t}),
\end{align*}
with \textbf{learning rate} $\rho$: too large overshoots, too small crawls.
\vskip2pt
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8]
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (7.2,0) node[right]{\scriptsize $\theta$};
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,2.7) node[above]{\scriptsize $R(\theta)$};
  \draw[dukeblue,very thick,smooth,domain=0.3:6.4,samples=80,variable=\x]
    plot ({\x},{0.35+0.04*(\x-1.6)^2*(\x-5.3)^2 + 0.12*\x});
  \foreach \x/\y in {6.2/1.78,5.8/1.22,5.4/1.00} \fill[accent] (\x,\y) circle (2pt);
  \draw[->,accent,thick] (6.15,1.70) -- (5.86,1.30);
  \draw[->,accent,thick] (5.75,1.16) -- (5.47,1.04);
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (1.6,0.25) {global};
  \node[font=\scriptsize] at (5.0,0.7) {local};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
{\footnotesize Started on the right, descent settles in the local minimum. Random restarts and
averaging (5 starts for the GSS network) are the usual defences.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backpropagation: the Chain Rule, Organized}
One case, one hidden layer; $z_k = w_{k0} + \sum_j w_{kj}X_j$, $A_k = \sigma(z_k)$,
residual $r = Y - f$, loss $R = \tfrac12 r^2$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial R}{\partial \beta_k}
  &= \frac{\partial R}{\partial f}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial \beta_k}
   = -\,r\cdot A_k
   && \text{\footnotesize output weights} \\
  \frac{\partial R}{\partial w_{kj}}
  &= \frac{\partial R}{\partial f}\cdot\frac{\partial f}{\partial A_k}\cdot\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial z_k}\cdot\frac{\partial z_k}{\partial w_{kj}}
   = -\,r\cdot\beta_k\cdot\sigma'(z_k)\cdot X_j
   && \text{\footnotesize hidden weights}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Both gradients start from the residual $r$: compute $f$ forward, then
        pass $r$ backward, multiplying by each layer's local derivative.
  \item Deep networks repeat the second line once per layer. If $\sigma'$ is small
        (sigmoid in its flat tails), products of many small numbers vanish:
        one reason ReLU replaced sigmoid.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stochastic Gradients, and Regularization}
\textbf{Stochastic gradient descent}: use a random mini-batch $B_t$ per step,
\begin{align*}
  \theta^{t+1} = \theta^t - \rho\,\frac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{i\in B_t}\nabla R_i(\theta^t);
\end{align*}
one pass through all batches is an \emph{epoch}. Cheap steps, and the noise helps
leave poor regions.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Regularization}:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Weight decay}: add $\lambda\sum\theta_j^2$ (ridge); each step also
        shrinks $\theta$ by $2\rho\lambda\theta$.
  \item \textbf{Dropout}: in each step, zero a random share $\phi$ of the hidden
        units and scale the rest by $1/(1-\phi)$; no unit can rely on another.
  \item \textbf{Early stopping}: watch error on a validation set; stop when it
        rises. For linear models, stopping gradient descent early acts like a
        ridge penalty.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize Tune architecture (layers, units), regularization ($\lambda$, $\phi$), and
optimization (learning rate, batch size, epochs), usually on a validation set.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Network on the GSS}
One hidden layer (\texttt{nnet}), inputs standardized; hidden units
$\{2, 5, 10, 20\}$ and weight decay $\{0.01, 0.1, 1\}$ by 5-fold CV; average of
5 random starts \srcp{figs/w12\_gss.R}.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item CV picks the \alert{smallest} network: 2 hidden units, decay 0.1, 55
        parameters.
  \item Test $R^2$ 0.342: the same as lasso (0.344), below random forests (0.373)
        and boosting (0.381). With 300 training cases: 0.256, below OLS.
  \item Nothing in 25 survey variables needs learned representations; the
        signal is mostly additive and smooth, and trees capture the few
        thresholds.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
This matches the benchmark literature: on tables of data, tree ensembles still
beat deep networks \srcp{Grinsztajn, Oyallon, and Varoquaux 2022}. Networks win
when the input is unstructured: text, images, audio.
\end{frame}

\section[Beyond]{Beyond: Text, Images, and Foundation Models}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Representations: Words as Vectors}
\textbf{Goal}: turn unstructured inputs into numbers a model can use.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Word embeddings}: train a network to predict a word from its
        neighbours; the hidden-layer weights give each word a vector (say 300
        numbers). Words used in similar contexts get similar vectors.
  \item Directions mean something: vector(\emph{rich}) $-$ vector(\emph{poor}),
        averaged over many antonym pairs, defines an affluence dimension;
        projecting other words onto it measures their class connotation.
  \item \srct{Kozlowski, Taddy, and Evans (2019, ASR)}: dimensions of class
        (affluence, education, cultivation) in a century of books, and how
        their meanings moved.
  \item \textbf{Images}: convolutional networks share weights across locations, so
        a detector for a shape works anywhere in the image.
        \srct{Gebru et al.\ (2017)}: cars in Google Street View images predict
        neighbourhood income, race, and voting.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Attention and Transformers}
A sentence is a sequence of word vectors $x_1,\dots,x_T$. Each position builds a
query $q_t$, key $k_t$, and value $v_t$ (three learned linear maps of $x_t$):
\begin{align*}
  \text{output}_t = \sum_{s=1}^{T} \alpha_{ts}\,v_s,
  \qquad
  \alpha_{ts} = \frac{\exp(q_t'k_s/\sqrt d)}{\sum_{s'}\exp(q_t'k_{s'}/\sqrt d)}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Each word's new representation is a weighted average of all words, with
        weights from how well its query matches their keys: context decides
        meaning (\emph{bank} near \emph{river} vs.\ near \emph{loan}).
  \item Stack many such layers, train on billions of words to predict the next
        word, and you have a large language model \srcp{Vaswani et al.\ 2017}.
  \item The softmax weights are the same logic as the multinomial logit (Week 4).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predictions as Measurements}
Sociologists increasingly use model outputs as \emph{variables}: an LLM codes
the topic of 100,000 speeches; a classifier labels images. Errors in those
labels bias every downstream estimate.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Prediction-powered inference} \srcp{Angelopoulos et al.\ 2023}: hand-code a
random subsample of $n$ cases; the model labels all $N$. For a mean,
\begin{align*}
  \hat\theta_{PP}
  = \underbrace{\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat Y_i}_{\text{model, everyone}}
  - \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\hat Y_i - Y_i\big)}_{\text{model's bias, estimated on the coded cases}}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Unbiased whatever the model's quality; the better the model, the smaller
        the variance relative to using the $n$ coded cases alone.
  \item The same correction extends to regressions: \srct{Egami, Hinck, Stewart, and
        Wei (2023)}. The coded subsample must be random.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Interpretation]{Interpretation and Uncertainty}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking Inside a Black Box}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Permutation importance}: shuffle one variable in the test data and
        record how much error rises. Correlated variables share credit
        unpredictably, and impurity-based importance favours variables with many
        cut points \srcp{Strobl et al.\ 2007}.
  \item \textbf{Partial dependence}: average prediction as one variable is set to
        each value for everyone, $\bar f(x_j) = \frac1n\sum_i \hat f(x_j, X_{i,-j})$.
        It evaluates the model at combinations (a 22-year-old with 30 years of
        experience) that never occur.
  \item \textbf{SHAP values}: split each prediction into additive contributions
        using Shapley values from game theory \srcp{Lundberg and Lee 2017}.
        Same caveat: the attribution describes the model, not the world.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{None of these is a causal effect}
They answer ``what does this fitted function do when I move this input?''
Turning that into ``what happens when a person's input changes'' needs Weeks 5--11.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Prediction Intervals Without a Model: Split Conformal}
\srct{Lei, G'Sell, Rinaldo, Tibshirani, and Wasserman (2018)}: any $\hat f$, any
distribution, exchangeable data.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Split the sample. Fit $\hat f$ on part 1.
  \item On part 2 ($n_2$ cases), compute absolute residuals $R_i = |Y_i - \hat f(X_i)|$.
  \item Let $\hat q$ be the $\lceil (n_2+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest $R_i$.
  \item Interval for a new case: $\hat f(x) \pm \hat q$.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Why it works}: the new residual is exchangeable with the $n_2$ calibration
residuals, so its rank among them is uniform; the chance it exceeds $\hat q$ is at
most $\alpha$. Then
\begin{align*}
  \Pr\big(Y_{\text{new}} \in \hat f(X_{\text{new}}) \pm \hat q\big) \geq 1 - \alpha
\end{align*}
in finite samples, whether or not $\hat f$ is any good. A bad $\hat f$ gives a wide
interval, not a wrong one. Coverage is on average over $x$, not at each $x$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Changed With Very Flexible Models}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Double descent} \srcp{Belkin, Hsu, Ma, and Mandal 2019}: past the point where a
        model can fit the training data exactly, test error can fall again as
        complexity keeps rising. The U-shaped curve is the left half of a
        bigger picture.
  \item \textbf{Why}: among the many exact fits, the fitting algorithm picks the
        smallest-norm one, an implicit ridge penalty; with very many parameters,
        that interpolant can predict well \srcp{Hastie, Montanari, Rosset, and Tibshirani 2022}.
  \item \textbf{For tables of social data} (rows of people, columns of
        variables), tree ensembles still beat deep networks on most benchmarks
        \srcp{Grinsztajn, Oyallon, and Varoquaux 2022}. Deep learning earns its
        keep on text, images, and audio.
  \item The bias--variance logic still governs choices; ``complexity'' is just not
        the number of parameters.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Social Science]{Prediction in Social Science}

\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: the Fragile Families Challenge}
{\footnotesize\color{gray}\srct{Salganik et al.\ (2020)}}\vskip1pt
\textbf{The question.} How predictable are life outcomes, given rich data from
birth to age 9?
\textbf{The design.} A mass collaboration: 4,242 families, 12,942 variables;
outcomes at age 15 released for half the families; 160 teams submitted
predictions, scored on a held-out set never seen by anyone.
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lc|lc@{}}
\toprule
outcome & best $R^2_{\text{holdout}}$ & outcome & best $R^2_{\text{holdout}}$ \\
\midrule
material hardship & 0.23 & eviction & 0.05 \\
child's GPA & 0.19 & caregiver job training & 0.05 \\
child's grit & 0.06 & caregiver layoff & 0.03 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item The best of 160 teams was only slightly better than a four-variable
        linear benchmark chosen by a domain expert (mother's race, marital
        status, and education at birth; the outcome measured at age 9).
  \item Errors depended on the \emph{family}, not on the method: the same families
        were mispredicted by everyone.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Fragile Families Challenge: What It Means}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{A ceiling, not a failure}: with these data, most variation in life
        outcomes is unpredictable from childhood measures. That is a substantive
        finding about stratification, measured honestly.
  \item \textbf{The holdout did the work}: 160 teams tuning against a public
        leaderboard would have overfit it; a set opened once, at the end, could not be.
  \item \textbf{GSS echo}: boosting reached $R^2 = 0.38$ for log earnings, OLS 0.34.
        Flexible methods add little when the signal is mostly additive and the
        noise is large.
  \item \textbf{Prediction as a benchmark for theory}: fit a flexible model on the
        same covariates as your theoretical model; a large gap in out-of-sample
        fit says the functional form is wrong \srcp{Verhagen 2024}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Machine Predictions and Bail \srcp{Kleinberg et al.\ 2018}}
\textbf{The question.} Judges decide pretrial release by predicting crime if
released. Could an algorithm predict better?
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} New York City arrests 2008--13; 554,689 cases in the working
sample. Judges release 73.6\%; 11.3\% of the released commit a crime before trial.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The results} (held-out test set):
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Same release rate as the judges: crime falls 24.7\%.
  \item Same crime rate: detention falls from 26.4\% to 15.3\% (a 41.9\% cut).
  \item Both can be achieved while reducing racial disparities in detention.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The catch: selective labels.} Crime is observed only for defendants the
judge released. The algorithm's prediction for the detained cannot be checked
directly.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Kleinberg et al.: Handling Selective Labels}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Cases are assigned to judges as good as randomly within courthouse-day
        cells (the judge design of Week 7).
  \item \textbf{Contraction}: start from the defendants released by the most
        lenient judges, whose outcomes are mostly observed. Detain more of them
        in order of predicted risk, and compare with what stricter judges
        achieved by detaining more.
  \item Stricter (second-quintile) judges cut crime 9.9\% relative to the most
        lenient; the algorithm matches that cut while jailing far fewer people.
  \item The headline imputations still assume selection on observables for the
        released; contraction is the check that does not.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\alert{A prediction problem with a causal core}: the policy question is what
happens if the rule changes, and that needs a design, not only a better $\hat f$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Biased Algorithm, and Why}
{\footnotesize\color{gray}\srct{Obermeyer, Powers, Vogeli, and Mullainathan (2019)}}\vskip1pt
A commercial algorithm ranks patients for a care-management program
(6,079 Black and 43,539 White patients). Above the 97th percentile of the score,
patients are enrolled automatically.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item At the same score, Black patients are sicker: at the 97th percentile they
        have 26.3\% more chronic conditions (4.8 vs.\ 3.8).
  \item \textbf{Cause: the label.} The algorithm predicts \emph{cost}. At the same
        health, Black patients generate \$1,801 less cost a year, through
        unequal access to care. The prediction is accurate; the target is wrong.
  \item Equalizing health at the threshold would raise the Black share of
        auto-enrolled patients from 17.7\% to 46.5\%.
  \item Retraining on a label combining health and cost cut the excess
        chronic conditions by 84\%.
\end{itemize}
{\footnotesize The sociological lesson: what is measured, and for whom, is built into the
outcome variable before any model is fit.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fairness Criteria Conflict}
A risk score $S$, groups $a$, outcome $Y$. Three reasonable requirements:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Calibration} within groups: $\Pr(Y = 1\mid S = s, a) = s$.
  \item \textbf{Balance for the negative class}: equal mean $S$ among those with $Y = 0$.
  \item \textbf{Balance for the positive class}: equal mean $S$ among those with $Y = 1$.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\srct{Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan (2017)}: all three hold only with
perfect prediction or equal base rates. For a yes/no rule
\srcp{Chouldechova 2017},
\begin{align*}
  \text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1 - \text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot(1 - \text{FNR}),
\end{align*}
with $p$ the base rate: equal PPV across groups with different $p$ forces
different error rates. COMPAS: recidivism 51\% among Black and 39\% among White
defendants, so the two sides of that debate were each right about a different
criterion.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{What ML Buys, and What It Does Not\core}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Buys}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item Prediction with many predictors, including $p > n$.
  \item Automatic nonlinearity and interactions, no functional form to guess.
  \item Honest out-of-sample performance estimates.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{Does not buy}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item \alert{Causal interpretation.} A variable importance score is not an
        effect.
  \item Valid standard errors for the coefficients it selects.
  \item Extrapolation: trees do return a prediction for out-of-range inputs, but
        only the boundary leaf's constant, so they cannot extend a trend.
  \item Freedom from the identification problems of Weeks 5--11.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip5pt
\begin{block}{The genuine sociological uses}
\emph{Prediction as the goal} (screening, triage, forecasting);
\emph{measurement} (constructing variables from text or images);
\emph{describing heterogeneity}; and \alert{estimating nuisance functions inside a
causal estimator}, which is next week.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking Ahead}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Every method today trades bias for variance, tuned by cross-validation.
  \item That trade is exactly why we cannot drop these estimates into a regression
        and read off a coefficient: they are \emph{deliberately biased} and
        converge slowly.
  \item \textbf{Next week}: what has to change before an estimate this biased can
        be used as an ingredient in a causal estimator, and what it buys us when
        it can.
\end{itemize}
