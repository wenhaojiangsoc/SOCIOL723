\section*{Part 1: Yes or No}
Circle \textsc{yes} or \textsc{no}. Three points each.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=6pt]
\item Suppose the true model is $\log(\text{earnings}_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,
      \text{schooling}_i + \epsilon_i$, where $\epsilon_i$ is the true, unobserved error
      (everything else that affects earnings, such as ability). You estimate it by OLS and
      find that the fitted residuals $\hat e_i$ are uncorrelated with schooling. This is
      evidence that $\E[\epsilon_i \mid \text{schooling}_i] = 0$.
      \slideref{1 Tue}{15--16} \yn
\begin{answer}
\textsc{No}. The normal equations force $\sum_i \text{schooling}_i\,\hat e_i = 0$ (and
$\sum_i \hat e_i = 0$) in every sample, whatever the true relation between schooling and
$\epsilon_i$. The zero correlation is algebra, not evidence. If ability raises earnings and
goes with schooling, $\epsilon_i$ is correlated with schooling, yet the residuals $\hat e_i$
are still uncorrelated with it: OLS has absorbed that correlation into a biased $\hat\beta_1$.
The assumption is about the unobserved $\epsilon_i$, and the data cannot check it.
\end{answer}

\item In the same regression, the spread of log earnings is wider among the more schooled,
      so the errors are heteroskedastic. This biases the OLS schooling coefficient.
      \slideref{2}{19} \yn
\begin{answer}
\textsc{No}. Unbiasedness needs only $\E[\epsilon_i \mid X_i] = 0$; it says nothing about
the variance of $\epsilon_i$. Heteroskedasticity makes the classical standard-error formula
wrong, not the coefficient. Keep $\hat\beta$ and report a robust (sandwich) standard error.
\end{answer}

\item You fit a logit of voting on age, then fit it again with education added. The
      log-likelihood at the MLE of the second model can never be lower than that of the
      first, so the second model will always have the lower (better) AIC.
      \slideref{3}{55, 69} \yn
\begin{answer}
\textsc{No}. The first half is true: the first model is the second with the education
coefficient fixed at zero, so the larger model's maximum is at least as high and
$LR = 2(\ell_{\text{big}} - \ell_{\text{small}}) \ge 0$. But $\text{AIC} = -2\ell + 2k$:
adding education lowers $-2\ell$ by $LR$ and raises the penalty by 2. AIC falls only if
$LR > 2$. Example: $\ell = -612.4$ with $k = 2$ and $\ell = -611.9$ with $k = 3$ give
AIC $1228.8$ and $1229.8$; the better-fitting model has the higher AIC.
\end{answer}

\item Places in a job-training program are assigned by lottery, and every winner enrolls.
      The difference in mean earnings between winners and losers two years later estimates
      the ATE. \slideref{5}{13} \yn
\begin{answer}
\textsc{Yes}. This is classic Random Control Trial (RCT) with completely randomized treatment. The lottery makes $(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)) \indep D_i$, so the baseline selection
term $\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0]$ is zero, ATT $=$ ATU $=$ ATE, and the
difference in means is unbiased for all three.
\end{answer}

\item A scholarship awarded by lottery is used as an instrument for college attendance in
      a regression of earnings at age 30. Because the lottery is random, the scholarship is
      a valid instrument. \slideref{7}{6--7} \yn
\begin{answer}
\textsc{No}. The lottery buys independence: winning is unrelated to ability or family
background. It does not buy exclusion. The money itself can change earnings through
channels other than attendance: winners graduate with less debt, can take unpaid
internships or lower-paid first jobs, and may enter a different field. The scholarship
affects $Y$ other than through $D$, so the IV estimate mixes the effect of attending with
the effect of the cash. Randomization makes an instrument independent, not excludable.
\end{answer}
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Part 2: Single Choice}
Circle one letter. Three points each.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=8pt]
\item A researcher regresses children's test scores on neighborhood poverty and omits
      parental income. Parental income raises test scores, and it is lower in poorer
      neighborhoods. The short regression's poverty coefficient is \slideref{1 Tue}{35}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item biased downward: poverty looks more harmful than it is.
      \item biased upward: poverty looks less harmful than it is.
      \item unbiased, because parental income only affects the outcome.
      \item impossible to sign without the data.
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). Bias $= \beta_2\,\delta_1$ with $\beta_2 > 0$ (income raises scores) and $\delta_1 < 0$
(income is lower where poverty is higher): the bias is negative.
\end{answer}

\item Students sit in 40 schools, and the treatment was assigned by school. You run a
      bootstrap with $B = 1{,}000$ draws, each draw resampling individual students with
      equal probability, and get a standard error of 0.8. The right bootstrap
      \slideref{2}{47}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item resamples whole schools with replacement, each bringing all its students.
      \item resamples students, but with $B = 5{,}000$ instead of $1{,}000$.
      \item resamples students within each school.
      \item is not needed: resampling students is already heteroskedasticity-robust.
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). Resample the units the assignment lottery ran over. Resampling students treats them
as independent and copies the too-small standard error of the naive formula; more draws
only estimate that wrong number more precisely.
\end{answer}

\item Five independent Poisson counts: $2, 5, 1, 4, 3$. The MLE $\hat\theta$ is
      \slideref{3}{29}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item $3$
      \item $2.5$
      \item $15$
      \item $0.6$
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i (y_i\log\theta - \theta - \log y_i!)$, so the score is
$\sum_i y_i/\theta - n = 0$, giving $\hat\theta = \bar y = 15/5 = 3$.
\end{answer}

\item In a logit of voting ($0/1$), the coefficient on holding a college degree is $0.693$,
      so $e^{0.693} = 2$. Which sentence is correct? \slideref{4}{14}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item Graduates are twice as likely to vote as non-graduates.
      \item A degree raises the probability of voting by $0.693$.
      \item A degree doubles the odds of voting, other covariates held fixed.
      \item A degree raises the probability of voting by 50 percentage points.
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(C). $e^{\beta}$ multiplies the odds. It is not a ratio of probabilities, and the change in
probability depends on where a person starts on the S-curve.
\end{answer}

\item You regress log earnings on schooling and age, and the schooling coefficient is
      $0.10$. Then you (i) regress log earnings on age and save the residuals,
      (ii) regress schooling on age and save the residuals, and (iii) regress the
      residuals from (i) on the residuals from (ii). The slope in (iii) is
      \slideref{1 Tue}{26, 29}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item exactly $0.10$.
      \item close to $0.10$, but not equal, because residuals are estimated.
      \item the slope of log earnings on schooling without age.
      \item the coefficient on age.
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). This is the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem: the coefficient on schooling in the
regression with age equals the slope from regressing the part of earnings not explained by
age on the part of schooling not explained by age. It holds exactly, in every sample.
\end{answer}

\needspace{12\baselineskip}
\item Four units: \slideref{5}{9--10}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.05}
\begin{tabular}{ccccc}
\toprule
Unit & $D_i$ & $Y_i(0)$ & $Y_i(1)$ \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & 3 & 9 \\
2 & 1 & 5 & 9 \\
3 & 0 & 4 & 6 \\
4 & 0 & 2 & 2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item ATE $= 3$, ATT $= 5$, naive comparison $= 6$
      \item ATE $= 5$, ATT $= 3$, naive comparison $= 6$
      \item ATE $= 3$, ATT $= 5$, naive comparison $= 5$
      \item ATE $= 4$, ATT $= 5$, naive comparison $= 6$
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). $\tau_i = 6, 4, 2, 0$. ATE $= 12/4 = 3$; ATT $= (6+4)/2 = 5$. Observed $Y_i = 9, 9, 4,
2$, so naive $= 9 - 3 = 6$ $=$ ATT $+$ selection bias $= 5 + (4 - 3)$.
\end{answer}

\needspace{18\baselineskip}
\item In the DAG below, $D$ is job training, $Y$ is earnings two years later, $X$ is
      earnings before training, $M$ is hours worked after training, and $S$ is whether the
      person is still in the survey at follow-up. To estimate the \emph{total} effect of $D$
      on $Y$, condition on \slideref{5}{32, 41, 46}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag]
  \node (D) at (0,0)      [label={[dagl]left:training}] {$D$};
  \node (Y) at (4.4,0)    [label={[dagl]above right:earnings}] {$Y$};
  \node (X) at (2.2,1.9)  [label={[dagl]above:prior earnings}] {$X$};
  \node (M) at (2.2,-1.6) [label={[dagl]left:hours}] {$M$};
  \node (S) at (7.0,0)    [label={[dagl]right:in survey}] {$S$};
  \draw[arr] (X) -- (D);
  \draw[arr] (X) -- (Y);
  \draw[arr] (D) -- (Y);
  \draw[arr] (D) -- (M);
  \draw[arr] (M) -- (Y);
  \draw[arr] (Y) -- (S);
  \draw[arr] (D) .. controls (1.0,-3.6) and (6.0,-3.6) .. (S);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item $X$ only.
      \item $X$ and $M$.
      \item $X$ and $S$ (that is, analyze only people still in the survey).
      \item $X$, $M$, and $S$.
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). $X$ is a confounder (the back door $D \leftarrow X \to Y$). $M$ is a mediator:
conditioning removes part of the effect. $S$ is a collider ($D \to S \leftarrow Y$):
conditioning, including by keeping only respondents still in the survey, opens a
spurious path.
\end{answer}

\item In a cell where the propensity score is $e(x) = 0.8$, weighting for the ATT gives each
      treated unit the weight $1$ (the treated are already the population we want).
      Each \emph{untreated} unit gets the weight \slideref{6}{41}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item $4$
      \item $1.25$
      \item $5$
      \item $0.25$
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). ATT weights: treated $1$, untreated $e(x)/(1-e(x)) = 0.8/0.2 = 4$, the odds of
treatment. The untreated are reweighted to look like the treated, of whom there are four
per untreated unit in this cell.
\end{answer}

\needspace{14\baselineskip}
\item Two cells of a covariate, with ignorability given the cell: \slideref{6}{8}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.05}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
cell & treated ($n$) & untreated ($n$) & treated mean $Y$ & untreated mean $Y$ \\
\midrule
1 & 10 & 30 & 8 & 5 \\
2 & 30 & 30 & 12 & 10 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
      The ATE from exact matching is
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item $2.4$
      \item $2.25$
      \item $2.5$
      \item $3.5$
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). Within-cell differences $8 - 5 = 3$ and $12 - 10 = 2$. The ATE weights cells by their
share of \emph{everyone}: $(40 \times 3 + 60 \times 2)/100 = 2.4$. The ATT weights by the
treated, $(10 \times 3 + 30 \times 2)/40 = 2.25$; the unweighted average of the two cells
is $2.5$; the naive comparison is $11 - 7.5 = 3.5$, inflated because the treated sit mostly
in cell 2.
\end{answer}

\item A regression of wages on schooling uses one instrument whose first-stage robust $F$
      is $2.5$ in a sample of $3{,}000$ men. Compared with OLS, the 2SLS estimate
      \slideref{7}{21--22, 65}
      \begin{enumerate}[label=(\Alph*),itemsep=0pt]
      \item is pulled toward the OLS estimate, and its usual 95\% interval covers the truth
            less than 95\% of the time.
      \item is unbiased; the only cost of a weak instrument is a wider interval.
      \item is pulled toward zero, as under classical measurement error.
      \item is fine, because the sample is large and the reduced form is what matters.
      \end{enumerate}
\begin{answer}
(A). With a weak instrument the first stage fits sampling noise that is correlated with the
structural error, so 2SLS is centered between the truth and OLS, and its sampling
distribution is far from normal: the usual interval is too narrow. Report the
Anderson--Rubin interval, which keeps its size at any $F$, together with the first stage
and the reduced form.
\end{answer}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Question 1: Schooling, Parental Education, and Earnings (20 points)}
The GSS extract has $3{,}509$ full-time workers aged 25--64 (GSS 2010--22). The outcome is
log annual earnings. Three OLS regressions:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & (1) log earnings & (2) log earnings & (3) parents' education \\
\midrule
years of schooling & $0.1187$ & $0.1099$ & $0.5703$ \\
parents' education (years) & & $0.0154$ & \\
intercept & $8.2307$ & $8.1546$ & $4.9405$ \\
$R^2$ & $0.129$ & $0.132$ & \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Standard errors for the schooling coefficient in model (2), three ways:
classical $0.0058$; HC3 $0.0064$; clustered on the GSS primary sampling unit ($839$ clusters,
median size four) $0.0065$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=6pt]
\item Interpret the schooling coefficient in model (1) in one sentence a reader of a
      sociology journal would understand. \slideref{1 Tue}{23}
\begin{answer}
Among these workers, each additional year of schooling is associated with about $0.119$ log
points, roughly $12\%$, higher annual earnings. It is a description (the best linear
approximation to the CEF), not yet a causal claim.
\end{answer}

\item Use the omitted-variable-bias formula to recover the coefficient in model (1) from
      models (2) and (3). Show the arithmetic, and explain in words why adding parents'
      education lowers the schooling coefficient. \slideref{1 Tue}{32--35}
\begin{answer}
Short $=$ long $+$ (effect of omitted) $\times$ (regression of omitted on included):
$0.1099 + 0.0154 \times 0.5703 = 0.1099 + 0.0088 = 0.1187$. Parents' education raises
earnings ($0.0154 > 0$) and moves with own schooling ($0.5703 > 0$), so model (1) credits
schooling with part of the family-background advantage; the bias is positive.
\end{answer}

\item Is $0.1099$ the causal effect of a year of schooling? Name one variable still omitted, and explain the bias it creates.
      \slideref{1 Tue}{39, 67--68}
\begin{answer}
No, not without arguing that nothing else confounds schooling and earnings. Example:
ability. It raises earnings and goes with more schooling, so by the OVB formula (positive
$\times$ positive) the coefficient is still biased upward.
\end{answer}

\item Using the HC3 standard error, compute the $t$-statistic and a 95\% confidence
      interval for the schooling coefficient in model (2). \slideref{2}{17, 30}
\begin{answer}
$t = 0.1099/0.0064 = 17.2$. Interval: $0.1099 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0064 = 0.1099 \pm 0.0125
= [0.097,\ 0.122]$.
\end{answer}

\item The GSS samples areas first and then people within them. Which of the three
      standard errors would you report? \slideref{2}{41}
\begin{answer}
The clustered one: cluster at the level of the sampling lottery, the primary sampling unit.
Here the clusters are tiny (median four people), so little within-cluster correlation can
accumulate and the clustered SE barely moves ($0.0065$ vs $0.0064$). Say so rather than
choosing the smaller number.
\end{answer}

\item A colleague suggests adding occupational prestige to model (2) ``to be safe.'' Do you
      agree? Answer with a reason that refers to what the coefficient would then estimate.
      \slideref{1 Tue}{72}
\begin{answer}
No. Occupation is a consequence of schooling on the path to earnings, a mediator. Holding it
fixed removes the part of the schooling effect that works through better jobs, so the
coefficient would no longer be the total effect. More controls are not more conservative.
\end{answer}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Question 2: A Logit of Ever Having Married (15 points)}
Same $3{,}509$ workers; $73\%$ have ever married. A logit of ever married ($0/1$):
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.0}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
 & $\hat\beta$ & SE & $z$ \\
\midrule
intercept & $-2.23$ & $0.279$ & $-7.98$ \\
years of schooling & $-0.01$ & $0.015$ & $-0.99$ \\
female & $0.04$ & $0.084$ & $0.49$ \\
Black & $-0.96$ & $0.107$ & $-9.04$ \\
age (years) & $0.09$ & $0.004$ & $19.91$ \\
\midrule
log-likelihood & \multicolumn{3}{l}{$-1{,}769.5$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=6pt]
\item Write the model twice: once in log-odds form and once in probability form.
      \slideref{4}{9}
\begin{answer}
$\log\frac{p_i}{1-p_i} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{school}_i + \beta_2\,\text{female}_i +
\beta_3\,\text{Black}_i + \beta_4\,\text{age}_i = X_i'\bm\beta$, and
$p_i = e^{X_i'\bm\beta}/(1+e^{X_i'\bm\beta})$.
\end{answer}

\item Compute, by hand, the predicted probability of having married for a woman with 16
      years of schooling who is not Black, at age 30 and at age 40. \slideref{4}{15--16}
\begin{answer}
Age 30: $X'\hat\beta = -2.23 - 0.01(16) + 0.04 + 0.09(30) = -2.23 - 0.16 + 0.04 + 2.70 =
0.35$; odds $e^{0.35} = 1.42$; $\hat p = 1.42/2.42 = 0.59$. Age 40: add $10 \times 0.09 =
0.90$, so $X'\hat\beta = 1.25$, odds $e^{1.25} = 3.49$, $\hat p = 3.49/4.49 = 0.78$.
\end{answer}

\item For this woman, ten years of age raise the probability by about 19 points. The
      average marginal effect of age is $0.014$ per year, so ten times the AME is only $14$
      points. Explain the difference. \slideref{4}{17--18}
\begin{answer}
The slope of the probability is $\hat p_i(1-\hat p_i)\hat\beta$: steepest at $\hat p = 0.5$.
This woman starts at $0.59$, near the steep part of the S-curve. The AME averages the slope
over all $3{,}509$ people, and with $73\%$ married most of them sit higher, where the curve
is flatter.
\end{answer}

\item Adding eight census-region dummies raises the log-likelihood to $-1{,}756.6$. Compute
      the BIC of both models, $BIC = -2\ell + k\log n$, with $k = 5$ and $k = 13$ parameters
      and $\log n = 8.16$. Under the BIC criterion, should the region dummies be included?
      \slideref{3}{70--71}
\begin{answer}
Without region: $-2(-1769.5) + 5 \times 8.16 = 3539.0 + 40.8 = 3579.8$. With region:
$-2(-1756.6) + 13 \times 8.16 = 3513.2 + 106.1 = 3619.3$. Smaller is better, so BIC says
no: the eight dummies bought $25.8$ units of fit, about $3.2$ each, less than BIC's price of
$8.16$ per parameter.
\end{answer}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Question 3: College Proximity as an Instrument (Card 1995; 20 points)}
Lab 7's data: $3{,}010$ men from the NLS Young Men, first interviewed in 1966. $D$ = years of
schooling; $Y$ = log hourly wage in 1976; $Z = 1$ if the man grew up in a county with a
four-year college ($68\%$ did). All four regressions control for experience, experience
squared, Black, metropolitan residence, and South. HC1 standard errors in parentheses:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
 & OLS of $Y$ on $D$ & first stage, $D$ on $Z$ & reduced form, $Y$ on $Z$ & IV \\
\midrule
estimate & $0.074$ & $0.337$ & $0.045$ & $0.132$ \\
(se) & $(0.004)$ & $(0.081)$ & $(0.016)$ & $(0.049)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Robust first-stage $F = 17.5$. (Unrounded: reduced form $0.0446$, first stage $0.3373$.)

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=6pt]
\item Draw a DAG with $Z$, $D$, $Y$, and unmeasured parental social capital $U$ (the
      parents' networks and connections), which affects both schooling $D$ and the son's
      wage $Y$. Include the two arrows whose presence would violate independence and
      exclusion, and mark them as the ones that must be absent. \slideref{7}{6--7}
\begin{answer}
Solid arrows are the design; the dashed orange arrows must be absent.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag]
  \node (Z) at (0,0)      [label={[dagl]below:college nearby}] {$Z$};
  \node (D) at (3.0,0)    [label={[dagl]below:schooling}] {$D$};
  \node (Y) at (6.0,0)    [label={[dagl]below:log wage}] {$Y$};
  \node (U) at (4.5,2.0)  [unobs,label={[dagl]right:parental social capital}] {$U$};
  \draw[arr] (Z) -- (D);
  \draw[arr] (D) -- (Y);
  \draw[uarr] (U) -- (D);
  \draw[uarr] (U) -- (Y);
  \draw[noarr] (Z) .. controls (1.5,-1.9) and (4.5,-1.9) .. (Y)
      node[elab,pos=0.5,below=2pt] {exclusion: no $Z \to Y$};
  \draw[noarr] (U) -- (Z)
      node[elab,pos=0.6,above left=1pt] {independence: no $U \to Z$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Present: $Z \to D \to Y$; $U \to D$ (well-connected parents steer their sons into more
schooling); $U \to Y$ (the same connections help them find better-paid jobs). These two
arrows are why OLS is biased and an instrument is needed. Must be absent: $Z \to Y$, any
effect of proximity on wages that does not run through schooling (exclusion); and
$U \to Z$, parental social capital shaping where the family lives (independence).
\end{answer}

\item For each of the two conditions, give one concrete reason the marked arrow might in
      fact be present in Card's setting. \slideref{7}{30}
\begin{answer}
\textbf{Independence} ($U \to Z$): families do not land near colleges at random. Parents
with more social capital (professional networks, ties to institutions) are more likely to
live in college towns, and the same connections raise their sons' wages; then $Z$ is
correlated with the error. Card's controls (metropolitan, South) and family background
variables only partly close this. \textbf{Exclusion} ($Z \to Y$): counties
with a college have different labor markets, more professional jobs and higher wages, which
raise a man's wage whether or not he attended. Neither can be tested; both have to be
argued.
\end{answer}

\item Show that the IV estimate is the ratio of two numbers in the table, and interpret it
      in one sentence. \slideref{7}{8--10, 31}
\begin{answer}
$\hat\beta_{IV} = \text{reduced form}/\text{first stage} = 0.0446/0.3373 = 0.132$. Growing up
near a college raises wages by $4.5\%$ and schooling by a third of a year; scaled up, a year
of schooling raises wages by about $13\%$ for the men whose schooling proximity changed.
\end{answer}

\item Omitted parental social capital predicts that OLS overstates the return to schooling,
      yet IV ($0.132$) is almost twice OLS ($0.074$). Give two explanations consistent with
      valid instruments.
      \slideref{7}{16, 28}
\begin{answer}
(i) \textbf{Measurement error} in reported schooling pulls OLS toward zero; IV is not
affected by classical error in $D$. (ii) \textbf{LATE}: IV estimates the return for compliers,
men whose schooling was moved by having a college nearby, typically from poorer families
facing cost barriers; their return can be higher than the average return OLS summarizes.
(A violated exclusion or weak-instrument bias would also push IV up, but those are failures
of the design, not explanations under valid instruments.)
\end{answer}

\item Treat schooling as ``attended college or not.'' Describe compliers, always-takers, and
      never-takers in this setting, and state monotonicity in words. Is the IV estimate the
      right number for a policy that opens new colleges in counties without one?
      \slideref{7}{12--13, 16--17}
\begin{answer}
Compliers attend college only if one is nearby; always-takers attend wherever they grow up;
never-takers do not attend either way. Monotonicity: no one attends \emph{because} there is
no college nearby. For opening new colleges the LATE is close to the right number: the men
such a policy would move are exactly those whose attendance responds to proximity. It is not
the effect for always-takers or for everyone.
\end{answer}
\end{enumerate}

\section*{Bonus: Deriving the Wald Estimate}
Ten points. Binary instrument $Z_i$, binary treatment $D_i$. Write $D_i(z)$ for the
treatment unit $i$ takes when assigned $Z_i = z$, and $Y_i(d)$ for its potential outcomes,
so that $D_i = D_i(Z_i)$ and $Y_i = Y_i(D_i)$. Write $\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$. The four
types are compliers ($D_i(1) = 1, D_i(0) = 0$), always-takers ($1, 1$), never-takers
($0, 0$), and defiers ($D_i(1) = 0, D_i(0) = 1$). Assume
(i) independence: $\big(Y_i(0), Y_i(1), D_i(0), D_i(1)\big) \indep Z_i$;
(ii) exclusion, already built into writing $Y_i(d)$ without $z$;
(iii) relevance: the first stage is not zero.
Do \emph{not} assume away defiers until part (c). \slideref{7}{12--15}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=6pt]
\item The denominator. Show that the first stage is
      $\E[D_i\mid Z_i=1] - \E[D_i\mid Z_i=0] = \Pr(\text{complier}) - \Pr(\text{defier})$.
\item The numerator. Show that the reduced form is
      \begin{align*}
        \E[Y_i\mid Z_i=1] - \E[Y_i\mid Z_i=0]
        = \Pr(\text{complier})\,\E[\tau_i\mid\text{complier}]
        - \Pr(\text{defier})\,\E[\tau_i\mid\text{defier}] .
      \end{align*}
      Say where you use exclusion and where you use independence.
\item Now assume monotonicity, $D_i(1) \ge D_i(0)$ for every $i$ (i.e., no defiers). Show that the Wald ratio,
      numerator over denominator, equals $\E[\tau_i\mid\text{complier}]$, the LATE.
\item Without monotonicity, suppose $20\%$ are compliers with an average effect of $1$ and
      $5\%$ are defiers with an average effect of $3$. Compute the Wald ratio and explain
      why it is not an average effect for anyone.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{answer}
\textbf{(a)} You can show it either by expression below, or by plotting two groups $Z = 1$ and $Z = 0$ with different shares of treated units, as we did in class. Same intuitions in the following questions. \\

When $Z_i = 1$ the unit takes $D_i(1)$, and $D_i(1) = 1$ exactly for compliers
and always-takers:
\begin{align*}
  \E[D_i\mid Z_i=1]
  &= \E[D_i(1)\mid Z_i=1]
  && \text{\footnotesize $D_i = D_i(Z_i)$} \\
  &= \E[D_i(1)]
  && \text{\footnotesize independence} \\
  &= \Pr(\text{complier}) + \Pr(\text{always-taker}) .
  && \text{\footnotesize $D_i(1)$ is a $0/1$ indicator}
\end{align*}
The same lines with $Z_i = 0$ give $\E[D_i\mid Z_i=0] = \E[D_i(0)] = \Pr(\text{always-taker})
+ \Pr(\text{defier})$. Subtracting, the always-takers cancel:
$\Pr(\text{complier}) - \Pr(\text{defier})$.
\par\textbf{(b)} Since $D_i$ is $0$ or $1$, $Y_i = Y_i(D_i) = Y_i(0) + D_i\,\tau_i$; this is
where exclusion enters, because $Y_i(d)$ carries no $z$. Then
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_i\mid Z_i=1]
  &= \E\big[Y_i(0) + D_i(1)\,\tau_i \mid Z_i=1\big]
  && \text{\footnotesize switching equation, $D_i = D_i(1)$} \\
  &= \E\big[Y_i(0) + D_i(1)\,\tau_i\big] ,
  && \text{\footnotesize independence: the bracket does not involve $Z_i$}
\end{align*}
and likewise $\E[Y_i\mid Z_i=0] = \E[Y_i(0) + D_i(0)\,\tau_i]$. Subtracting, $\E[Y_i(0)]$
cancels and the reduced form is $\E[(D_i(1) - D_i(0))\,\tau_i]$. The difference
$D_i(1) - D_i(0)$ is $1$ for compliers, $-1$ for defiers, and $0$ for always- and
never-takers, so by iterated expectations over the four types
\begin{align*}
  \E[(D_i(1) - D_i(0))\,\tau_i]
  = \Pr(\text{complier})\,\E[\tau_i\mid\text{complier}]
  - \Pr(\text{defier})\,\E[\tau_i\mid\text{defier}] .
\end{align*}
\par\textbf{(c)} Monotonicity rules out $D_i(1) = 0, D_i(0) = 1$, so $\Pr(\text{defier}) = 0$.
The numerator becomes $\Pr(\text{complier})\,\E[\tau_i\mid\text{complier}]$ and the
denominator $\Pr(\text{complier})$, which is positive by relevance. Their ratio is
$\E[\tau_i\mid\text{complier}]$: the average effect among the people the instrument moves.
\par\textbf{(d)} Numerator $0.20 \times 1 - 0.05 \times 3 = 0.05$; denominator $0.20 - 0.05 =
0.15$; Wald ratio $0.05/0.15 = 0.33$. Every person the instrument moves has an effect of at
least $1$, yet the ratio is $0.33$: defiers enter with a negative weight, so the ratio is a
difference of two group averages, not an average over any group. With the defiers' effect
at $4$ or more the ratio would be zero or negative.
\end{answer}
