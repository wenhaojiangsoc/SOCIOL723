\section[Sampling Distribution]{The Sampling Distribution of OLS}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Question, in Plain Words\core}
Last week we computed $\hat\beta_1 = 0.119$: one more year of schooling goes
with about $12\%$ higher earnings in our sample of $3{,}509$ people.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The GSS could just as easily have interviewed a \emph{different}
        3{,}509 people. That sample would have produced a different number,
        perhaps $0.112$, perhaps $0.127$.
  \item So $0.119$ is one draw from a lottery. Before treating it as evidence
        about the population, we need to know how much it moves from draw to
        draw.
  \item The \alert{standard error} is exactly that: the give-or-take attached
        to the estimate. Formally, it is (an estimate of) the standard
        deviation of $\hat\beta_1$ across hypothetical repeated samples.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{This week in one sentence}
Everything called ``statistical inference'', standard errors, $t$-statistics,
$p$-values, confidence intervals, is machinery for learning that give-or-take
number from the single sample we actually have.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What a Sampling Distribution Is\core}
$\hat\beta_1$ is a number computed from \emph{your} sample. Had you drawn a
different sample from the same population, you would have got a different number.
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{The thought experiment}
Imagine drawing sample after sample from the population and computing
$\hat\beta_1$ each time. The histogram of those numbers is the \alert{sampling
distribution}. Where it is centred tells you about bias; how wide it is
\emph{is} the standard error.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item You only ever see one draw. Everything in this lecture is about inferring
        the shape of that histogram from a single sample.
  \item In lab we run the experiment by simulation, which is the fastest way to
        make the idea concrete.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Theorems We Lean On All Semester\core}
Both are statements about what happens to a \emph{sample average} as $n$ grows.
\vskip2pt
\begin{block}{Law of large numbers (LLN)}
The average of $n$ independent draws settles down on the population mean:
$\frac1n\sum_i Z_i \pto \E[Z]$. Flip a fair coin 10 times and the share of
heads could be anything; flip it 10{,}000 times and it sits within a whisker
of $0.5$.
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
\begin{block}{Central limit theorem (CLT)}
Not only does the average settle down; the \emph{shape} of its wobble around
the truth becomes a bell curve, $\sqrt n\,(\bar Z - \E[Z]) \dto \mathcal
N\big(0, \Var(Z)\big)$, \alert{whatever the distribution of $Z$ itself},
skewed, lumpy, or binary, so long as its variance is finite.
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
Why we care: $\hat\beta_1$ is built out of sample averages, so the LLN will
give consistency and the CLT approximate normality. We verify both by
simulation in Thursday's lab; watching the bell curve emerge once is worth a
dozen proofs.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Route 1: Exact, Under Normality}
Assume A1--A6, including $\epsilon_i\mid X \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)$. Since
$\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1$ is a \emph{weighted sum of the errors}, it is normal:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_1 \sim \mathcal N\!\left(\beta_1,\ \frac{\sigma^2}{\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)^2}\right),
  \qquad
  t = \frac{\hat\beta_1 - \beta_1}{\SE(\hat\beta_1)} \sim t_{n-k} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The $t$ (rather than the normal) appears because we replaced the unknown
        $\sigma^2$ by the estimate $s^2$.
  \item Exact at any $n$, but it requires normal errors, which is rarely
        credible for earnings, counts, durations, or attitude scales.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Route 2: Approximate, Without Normality\core}
Drop A6 and look again at
\begin{align*}
  \hat\beta_1 - \beta_1 = \frac{\frac1n\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)\epsilon_i}
                               {\frac1n\sum_i (X_i-\bar X)^2} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The \textbf{denominator} is a sample average. By the \alert{law of large
        numbers} it settles down to $\Var(X_i)$ as $n$ grows.
  \item The \textbf{numerator} is also a sample average, of the mean-zero terms
        $(X_i - \bar X)\epsilon_i$. By the \alert{central limit theorem}, sample
        averages of i.i.d.\ mean-zero terms are approximately normal once $n$ is
        large, \emph{whatever} the distribution of $\epsilon_i$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
So $\hat\beta_1$ is approximately normal in large samples with no assumption
about the shape of the error distribution. \alert{This is the modern default}, and
it is the route that generalizes to MLE (Week 3), IV, and everything after.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The General Statement\adv}
For the full coefficient vector, writing sample averages as $\En$:
\begin{align*}
  \bhat - \bm\beta
  = \big(\En[X_iX_i']\big)^{-1}\En[X_i\epsilon_i] .
\end{align*}
The LLN gives $\En[X_iX_i'] \pto \E[X_iX_i'] \equiv \bm{Q}$ and
$\En[X_i\epsilon_i]\pto \zero$, so $\bhat \pto \bm\beta$ (\emph{consistency}).
Multiplying by $\sqrt n$ and applying the CLT to the second factor,
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;\sqrt n(\bhat-\bm\beta) \dto
  \mathcal N\Big(\zero,\; \bm{Q}^{-1}\Om^{\!*}\bm{Q}^{-1}\Big)\;},
  \qquad \Om^{\!*} = \E[\epsilon_i^2 X_iX_i'] .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
Note what is \emph{not} assumed: no normality, no homoskedasticity. Note also the
weaker exogeneity requirement, $\E[X_i\epsilon_i]=0$ suffices for consistency;
$\E[\epsilon_i\mid X_i]=0$ is not needed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading the ``Sandwich''\adv}
\begin{align*}
  \bm{V} = \underbrace{\bm{Q}^{-1}}_{\text{bread}}\;
           \underbrace{\Om^{\!*}}_{\text{meat}}\;
           \underbrace{\bm{Q}^{-1}}_{\text{bread}},
  \qquad
  \bm Q = \E[X_iX_i'],\quad \Om^{\!*} = \E[\epsilon_i^2X_iX_i'] .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The \textbf{bread} depends only on the distribution of the regressors.
  \item The \textbf{meat} is where every assumption about the error structure
        lives. Classical, robust, clustered, and HAC standard errors differ
        \alert{only} in how the meat is estimated.
  \item If $\E[\epsilon_i^2\mid X_i] = \sigma^2$ then
        $\Om^{\!*} = \sigma^2\bm Q$ and $\bm V = \sigma^2\bm Q^{-1}$, the
        classical formula.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{The organizing idea for the whole lecture}
Keep the estimator $\hat\beta$; change how you estimate the meat.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Testing]{Hypothesis Testing}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Single Restrictions\core}
To test $H_0: \beta_j = \beta_j^0$,
\begin{align*}
  t = \frac{\hat\beta_j - \beta_j^0}{\SE(\hat\beta_j)}
  \ \sim\ t_{n-k} \ \text{ (exactly, under A6)}
  \ \text{ or }\ \mathcal N(0,1) \ \text{ (approximately, for large }n\text{)} .
\end{align*}
A $95\%$ confidence interval is $\hat\beta_j \pm 1.96\,\SE(\hat\beta_j)$.
\vskip6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The $t_{n-k}$ and $\mathcal N(0,1)$ critical values differ by less than
        $2\%$ once $n-k > 100$. Reporting $t$ is a convention, not a claim to
        exactness.
  \item \alert{Everything depends on whether $\SE$ is right.} A $t$ of 3.0
        computed from a standard error that is half of what it should be is
        really a $t$ of 1.5.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Joint Restrictions: the $F$-Test \emph{Is} Model Comparison\core}
To test $q$ restrictions at once (``neither \texttt{wordsum} nor
\texttt{pareduc} matters''), fit the restricted (compact) and unrestricted
(augmented) models and compare their errors:
\begin{align*}
  F = \frac{(SSE_C - SSE_A)/q}{SSE_A/(n-k)} \ \sim\ F_{q,\,n-k}
  \qquad\text{under A1--A6}.
\end{align*}
Exactly the model-comparison $F$ from \textit{Social Statistics I}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{An important caveat}
The sums-of-squares form is valid \alert{only under homoskedasticity}. With
robust or clustered standard errors you must use the Wald form, 
\texttt{car::linearHypothesis(..., vcov = )} or
\texttt{lmtest::waldtest(..., vcov = )}. Plain \texttt{anova()} on two
\texttt{lm} objects gives you the \emph{non-robust} test. This is a very common
error in published work.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Wald Statistic\adv}
Write $q$ linear restrictions as $\bm{R}\bm\beta = \bm q$ with $\bm R$ a
$q\times k$ matrix of rank $q$:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Hypothesis & $\bm R$, $\bm q$ \\
\midrule
$\beta_2 = 0$ & $\bm R = (0,1,0,\dots)$, $\bm q = 0$ \\
$\beta_2 = \beta_3$ & $\bm R = (0,1,-1,0\dots)$, $\bm q = 0$ \\
$\beta_2 = \beta_3 = 0$ & two rows, $\bm q = (0,0)'$ \\
All slopes zero & $\bm R = [\zero \ \ \Ix_{k-1}]$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip3pt
\begin{align*}
  W = (\bm R\bhat - \bm q)'
      \big[\bm R\,\hat{V}(\bhat)\,\bm R'\big]^{-1}
      (\bm R\bhat - \bm q)\ \dto\ \chi^2_q ,
  \qquad F = W/q .
\end{align*}
Substituting any $\hat{V}(\bhat)$ you like (robust, clustered) gives
the corresponding robust joint test.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Nonlinear Restrictions: the Delta Method\adv}
For a smooth function $g(\bm\beta)$, a ratio of coefficients, a turning point
$-\beta_1/2\beta_2$, a marginal effect:
\begin{align*}
  \hat{V}\big(g(\bhat)\big)
  = \nabla g(\bhat)'\,\hat{V}(\bhat)\,\nabla g(\bhat) .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Intuition: a smooth function is locally a straight line, and we know the
        variance of a linear function. So linearize, then use what we know.
  \item Implemented as \texttt{car::deltaMethod()} or the
        \texttt{marginaleffects} package.
  \item First-order approximation: it can be poor when $g$ is strongly nonlinear
        near $\bhat$, or when the denominator of a ratio is close to zero. The
        bootstrap is often better there.
  \item We use it constantly in Week 4 for marginal effects and predicted
        probabilities.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{A Note on Multiple Testing}
Everything so far controlled the error rate for \emph{one} planned comparison.
The moment you look at many outcomes, many subgroups, or many specifications, the
guarantee has to hold across the whole family of claims, and it does not.
\vskip3pt
If you run $m$ independent tests at level $\alpha$, the chance of at least one
false rejection is $1-(1-\alpha)^m$: for $m=20$, about $64\%$.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Bonferroni}: test each at $\alpha/m$. Controls the family-wise
        error rate; conservative.
  \item \textbf{Holm}: sequentially rejective; uniformly more powerful than
        Bonferroni with the same guarantee.
  \item \textbf{Benjamini--Hochberg}: controls the \emph{false discovery rate}, 
        the right tool with many outcomes or subgroups when you can tolerate some
        false positives.
  \item \texttt{p.adjust(p, method = "holm" / "BH")}.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
Sociologically relevant cases: many outcomes, many subgroups, many
specifications. Pre-registering a small set of primary hypotheses is a better
solution than any correction.
\end{frame}

\section[Heteroskedasticity]{Heteroskedasticity and Robust Standard Errors}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Heteroskedasticity Is\core}
\textbf{Homoskedasticity} (A5) says $\Var(\epsilon_i\mid X_i) = \sigma^2$: the
spread of the outcome around the line is the same everywhere.
\vskip2pt
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.82]
  \begin{scope}
    \draw[->] (0,0) -- (4.6,0) node[right]{\scriptsize $X$};
    \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,2.6) node[above]{\scriptsize $Y$};
    \draw[dukeblue,thick] (0.2,0.4) -- (4.3,2.2);
    \foreach \x in {0.5,1.0,...,4.2} {
      \foreach \d in {-0.35,-0.12,0.12,0.35} {
        \fill[dukeblue!55] (\x, {0.4+(\x-0.2)*0.44+\d}) circle (0.035);}}
    \node at (2.3,-0.45) {\footnotesize homoskedastic};
  \end{scope}
  \begin{scope}[xshift=6cm]
    \draw[->] (0,0) -- (4.6,0) node[right]{\scriptsize $X$};
    \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,2.6) node[above]{\scriptsize $Y$};
    \draw[accent,thick] (0.2,0.4) -- (4.3,2.2);
    \foreach \x in {0.5,1.0,...,4.2} {
      \foreach \d in {-1,-0.34,0.34,1} {
        \fill[accent!65] (\x, {0.4+(\x-0.2)*0.44+\d*0.11*\x}) circle (0.035);}}
    \node at (2.3,-0.45) {\footnotesize heteroskedastic};
  \end{scope}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
Under exogeneity, both lines are estimated without bias: heteroskedasticity by
itself does not bias $\hat\beta$. Only the \emph{uncertainty} about them differs,
and only the right-hand panel needs a different formula.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heteroskedasticity Is the Norm}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Earnings dispersion rises steeply with education and experience: at 8
        years of schooling almost everyone earns a modest wage; at 20 years the
        range is enormous.
  \item Group-level outcomes built from different numbers of individuals, a
        county mean over 50 people is noisier than one over 50{,}000.
  \item Binary outcomes: $\Var(Y_i\mid X_i) = p(X_i)(1-p(X_i))$ is
        \emph{necessarily} heteroskedastic, so the linear probability model always
        violates A5.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{What it does and does not do}
\textbf{Does not}: bias $\hat\beta$, or break consistency. \\
\textbf{Does}: invalidate the standard errors your software prints by default, so
$t$-statistics, $p$-values and confidence intervals are wrong; and cost
efficiency (OLS is no longer BLUE).
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Scalar Sandwich\core}
Go back to the exact variance, \emph{without} assuming constant variance. Write
$\sigma_i^2 \equiv \Var(\epsilon_i \mid X)$ and $d_i \equiv X_i - \bar X$:
\begin{align*}
  \Var(\hat\beta_1\mid X)
  = \Var\!\left(\frac{\sum_i d_i\epsilon_i}{\sum_i d_i^2}\ \Bigg|\ X\right)
  = \frac{\sum_i d_i^2\,\sigma_i^2}{\big(\sum_i d_i^2\big)^2} .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item If $\sigma_i^2 = \sigma^2$ for all $i$, it factors out and we recover
        $\sigma^2/\sum_i d_i^2$.
  \item If not, the numerator is a \alert{$d_i^2$-weighted average of the
        individual error variances}. Observations far from $\bar X$, the
        high-leverage ones, get the most weight.
  \item \alert{This is the entire idea.} The ``bread'' is $\sum_i d_i^2$, twice;
        the ``meat'' is $\sum_i d_i^2\sigma_i^2$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{White's Estimator, in Scalar Form\core}
We cannot observe $\sigma_i^2$. But we have $\hat e_i^2$, and
$\E[\hat e_i^2] \approx \sigma_i^2$. Plug it in:
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;
  \hat{V}_{\text{HC0}}(\hat\beta_1)
  = \frac{\sum_i d_i^2\,\hat e_i^2}{\big(\sum_i d_i^2\big)^2} \;}
  \qquad
  \text{versus}\qquad
  \hat{V}_{\text{classical}}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{s^2}{\sum_i d_i^2} .
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item That is the whole of ``heteroskedasticity-robust,'' ``Huber--White,''
        ``Eicker--Huber--White,'' and the \texttt{robust} option in \texttt{Stata}.
  \item Due to \srct{White (1980)}, building on \srct{Eicker (1967)} and
        \srct{Huber (1967)}.
  \item Notice the difference: the classical formula uses \emph{one} pooled
        $s^2$; the robust formula lets every observation carry its own
        $\hat e_i^2$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{``But You Only Have One Residual Per Observation''}
A frequent and reasonable objection: how can we estimate $n$ different variances
from $n$ observations?
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item We never estimate $\sigma^2_i$ consistently for any particular $i$, 
        and we do not need to.
  \item The object we need is the \emph{sum} $\sum_i d_i^2\sigma_i^2$: a single
        number. Each $\hat e_i^2$ is a noisy but roughly unbiased estimate of
        $\sigma_i^2$, and adding up $n$ of them averages the noise away.
  \item Same logic that lets you estimate $\Var(\bar Y)$ from one sample: you
        never learn any individual's deviation, only the average square.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
The general version\adv:
$\hat\Om^{\!*} = \En\big[\hat e_i^2\, X_iX_i'\big] \pto \E[\epsilon_i^2 X_iX_i']$,
so $\hat{V}_{\text{HC0}}(\bhat) =
(\X'\X)^{-1}\big(\sum_i \hat e_i^2 X_iX_i'\big)(\X'\X)^{-1}$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Finite-Sample Corrections: HC0--HC3\core}
$\hat e_i^2$ is biased downward: \emph{under homoskedasticity}
$\E[\hat e_i^2] = \sigma^2(1-h_{ii})$, where $h_{ii} = X_i'(\X'\X)^{-1}X_i$
is observation $i$'s \alert{leverage} (large when its covariates are unusual).
So HC0 \emph{understates} standard errors in small samples. The fixes
rescale the squared residuals (motivated by the homoskedastic benchmark, used
regardless):
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
& Meat uses & Comment \\
\midrule
HC0 & $\hat e_i^2$ & White's original; anticonservative when $n$ is small \\
HC1 & $\frac{n}{n-k}\hat e_i^2$ & simple d.f.\ correction; \texttt{Stata}'s
      \texttt{robust} default \\
HC2 & $\dfrac{\hat e_i^2}{1-h_{ii}}$ & unbiased under homoskedasticity \\
HC3 & $\dfrac{\hat e_i^2}{(1-h_{ii})^2}$ & approximates the jackknife;
      \alert{recommended default} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
\srct{MacKinnon and White (1985)} and \srct{Long and Ervin (2000)}: with
$n < 250$ or high leverage, use \textbf{HC3}. All four coincide as $n\to\infty$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How Much Does It Matter?}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Robust standard errors can be \emph{larger or smaller} than classical
        ones. There is no general direction.
  \item The difference is driven by whether the error variance happens to be
        large where $X$ is extreme. In our GSS earnings regression it is modest:
        $\SE(\hat\beta_{\texttt{educ}})$ goes from $0.00510$ (classical) to
        $0.00547$ (HC0) to $0.00549$ (HC3), about $7\%$.
  \item With a skewed or sparse regressor, a rare binary treatment, a
        long-tailed count; the difference can be a factor of two.
  \item \alert{Clustering, by contrast, routinely changes standard errors by
        factors of three to ten.} In most sociological data that is the bigger
        issue.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Should You \emph{Test} for Heteroskedasticity?}
Classical tests exist: Breusch--Pagan, White.
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{Recommendation: no}
Do not pre-test and then choose. Just report robust standard errors.
\end{alertblock}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item A pre-test makes your final inference conditional on the pre-test outcome,
        which distorts the very sampling distribution you are claiming.
  \item The cost of robust standard errors when the errors happen to be
        homoskedastic is small, a modest efficiency loss in the \emph{variance
        estimate}.
  \item The cost of classical standard errors when they are wrong is invalid
        inference.
  \item Exception: the tests are useful \emph{diagnostically}. Heteroskedasticity
        is often a symptom of a misspecified functional form, which is worth
        knowing about for its own sake.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Weighted Least Squares: the Road Not Taken}
If we knew $\Var(\epsilon_i\mid X_i)=\sigma^2/w_i$, weighted least squares would
restore efficiency: minimize $\sum_i w_i(Y_i - X_i'b)^2$.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item BLUE under the \emph{correct} weights, Gauss--Markov applied to the
        transformed model.
  \item But we almost never know the weights, and estimating them (``feasible
        GLS'') introduces problems of its own.
  \item Worse: if the CEF is not exactly linear, OLS and WLS estimate
        \emph{different} weighted approximations to it. They answer different
        questions, so a discrepancy between them is not a bug to be fixed.
  \item \alert{Modern practice}: keep OLS, fix the standard errors. Use weights
        when they come from the \emph{design} (survey sampling weights, group
        sizes), not from a guess about the error variance.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section[Clustering]{Clustered Data}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Independence Fails\core}
Sociological data are almost never i.i.d.\ draws:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item Students within schools; workers within firms; siblings within families.
  \item Repeated observations on the same individual (panel data).
  \item Multi-stage survey designs, the GSS samples tracts, then households.
  \item Treatment assigned at the group level (a state law), outcomes measured at
        the individual level.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{Error components model}
\begin{align*}
  Y_{ig} = X_{ig}'\bm\beta + \underbrace{\nu_g}_{\text{cluster shock}}
  + \underbrace{\eta_{ig}}_{\text{idiosyncratic}} ,
  \qquad
  \rho_e = \frac{\sigma_\nu^2}{\sigma_\nu^2+\sigma_\eta^2}
\end{align*}
Everyone in cluster $g$ shares the shock $\nu_g$, so their errors are correlated.
$\rho_e$ is the \alert{intra-class correlation}.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the Scalar Formula Breaks\core}
The variance of $\hat\beta_1$ involves \emph{all} the covariances of the errors:
\begin{align*}
  \Var\!\left(\sum_i d_i\epsilon_i\right)
  = \underbrace{\sum_i d_i^2\,\sigma_i^2}_{\text{what HC0 estimates}}
  + \underbrace{\sum_{i}\sum_{j\neq i} d_i d_j\,\Cov(\epsilon_i,\epsilon_j)}
               _{\text{\alert{assumed zero so far}}} .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Independent sampling makes the second term vanish. Clustering does not.
  \item If errors within a cluster are \emph{positively} correlated and $d_i$ has
        the same sign within clusters, every one of those cross terms is positive
, so the true variance is \alert{larger} than the formula we have been
        using, often by a lot.
  \item There are on the order of $n(\bar n - 1)$ such cross terms versus $n$
        terms in the first sum, so even tiny correlations can dominate.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Moulton Factor: How Bad Is It?\core}
With equal cluster sizes $\bar n$ and an error-components structure,
\srct{Moulton (1986)} showed
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\Var_{\text{clustered}}(\hat\beta_1)}{\Var_{\text{OLS}}(\hat\beta_1)}
  = 1 + (\bar n - 1)\,\rho_x\,\rho_e ,
\end{align*}
where $\rho_x$ is the intra-class correlation of the regressor.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item If the regressor is \emph{constant within clusters}, treatment
        assigned at the cluster level, then $\rho_x = 1$ and the inflation is
        $1+(\bar n -1)\rho_e$. This is the worst case.
  \item 50 states, 1{,}000 individuals each, $\rho_e = 0.01$ (small!):
        $\sqrt{1 + 999(0.01)} \approx 3.3$. \alert{Standard errors are more than
        three times too small}, so a reported $t$ of 6 is really a $t$ of 1.8.
  \item Moral: the effective sample size is closer to the number of
        \emph{clusters} than the number of observations.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cluster-Robust Variance (Liang--Zeger)\adv}
Let $g = 1,\dots,G$ index clusters, with $\X_g$ and $\ehat_g$ the stacked
regressors and residuals in cluster $g$:
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;
  \hat{V}_{\text{CR}}(\bhat)
  = c\,(\X'\X)^{-1}
    \Big(\sum_{g=1}^{G} \X_g'\ehat_g\ehat_g'\X_g\Big)
    (\X'\X)^{-1} \;}
\end{align*}
with the usual small-sample scaling $c = \frac{G}{G-1}\cdot\frac{n-1}{n-k}$
(CR1; \texttt{Stata}'s default).
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Same sandwich; the meat now sums outer products of \emph{cluster} score
        vectors instead of individual ones, which is exactly how the cross
        terms get included.
  \item Allows \alert{arbitrary} correlation within cluster (including serial
        correlation over time) and any heteroskedasticity, while assuming
        independence \emph{across} clusters.
  \item Consistency is in $G\to\infty$, not $n\to\infty$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{At What Level Should You Cluster?\core}
\srct{Abadie et al.\ (2023)}: clustering is a statement about the \emph{design},
not a knob to tune.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Sampling-based}: cluster at the level of the sampling units, when
        the clusters in your data are a small share of the clusters in the
        population.
  \item \textbf{Design-based}: cluster at the level at which \emph{treatment} is
        assigned. If a policy varies by state, cluster on state, even with
        individual-level data.
  \item Cluster at the \emph{coarsest} level for which you have a plausible
        argument; never finer than the treatment assignment.
  \item ``Cluster on everything'' is not free: with few clusters the estimator is
        biased downward and the test over-rejects.
  \item \alert{Never} choose the level by whichever gives the smallest $p$-value.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Few-Clusters Problem}
With small $G$, $\hat{V}_{\text{CR}}$ is biased downward and the reference
distribution is too thin. Worry below $G \approx 40$--$50$; be alarmed below
$20$. The symptom is rejection rates of $10$--$15\%$ for a nominal $5\%$ test.
\vskip2pt
\begin{block}{Remedies}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{CR2 with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom}
        \srcp{Bell and McCaffrey 2002; Imbens and Kolesar 2016}: a leverage
        correction (the cluster analogue of HC2) plus data-dependent d.f.
        \texttt{clubSandwich::coef\_test()}.
  \item \textbf{Wild cluster bootstrap-$t$} \srcp{Cameron et al.\ 2008}: impose
        $H_0$, resample cluster-level $\pm1$ weights, and build the reference
        distribution empirically. \texttt{fwildclusterboot}.
  \item \textbf{Aggregate}: collapse to $G$ cluster-level observations and analyze
        those. Crude but honest.
  \item \textbf{Randomization inference} when treatment is assigned to few units.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Serial Correlation: Bertrand, Duflo, and Mullainathan (2004)}
A famous demonstration of what happens when you ignore within-unit correlation.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item They ran \emph{placebo} difference-in-differences: take CPS data, invent
        a state-level ``law'' beginning in a random year, and estimate its effect
        on women's wages. The true effect is zero by construction.
  \item With conventional standard errors the fake law came out ``significant'' at
        $5\%$ in \alert{$45\%$} of the placebo runs.
  \item Cause: state-level outcomes are highly serially correlated over time, and
        the standard errors treated $50 \times 21$ observations as independent.
  \item Clustering by state fixed it, with 50 clusters, just barely enough.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
We return to this in Week 10. Design and inference are not separable questions.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Other Dependence Structures\adv}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{Two-way / multi-way clustering} \srcp{Cameron et al.\ 2011}: firms
        \emph{and} years, individuals \emph{and} places. Add the two
        cluster-robust matrices and subtract their intersection.
  \item \textbf{HAC (Newey--West)}: time-series dependence of unknown form,
        handled with a bandwidth-weighted sum of autocovariances. For one long
        series rather than many short ones.
  \item \textbf{Spatial HAC (Conley)}: correlation decaying in geographic
        distance.
  \item \textbf{Network dependence}: correlation along a graph; an active area
        with fewer settled answers.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
All are the same sandwich with a different meat. Once you can read
$\hat{V}$, you can read any of these off the formula.
\end{frame}

%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\section[Bootstrap]{The Bootstrap}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Idea}
Everything so far derived a \emph{formula} for the sampling distribution. The
bootstrap instead \alert{simulates} it.
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{Plug-in principle \srcp{Efron 1979}}
We want the sampling distribution of a statistic when samples are drawn from the
population $F$. We do not know $F$, but the sample \emph{is} our best estimate
of $F$. So draw new samples from the sample.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item In our notation: replace $\E$ by $\En$, and resample.
  \item Especially valuable for statistics with no convenient closed-form
        variance: quantiles, ratios, decompositions, two-step estimators,
        indirect effects.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Nonparametric (Pairs) Bootstrap\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item For $b=1,\dots,B$: draw $n$ observations $(Y_i, X_i)$ \emph{with
        replacement} from the sample.
  \item Re-estimate on the resample to get $\hat\theta^{(b)}$.
  \item Use the empirical distribution of $\{\hat\theta^{(b)}\}_{b=1}^B$.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $\SE_{\text{boot}} = $ the standard deviation of the $\hat\theta^{(b)}$.
  \item \textbf{Percentile CI}: the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles of
        $\{\hat\theta^{(b)}\}$.
  \item Because it resamples $(Y_i,X_i)$ jointly, the pairs bootstrap is
        automatically heteroskedasticity-robust, it makes no assumption about
        $\sigma_i^2$ at all.
  \item $B = 1{,}000$ for standard errors; $B \geq 5{,}000$ for percentile
        intervals or $p$-values.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Variants}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{lp{5.0cm}p{4.3cm}}
\toprule
Variant & What is resampled & When to use \\
\midrule
Pairs & $(Y_i,X_i)$ jointly & default; robust to heteroskedasticity \\
Residual & $\hat e_i$, then $Y_i^\ast = X_i'\bhat + \hat e_i^\ast$
        & requires homoskedasticity; fixed design \\
Wild & $Y_i^\ast = X_i'\bhat + \hat e_i v_i$, $v_i \in \{-1,1\}$
        & heteroskedastic, small $n$ \\
Block / cluster & whole clusters & clustered data \\
Wild cluster & cluster-level $v_g$, $H_0$ imposed & \alert{few clusters} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
Rademacher weights: $v_i = +1$ or $-1$ with probability $1/2$ each, so
$\E[v_i]=0$ and $\E[v_i^2]=1$, the resampled errors keep the right variance
observation by observation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Confidence Intervals: Not All Equal}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Normal-approximation}: $\hat\theta \pm 1.96\,\SE_{\text{boot}}$.
        Simple; assumes symmetry.
  \item \textbf{Percentile}: quantiles of $\{\hat\theta^{(b)}\}$. Handles skew and
        respects transformations.
  \item \textbf{BC$_a$}: bias-corrected and accelerated; second-order accurate and
        the usual recommendation for a percentile-type interval.
  \item \alert{\textbf{Bootstrap-$t$ (studentized)}}: bootstrap the \emph{$t$
        statistic} $(\hat\theta^{(b)}-\hat\theta)/\SE^{(b)}$ and use its
        quantiles. \textbf{Asymptotic refinement}: error $O(n^{-1})$ instead of
        $O(n^{-1/2})$. Requires a standard error at each replication.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
The wild cluster bootstrap-$t$ is exactly this idea applied to the few-clusters
problem, which is why it works so much better than a naive cluster bootstrap.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When the Bootstrap Fails}
The bootstrap is not magic; it needs the statistic to be a \emph{smooth}
functional of the data.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Non-smooth estimators}: the sample maximum or minimum;
        nearest-neighbour matching estimators \srcp{Abadie and Imbens 2008}.
  \item \textbf{Parameters on a boundary}, a variance component at zero.
  \item \textbf{Heavy tails} with infinite variance.
  \item \textbf{Dependence the resampling scheme does not preserve.} If you
        resample individuals when the data are clustered, the bootstrap reproduces
        the \emph{wrong}, too-small standard error just as faithfully as the
        analytic formula does.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
\begin{alertblock}{Bootstrap the design}
Resample the units that were independently sampled. That single rule prevents
most bootstrap mistakes.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Limits]{What Robust Standard Errors Cannot Fix}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Freedman's Critique}
\srct{Freedman (2006)} makes an uncomfortable point.
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{The argument}
The sandwich gives a consistent estimate of the variance of $\hat\beta$
\emph{around the population regression coefficient} $\beta$. If the model is
misspecified, if the CEF is not $X_i'\beta$, then $\beta$ is merely the
best linear approximation, and a correct standard error for a quantity you do not
care about is small comfort.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item In his words: ``if the model is seriously in error, the sandwich may help
        on the variance side, but the parameters being estimated\dots\ are likely
        to be meaningless.''
  \item If the model is right, you did not need the sandwich (much).
  \item If the model is wrong, the sandwich fixes the second-order problem
        (variance) and leaves the first-order problem (bias) untouched.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Scoreboard\core}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
Problem & Robust SEs? & What actually helps \\
\midrule
Heteroskedasticity & \textbf{yes} & nothing more needed \\
Within-cluster correlation & \textbf{yes} (cluster-robust) & design-based clustering \\
Serial correlation in panels & \textbf{yes} & cluster on unit \\
Non-normal errors & \textbf{yes} (asymptotically) & larger $n$ \\
\midrule
Omitted variable bias & \alert{no} & identification strategy (Wks 5--11) \\
Simultaneity, reverse causation & \alert{no} & IV, RDD, DID \\
Measurement error in $X$ & \alert{no} & IV, validation data \\
Wrong functional form & \alert{no} & saturate, splines, flexible ML \\
Selection into the sample & \alert{no} & modelling the selection \\
Wrong estimand & \alert{no} & say what you are estimating \\
Influential outliers & \alert{no} & diagnostics; robust regression \\
$p$-hacking, specification search & \alert{no} & pre-registration \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Practical Protocol\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item State the estimand and the design \emph{before} choosing a standard error.
  \item \textbf{Cluster where treatment is assigned or where sampling occurred.}
        Otherwise use HC2 or HC3.
  \item Report the number of clusters $G$. If $G < 40$, use CR2 with
        Satterthwaite d.f.\ or a wild cluster bootstrap, and say so.
  \item Never pair a robust variance with a non-robust joint test
        (\texttt{anova()} defaults are not robust).
  \item If robust and classical standard errors differ dramatically, treat it as a
        diagnostic: something about the specification or the influence structure
        deserves attention.
  \item Report the specification you pre-committed to, then robustness checks, 
        not the reverse.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}
