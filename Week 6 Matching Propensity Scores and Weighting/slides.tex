\begin{frame}{Where We Are}
Last week we established that under \textbf{conditional ignorability},
\textbf{positivity} (today also called \emph{overlap} or \emph{common
support}), \textbf{consistency}, and \textbf{SUTVA},
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATE} = \E\Big[\,\E[Y_i\mid D_i=1,X_i] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0,X_i]\,\Big] .
\end{align*}
Today we take that identification result as \emph{given} and ask a purely
practical question: \alert{how should we actually do the adjustment?}
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{Four answers, and they are more alike than they look}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Matching}: find comparison units that look like the treated ones.
  \item \textbf{Regression / g-computation}: model $\E[Y\mid D,X]$ and average.
  \item \textbf{Weighting}: reweight the sample so treatment is unrelated to $X$.
  \item \textbf{Doubly robust}: do both, and get insurance.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
All four estimate the \emph{same} identified quantity. They differ in what they
model, \alert{what weights they put on covariate cells}, what they extrapolate,
and how they fail.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Regressing $Y$ on $D$ (and $X$) Gives\core}
\textbf{On $D$ alone.} One dummy is a saturated model, so OLS returns the CEF
$\E[Y_i\mid D_i]$ at $1$ and $0$: the coefficient on $D_i$ is the naive
comparison $\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0$. With $\tau=\text{ATE}$ and $\pi=\Pr(D_i=1)$,
\begin{align*}
  &\E[Y_i\mid D_i = 1]-\E[Y_i\mid D_i = 0] - \tau \\
  &\quad = \underbrace{\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i = 1]-\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i = 0]}_{\text{baseline selection}}
  + \underbrace{(1-\pi)(\text{ATT}-\text{ATU})}_{\text{selection on the effect}}
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\textbf{On $D$ and $X$, under conditional ignorability.} Within a cell
$X_i=x$, treatment is as good as random: the treated and the untreated share
the same distribution of baselines \emph{and} of gains. Both terms vanish cell
by cell, and
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_i\mid D_i = 1,X_i = x]-\E[Y_i\mid D_i = 0,X_i = x] = \tau(x)
  && \text{\footnotesize Week 5's CATE$(x)$}
\end{align*}
Selection is gone. Heterogeneity is not gone; it is now a set of numbers
$\tau(x)$, one per cell, waiting to be averaged.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Targets, One Set of Cell Effects\core}
The $\tau(x)$ are fixed by the data and ignorability. An estimand is a rule for
averaging them; each rule answers a different question:
\vspace{-3pt}
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATE} &= \sum_x \tau(x)\,\Pr(X_i = x)
  && \text{\footnotesize everyone: a universal policy} \\
  \text{ATT} &= \sum_x \tau(x)\,\Pr(X_i = x \mid D_i = 1)
  && \text{\footnotesize the treated: what treatment did for them} \\
  \text{ATU} &= \sum_x \tau(x)\,\Pr(X_i = x \mid D_i = 0)
  && \text{\footnotesize the untreated: what it would do for them}
\end{align*}
with $\text{ATE} = \pi\,\text{ATT} + (1-\pi)\,\text{ATU}$ (Week 5).
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt\small
  \item College: did graduates gain (ATT)? Would non-graduates (ATU)? Would
        everyone (ATE)? Under selection on gains they differ.
  \item The weights are covariate distributions: the ATT counts a cell by how
        common it is \emph{among graduates}.
  \item Name the target first (Week 5). Every method today computes one of
        these, or, unnoticed, a different one.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What OLS Returns\core}
OLS of $Y_i$ on $D_i$ and a saturated $X_i$ returns \emph{one} coefficient. It
too is an average of the $\tau(x)$, but with weights OLS picks, where
$e(x) = \Pr(D_i = 1 \mid X_i = x)$:
\begin{align*}
  \tau_R = \frac{\sum_x \tau(x)\;e(x)\big(1-e(x)\big)\Pr(X_i = x)}
                {\sum_x e(x)\big(1-e(x)\big)\Pr(X_i = x)}
  && \text{\footnotesize weight: the variance of treatment in the cell}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\tau_R$ is none of the three targets unless $\tau(x)$ is constant. It
        over-weights coin-flip cells and ignores lopsided ones: \alert{a number
        without a named target}. GSS, college on log earnings: naive $0.683$,
        $\tau_R = 0.631$, ATE $0.635$, ATT $0.625$.
  \item \alert{Ignorability settles identification; it does not choose the
        weights.} Where OLS's weights come from is derived at the end of the
        lecture.
\end{itemize}
\begin{block}{Matching: take the weights back}
Compute $\tau(x)$ cell by cell and average with the weights of the target you
named: the next section. The rest of the lecture is what to do when cells run out.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Exact Matching for the ATE\core}
Slide 5's ATE with sample quantities. Cells are the values of $X_i$; in every
cell that holds both treated and untreated units,
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau(x) = \bar Y_{1x} - \bar Y_{0x},
  \qquad
  \widehat{\text{ATE}} = \sum_x \frac{n_x}{n}\,\hat\tau(x)
  && \text{\footnotesize $n_x$ units in cell $x$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Each treated unit is compared only with untreated units that share its
        $X_i$: ``like with like'', nothing modelled. Under ignorability
        $\hat\tau(x)$ estimates $\tau(x)$, and the weights are the population's.
  \item The requirement is visible: \alert{a cell needs both arms}. Where it
        lacks one, $\hat\tau(x)$ does not exist. Next frame.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Unmatched Cells\core}
A cell with treated units and no untreated ones, or the reverse, has no
$\hat\tau(x)$. Three responses, in order of honesty:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Drop the cell and say so.} The estimand becomes the ATE on the
        matched population, $\sum_x \tau(x)\Pr(X_i = x \mid \text{matched})$.
        Report the share of units, and of treated units, that were dropped, and
        what those cells look like.
  \item \textbf{Coarsen.} Merge the cell with its neighbours (five-year age
        bands, education groups). Merged cells hold both arms more often, at
        the price of comparing units that differ within a band: a bias you then
        check (two frames on).
  \item \textbf{Model.} Predict $\bar Y_{0x}$ for the cell from nearby cells.
        This is regression's answer, and it is extrapolation: right only if the
        model is, and untestable where the data are absent.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\alert{Positivity fails in the sample before it fails in the population.} An
empty cell is not an error message; it is the estimand quietly changing.
Matching's merit is that it makes you look.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What You Check, for the ATE\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Coverage.} How many treated and untreated units sit in matched
        cells, and which cells were lost. This is the common-support check in
        its most transparent form.
  \item \textbf{Balance inside the cells.} Variables matched on exactly agree
        within every cell by construction. Variables you coarsened do not:
        inside a cell, graduates may still have more parental education than
        non-graduates. For each such variable take the treated--untreated gap
        within each matched cell, average the gaps over cells with the ATE
        weights $n_x/n$, and divide by the pooled SD: the \emph{standardized
        mean difference}, with $0.1$ the usual bar. Do the same for variables
        you did not match on at all.
  \item \textbf{Representativeness.} Compare the matched population's cell
        shares with the full sample's. If dropping cells changed whom you
        describe, say so.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Matching for the ATT\core}
Now the treated are the target population, and the operation changes:
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{\text{ATT}} = \sum_x \frac{n_{1x}}{n_1}\,\hat\tau(x)
  && \text{\footnotesize $n_{1x}$ treated in cell $x$, $n_1$ treated in matched cells}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Keep every treated unit. In each treated cell, weight the untreated
        units so that, cell by cell, the untreated look like the treated. The
        treated themselves are never reweighted.
  \item \textbf{Asymmetry.} Untreated units in cells with no treated unit go
        unused at no cost. Treated units in cells with no untreated unit are the
        problem: dropping them makes it the effect on the \emph{matchable} treated.
  \item \textbf{The checks follow.} Coverage: share of treated dropped. Balance:
        reweighted untreated against the treated, the treated SD as yardstick.
        Variance: how many untreated units each treated cell draws on.
  \item Positivity is needed only where the treated are: $e(x) < 1$ suffices.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{The Curse of Dimensionality\core}
The GSS example needed 108 cells for five coarsened covariates and still lost
14. With $p$ binary covariates there are $2^p$ cells: $p=10$ gives 1{,}024,
$p=20$ over a million. Exact matching becomes impossible long before the
covariates run out.
\vskip4pt
Practical responses, in rough order of desperation:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Coarsened exact matching} (CEM): bin continuous covariates
        coarsely, age in five-year bands, income in quintiles, then match
        exactly on the bins. Transparent, and you see immediately what you had to
        discard.
  \item \textbf{Nearest-neighbour matching} on a distance: Euclidean, or
        \emph{Mahalanobis} $\big[(X_i - X_j)'\hat{\bm\Sigma}^{-1}(X_i - X_j)\big]^{1/2}$,
        which scales by the covariance so no variable dominates by its units.
  \item \textbf{Propensity score matching}: collapse $X$ to a single number, 
        the next section.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\alert{Every one of these is a way of admitting that exact comparisons run out in
\emph{your sample}.} That is finite-sample sparsity, which is not the same thing
as a structural positivity failure ($e(x)\in\{0,1\}$ in the population), though
in practice you often cannot tell them apart. Being explicit about where the
comparisons thinned is a feature.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Matching Is Preprocessing, Not Estimation\core}
\srct{Ho, Imai, King, and Stuart (2007)} make the point that should organize your
workflow: matching is a \emph{nonparametric preprocessing step} that reduces how
much your final model has to do.
\vskip2pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Match, using \alert{no outcome data at all}. This is the design stage; it
        can be iterated freely without $p$-hacking, because you cannot see the
        answer.
  \item Check \textbf{coverage and balance}, as above: standardized mean
        differences below $0.1$, variance ratios near $1$, density overlaps.
  \item \emph{Then} run your estimate on the matched sample.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Standard Error of a Matching Estimate: Cells}
The estimate is a weighted sum of cell differences with weights fixed by the
target, so its variance is the weighted sum of the cells' variances:
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{\text{ATE}} &= \sum_x \frac{n_x}{n}\,\hat\tau(x)
  && \text{\footnotesize each } \hat\tau(x) \text{ a difference of two means} \\
  \Var\big(\widehat{\text{ATE}}\big)
  &= \sum_x \Big(\frac{n_x}{n}\Big)^2
    \Big(\frac{s^2_{1x}}{n_{1x}} + \frac{s^2_{0x}}{n_{0x}}\Big)
  && \text{\footnotesize ATT: weights } n_{1x}/n_1
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Bootstrap.} Resample units, rebuild the cells, recompute.
        Valid here, since the estimate is a smooth function of cell means.
        GSS: formula $0.035$ (ATE), $0.039$ (ATT); bootstrap $0.035$, $0.040$.
  \item \textbf{The regression route.} Weighted regression of $Y$ on $D$ with
        the matching weights gives the same estimate; its default standard
        error ignores the weights (GSS $0.030$, too small), a sandwich
        clustered by cell gives $0.036$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Standard Error: Nearest-Neighbour Matching}
Treated unit $i$ takes its $M$ closest controls, with replacement; $K_j$
counts how many times control $j$ was used.
\begin{align*}
  \widehat{\text{ATT}}
  &= \tfrac{1}{n_1}\textstyle\sum_{\text{treated}}\big(Y_i - \tfrac{1}{M}\sum_{j \in \text{match}(i)} Y_j\big)
  && \text{\footnotesize unit minus its matches' mean} \\
  &= \tfrac{1}{n_1}\big[\textstyle\sum_{\text{treated}} Y_i - \sum_{\text{controls}} \tfrac{K_j}{M}\,Y_j\big]
  && \text{\footnotesize regroup by control} \\
  \Var\big(\widehat{\text{ATT}}\big)
  &= \tfrac{1}{n_1^2}\big[\textstyle\sum_{\text{treated}} \sigma_1^2(X_i)
     + \sum_{\text{controls}} \big(\tfrac{K_j}{M}\big)^{2} \sigma_0^2(X_j)\big]
\end{align*}
with matches held fixed and outcomes independent, $\sigma_d^2(x) = \Var(Y\mid D = d, X = x)$.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{The square is the point.} A control used 20 times enters 20
        differences with the same sign; its noise adds up rather than
        averaging out, so it contributes $400\,\sigma_0^2$, not $20$. Reuse
        buys the best match every time and pays in variance.
  \item $\sigma_d^2(x)$ is estimated locally, as half the squared difference
        between a unit and its nearest neighbour in the \emph{same} arm; this
        is the variance \texttt{MatchIt} reports \srcp{Abadie and Imbens 2006}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Standard Error: Why Not Just Bootstrap It?}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The bootstrap resamples units with replacement and reruns the whole
        procedure. It works when the estimator moves smoothly as the data
        move. Matching does not: a small change in the sample changes
        \emph{which} control is nearest, and the $K_j$ jump by whole units.
  \item In a bootstrap sample a control appears two or three times, and its
        copies are each other's nearest neighbours; the distribution of the
        $K_j$ in bootstrap samples is not their distribution across real
        samples. Abadie and Imbens (2008) prove the bootstrap variance does
        not converge to the true one, in either direction.
  \item So: for cells or coarsened cells, bootstrap freely; for nearest
        neighbour with replacement, use the analytic formula on the previous
        frame (with an extra term if the score was estimated).
  \item The weighting section's trade, seen from the other side: weighting
        hides thin overlap in large $1/\hat e$, nearest-neighbour matching in
        large $K_j$. Report the largest of either.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mazrekaj et al.: What CEM Did}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Coarsen.} Income to deciles; parents' average age to six
        bands; family size to 1, 2, 3+. Sex, birth year, ethnicity, parental
        education, family structure, and neighbourhood were already categorical.
  \item \textbf{Match exactly on every covariate.} A cell is one combination of
        all of them. Each of the 1,390 treated children is compared only with
        untreated children in its own cell.
  \item \textbf{Prune.} Every untreated child who shares a cell with a treated
        child is kept, with no limit per treated child: 46,998 of them, about
        34 per treated child. The million set aside sit in cells with no
        treated child, because the cells include neighbourhood at birth and
        birth year. Our ``untreated-only cells go unused'', at scale. Every
        treated child found a cell, so the estimand is the ATT on all 1,390.
  \item \textbf{Why also OLS.} Agreement is a check of \emph{model
        dependence}: the linear specification was not doing the work. It says
        nothing about unobservables; that is the bounds, next.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Mazrekaj et al.: Lessons for Your Own Matching}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Define the treatment before you match.} ``Ever lived with''
        and ``born to'' are different populations with different answers;
        the first mixes in the effect of separation.
  \item \textbf{Measure covariates before the treatment}, here at birth. Income
        or neighbourhood at the test would be post-treatment (Week 5).
  \item \textbf{Show the selection}, as their Table 1 does, so the reader
        knows which way the raw gap is biased.
  \item \textbf{Report what pruning did}: cells kept, controls set aside,
        whether any treated unit was lost.
  \item \textbf{Regression beside matching} checks model dependence, not
        ignorability. The causal claim rests on the bounds, and on saying
        what they assume: that observed selection is informative about
        unobserved selection.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section[Propensity Score]{The Propensity Score}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Remarkable Theorem\core}
Define the \alert{propensity score}
\begin{align*}
  e(x) \equiv \Pr(D_i = 1 \mid X_i = x) .
\end{align*}
\begin{block}{Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983)}
If $\big(Y_i(0),Y_i(1)\big)\indep D_i \mid X_i$, then
\begin{align*}
  \big(Y_i(0),Y_i(1)\big)\indep D_i \mid e(X_i) .
\end{align*}
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Conditioning on a \emph{single number} suffices. A $p$-dimensional
        matching problem collapses to a one-dimensional one.
  \item $e(X)$ is a \emph{balancing score}: within strata of $e(X)$, the whole
        covariate vector is distributed identically in the two groups.
  \item This is why the propensity score is everywhere. It is also why it is
        routinely oversold; it does nothing about \emph{unobserved}
        confounders.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Proof of the Balancing Property\adv}
It suffices to show $\Pr(D_i = 1 \mid X_i, e(X_i)) = \Pr(D_i=1\mid e(X_i))$; the
left side is $e(X_i)$ by definition. For the right side, use iterated
expectations:
\begin{align*}
  \Pr\big(D_i = 1 \mid e(X_i)\big)
  = \E\big[\, \E[D_i \mid X_i] \ \big|\ e(X_i)\big]
  = \E\big[\, e(X_i)\ \big|\ e(X_i)\big]
  = e(X_i) .
\end{align*}
So $D_i \indep X_i \mid e(X_i)$. Now the potential outcomes, by the same move:
{\small
\begin{align*}
  &\Pr\big(D_i=1 \mid Y_i(0),Y_i(1),e(X_i)\big) \\
  &\quad= \E\big[\Pr(D_i=1\mid Y_i(0),Y_i(1),X_i)\ \big|\ Y_i(0),Y_i(1),e(X_i)\big]
  && \text{\footnotesize iterated expectations} \\
  &\quad= \E\big[e(X_i)\ \big|\ Y_i(0),Y_i(1),e(X_i)\big] = e(X_i)
  && \text{\footnotesize ignorability given } X_i
\end{align*}}
which is $\Pr(D_i=1\mid e(X_i))$: given the score, the potential outcomes say
nothing more about treatment. $\blacksquare$
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Note this is a statement about the \emph{true} $e(x)$. In practice we
        estimate it, and the properties of $\hat e$ matter, Week 13.
  \item Any function of $X$ that is finer than $e(X)$ is also a balancing score;
        $e(X)$ is the coarsest one.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimating and Checking It\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Usually a logit of $D$ on $X$ (Week 4). \alert{The model is not judged by
        fit.} A propensity model with a high AUC is often a bad sign, it means
        treated and control units are easy to tell apart, i.e.\ poor overlap.
  \item Judge it by \textbf{balance}: does weighting or matching on $\hat e$
        equalize the covariate distributions? Iterate on the specification until
        it does, adding interactions and polynomials as needed.
  \item Plot the two distributions of $\hat e$. Mass near $0$ among the treated,
        or near $1$ among the controls, is a positivity failure staring at you.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{The propensity score paradox}
The better your propensity model predicts treatment, the less common support you
have, and the less the data can tell you. Prediction quality and identification
pull in opposite directions.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Weighting]{Weighting}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Weighting, After Matching\core}
\textbf{It is the same operation.} Matching's ATT gave each untreated unit in
cell $x$ the weight $n_{1x}/n_{0x}$. Weighting gives it $e(x)/(1-e(x))$: the
same ratio with a model in place of the count. Both average $\tau(x)$ with the
target's weights.
\vskip2pt
\textbf{What the model buys.} A one-armed or empty cell is not dropped: a
logit says how $\Pr(D = 1\mid X)$ moves with $X$ and lends that to every
cell. Every unit is used, continuous $X$ and many covariates are no obstacle,
one formula serves every target, and it plugs into the doubly robust
estimator.
\vskip2pt
\textbf{What it costs.} Thin overlap does not disappear; it moves. Matching
drops a treated unit with no comparison and says so. Weighting keeps it at
weight $1/\hat e = 100$ and says so only through the variance and the largest
weights; and the weights are right only where the model is, including where
the data are too thin to check.
\vskip2pt
\alert{The trade:} matching narrows the target and says so; weighting keeps
the target and pays in variance and model dependence. Trimming $\hat e$ to
$[0.1,0.9]$ first makes the two agree, and is common practice.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recovering the ATT\core}
In cell $x$, $n_{1x}/n_{0x}$ estimates
$\Pr(D_i = 1\mid x)/\Pr(D_i = 0\mid x) = e(x)/(1-e(x))$, the \emph{odds} of
treatment. So matching's ATT weights were, in population terms:
\begin{align*}
  \text{treated: } 1, \qquad \text{untreated: } \frac{e(X_i)}{1-e(X_i)} .
\end{align*}
Check that the weighted untreated mean is the treated group's counterfactual:
{\small
\begin{align*}
  \E\!\left[\tfrac{e(X_i)}{1-e(X_i)}(1-D_i)\,Y_i\right]
  &= \E\!\left[\tfrac{e(X_i)}{1-e(X_i)}\,\E[(1-D_i)Y_i(0)\mid X_i]\right]
  && \text{\footnotesize iterate on } X_i \\
  &= \E\!\left[\tfrac{e(X_i)}{1-e(X_i)}\,(1-e(X_i))\,\E[Y_i(0)\mid X_i]\right]
  && \text{\footnotesize ignorability} \\
  &= \E\big[e(X_i)\,\E[Y_i(0)\mid X_i]\big]
  && \text{\footnotesize cancel} \\
  &= \E\big[D_i\,Y_i(0)\big] = \pi\,\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i = 1]
  && \text{\footnotesize } e(X_i) = \E[D_i\mid X_i] \text{, iterate}
\end{align*}}
Divide by $\pi = \E[D_i]$, which is what the weights sum to. The treated need
no weights: $\E[D_iY_i]/\pi = \E[Y_i(1)\mid D_i = 1]$. Subtract: the ATT.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recovering the ATE\core}
Now the target is everyone. Cell $x$ holds $n_x$ units, of which $n_{1x}$ are
treated; for the treated to stand in for the whole cell, each must count
$n_x/n_{1x}$ times, which estimates $1/e(x)$. Likewise each untreated unit
counts $1/(1-e(x))$:
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;\widehat{\text{ATE}}_{\text{IPW}}
  = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \left[\frac{D_i Y_i}{\hat e(X_i)}
  - \frac{(1-D_i)Y_i}{1-\hat e(X_i)}\right]\;}
\end{align*}
Rare treated units in a cell count for more, because they stand in for
everyone like them who was not treated. The same check as before, treated arm:
\begin{align*}
  \E\!\left[\frac{D_i Y_i}{e(X_i)}\right]
  = \E\!\left[\frac{\E[D_i\mid X_i]\,\E[Y_i(1)\mid X_i]}{e(X_i)}\right]
  = \E\big[\E[Y_i(1)\mid X_i]\big] = \E[Y_i(1)] ,
\end{align*}
by ignorability given $X_i$ and then iterated expectations; the same for the
untreated arm with $1-e$. $\blacksquare$ This is the
Horvitz--Thompson estimator of survey sampling, with treatment assignment as
the sampling mechanism.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{IPW Is a Weighted Regression}
Define the \alert{Horvitz--Thompson transformation}
\begin{align*}
  H_i = \frac{D_i}{e(X_i)} - \frac{1-D_i}{1-e(X_i)} .
\end{align*}
Then $\E[H_i Y_i] = \E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)]$: the ATE weights of the last frame in
one term. Equivalently:
\begin{block}{}
The coefficient on $D_i$ from a \textbf{weighted least squares} regression of
$Y_i$ on $D_i$ (with an intercept), weights $1/\hat e(X_i)$ for the treated
and $1/(1-\hat e(X_i))$ for the controls, is a difference in weighted means
with the weights normalized to sum to one in each arm (the H\'ajek form),
rather than the raw average that divides by $n$.
\end{block}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Practically convenient: \texttt{lm(y \textasciitilde\ d, weights = w)}
        runs IPW, and covariates can be added for extra protection.
  \item Conceptually important: weighting and regression are one continuum,
        and this is the bridge to the doubly robust estimator.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Stabilized Weights}
The ATE weights $1/e$ and $1/(1-e)$ make each arm stand in for everyone, so
each arm's weights sum to about $n$. Multiply by the size of the unit's own
arm, with $\pi = \Pr(D_i = 1)$:
\begin{align*}
  w_i^{S} = \frac{\Pr(D_i = d)}{\Pr(D_i = d\mid X_i)}
  = \begin{cases} \pi/e(X_i), & D_i = 1 \\ (1-\pi)/(1-e(X_i)), & D_i = 0 . \end{cases}
\end{align*}
Each arm's weights now sum to about $n_1$ and $n_0$ and average $1$; a mean
far from $1$ says the model for $e$ is off. Within an arm the multiplier is a
constant, so the H\'ajek estimate does not move:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\sum_i w_i^{S} D_i Y_i}{\sum_i w_i^{S} D_i}
  = \frac{\pi\sum_i D_iY_i/\hat e_i}{\pi\sum_i D_i/\hat e_i}
  = \frac{\sum_i D_iY_i/\hat e_i}{\sum_i D_i/\hat e_i}
  && \text{\footnotesize $\pi$ cancels; $1-\pi$ likewise in the control arm}
\end{align*}
The Horvitz--Thompson form, which divides by $n$, is not available: it needs
weights that sum to $n$ in each arm, and these sum to $n_d$.
\end{frame}

        $100$ and now carries $41$, still $41$ times its neighbours.
  \item \textbf{Means near $1$}: the logit is not badly off. The raw means
        are the stabilized ones divided by $\pi = 0.41$ and $1-\pi$.
  \item \textbf{Where stabilizing matters:} weights not normalized inside a
        ratio, because they are multiplied over periods or enter a regression
        with fewer parameters than treatment histories.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recovering the ATU, and the Whole Table\core}
The ATU mirrors the ATT: the untreated are the target, so they keep weight
$1$, and the treated are reweighted to look like them with the inverse odds
$(1-e(X_i))/e(X_i)$. Side by side, every weighting is a choice of whom the
other arm must resemble:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Target & Weight on treated & Weight on untreated & Who is reweighted \\
\midrule
ATE & $1/e(X)$ & $1/(1-e(X))$ & both, to the population \\
ATT & $1$ & $e(X)/(1-e(X))$ & the untreated, to the treated \\
ATU & $(1-e(X))/e(X)$ & $1$ & the treated, to the untreated \\
Overlap (ATO) & $1-e(X)$ & $e(X)$ & both, to where they overlap \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Each row is a covariate distribution the arms are pushed toward; the
        ATT row is $\Pr(X\mid D = 1)$, matching's weights from slide 5.
  \item The last row is new: a target defined by the data rather than by a
        population. Next frame.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Overlap Weights, Cell by Cell\core}
Cell $x$ holds $n_{1x} = e(x)\,n_x$ treated and $n_{0x} = (1-e(x))\,n_x$
untreated units. Give each treated unit the weight $1 - e(x) = n_{0x}/n_x$,
the share of its cell it can be compared with, and each untreated unit
$e(x) = n_{1x}/n_x$. Then in every cell
\begin{align*}
  \underbrace{n_{1x}\,\tfrac{n_{0x}}{n_x}}_{\text{treated total}}
  = \frac{n_{1x}n_{0x}}{n_x}
  = \underbrace{n_{0x}\,\tfrac{n_{1x}}{n_x}}_{\text{untreated total}} .
\end{align*}
\vspace{-6pt}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Balanced by construction}: both arms carry the same total
        weight in every cell. \textbf{Bounded}: no weight exceeds $1$.
  \item \textbf{Whom it describes.} Cell $x$ counts in proportion to
        $n_{1x}n_{0x}/n_x = n_x\,e(x)(1-e(x))$, the comparisons it affords: a
        one-armed cell drops out on its own, a half-and-half cell counts most.
        The target is ``people for whom the data contain a comparison'', the
        \emph{overlap population}, ATO.
  \item $n_x\,e(x)(1-e(x))$ is slide 6's regression weighting: ATO is what
        saturated OLS targeted all along, now chosen deliberately
        \srcp{Li, Morgan, and Zaslavsky 2018}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why It Works, and When It Does Not\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{The mechanism.} Within cell $x$, ignorability makes the treated
        a random sample of the cell. Weighting corrects only the \emph{sizes}
        of those samples so that each arm has the target's cell distribution.
        Then a weighted mean of $Y$ in an arm is a mean of $Y(d)$ over the
        target population. That is the whole proof, twice over on the last two
        frames.
  \item \textbf{It uses every unit.} No pairing, no discarding, and one
        formula for every estimand; only $e(x)$ has to be estimated.
  \item \textbf{It needs $e(x)$ right.} Matching on cells needed nothing
        modelled; weighting needs $\Pr(D = 1\mid X)$, usually a logit. A wrong
        model gives wrong weights everywhere at once.
  \item \textbf{It needs positivity, loudly.} A treated unit with
        $\hat e = 0.01$ carries the weight of a hundred; the variance goes with
        it. Where matching dropped a cell, weighting inflates it. Diagnose with
        the largest weights and the effective sample size,
        $(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$; then trim, or change the estimand.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Sharkey and Elwert (2011)}
\textbf{The question.} Does a poor neighbourhood lower a child's cognitive

\vskip3pt

\vskip3pt

not control for the same variable.''

confounders, then regress $A$ on $N_P$ and $N_O$ with no controls: a
\emph{marginal structural model} \srcp{Robins, Hern\'an, and Brumback 2000}.

\section[Doubly Robust]{Doubly Robust Estimation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{G-Computation: the Other Half\core}
Instead of modelling treatment, model the outcome. Under ignorability and
common support, fit $m_d(x) = \E[Y_i \mid D_i = d, X_i = x]$ for $d = 0, 1$,
then \emph{impute both potential outcomes for everyone} and average:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau_{\text{g-comp}} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
  \big[\hat m_1(X_i) - \hat m_0(X_i)\big] .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Week 4's predicted-probability procedure. One common-slope linear
        model returns $\tau_R$; separate models per arm average $\tau(x)$ with
        the ATE weights $\Pr(X_i=x)$, and slide 6's variance weighting is gone.
  \item Uses the whole sample; efficient when the outcome model is right;
        \alert{extrapolates silently} when it is not, since $\hat m_1(x)$ is
        computed even where nobody was treated (the anatomy shows how badly).
  \item The symmetry: IPW models $\Pr(D\mid X)$ and never touches $Y$;
        g-computation models $\E[Y\mid D,X]$ and never touches assignment.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Not Both?\core}
IPW is consistent only if $e(X_i)$ is right. G-computation is consistent only if
$m_d(X_i)$ is right. Combine them so that \emph{either} suffices.
\vskip3pt
Given the two nuisance functions
$m_d(x) = \E[Y_i\mid D_i=d,X_i=x]$ and $e(x) = \Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i=x)$, the
\alert{augmented IPW} estimand is
\begin{align*}
  \tau_{\text{AIPW}} = \E\Bigg[
  \underbrace{m_1(X_i) - m_0(X_i)}_{\text{g-computation}}
  + \underbrace{\frac{D_i\big(Y_i - m_1(X_i)\big)}{e(X_i)}
  - \frac{(1-D_i)\big(Y_i-m_0(X_i)\big)}{1-e(X_i)}}_{\text{IPW-weighted residuals}}
  \Bigg]
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{Double robustness}
$\tau_{\text{AIPW}} = \E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)]$ if \alert{either} $m_d$ \alert{or} $e$ is correctly
specified, not necessarily both. And when both are right, AIPW attains the
semiparametric efficiency bound.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Case 1: the Outcome Model Is Correct\adv}
Suppose $m_1$ is right but $e$ is arbitrary. Look at the augmentation term for
the treated arm, conditioning on $X_i$:
\begin{align*}
  \E\!\left[\frac{D_i}{e(X_i)}\big(Y_i - m_1(X_i)\big)\ \middle|\ X_i\right]
  &= \frac{1}{e(X_i)}\,\Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i)\,
     \E\big[Y_i - m_1(X_i)\mid D_i=1,X_i\big] \\[2pt]
  &= \frac{1}{e(X_i)}\,\E[D_i \mid X_i]\cdot \underbrace{0}_{\text{by definition of } m_1}
   \;=\; 0 .
\end{align*}
\pause
The same argument gives
$\E\big[\tfrac{1-D_i}{1-e(X_i)}(Y_i - m_0(X_i)) \mid X_i\big] = 0$. Hence
\begin{align*}
  \tau_{\text{AIPW}} = \E\big[m_1(X_i) - m_0(X_i)\big] = \E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)] .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\alert{The whole augmentation term vanishes}, whatever $e$ is. Note that this
did not use a correct propensity score anywhere.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Case 2: the Propensity Score Is Correct\adv}
Now suppose $e$ is right but $m_1$ is some wrong function. Then
\begin{align*}
  \E\!\left[\frac{D_i}{e(X_i)}\big(Y_i - m_1(X_i)\big)\ \middle|\ X_i\right]
  = \frac{1}{e(X_i)}\E[D_iY_i\mid X_i] - m_1(X_i) .
\end{align*}
Since $\E[D_iY_i\mid X_i] = e(X_i)\,\E[Y_i(1)\mid X_i]$ by ignorability, this is
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_i(1)\mid X_i] - m_1(X_i) .
\end{align*}
\pause
Symmetrically the control term contributes
$-\big(\E[Y_i(0)\mid X_i] - m_0(X_i)\big)$. Adding everything up, the wrong
$m_1, m_0$ cancel exactly:
\begin{align*}
  \tau_{\text{AIPW}} = \E\Big[\E[Y_i(1)\mid X_i] - \E[Y_i(0)\mid X_i]\Big]
         = \E[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)] . \qquad\blacksquare
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
Two independent routes to the same answer. That is the insurance.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the Cancellation Matters Beyond This Week}
Look again at what happened in the two cases. A first-order error in \emph{one}
nuisance function was wiped out by the other term. That is not a coincidence of
AIPW; it is a property the estimating equation was built to have.
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{The consequence}
The bias of AIPW is roughly the \emph{product} of the two nuisance errors, not
their sum. So if both $\hat e$ and $\hat m_d$ are merely \emph{approximately}
right, the bias is second-order small, much smaller than either model's own
error.
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item This is why doubly robust estimators are the default in modern practice
        even when nobody believes either model exactly.
  \item It is also what eventually licenses estimating $\hat e$ and $\hat m_d$
        with flexible, machine-learning methods rather than logit and OLS.
        \alert{That is a Week 13 topic}; the property has a name (\emph{Neyman
        orthogonality}) and we will not need it before then.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An Honest Warning\core}
Double robustness is often oversold.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item ``Either model correct'' is a low bar in theory and an unreachable one in
        practice: both models are almost certainly wrong, and then AIPW is
        inconsistent too.
  \item What it really buys is \alert{second-order bias}: the bias is the
        \emph{product} of the two models' errors, so it is small when both are
        approximately right. That is genuinely valuable, and it is the honest
        version of the claim.
  \item With extreme weights, AIPW can be more unstable than either component
        alone \srcp{Kang and Schafer 2007}.
  \item \alert{None of this addresses unobserved confounding.} Everything today
        assumes conditional ignorability, and no diagnostic can test it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sensitivity Analysis: Taking the Assumption Seriously\core}
Since ignorability is untestable, ask instead: \emph{how strong would an
unobserved confounder have to be to overturn my conclusion?}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Rosenbaum bounds}: how much would two matched units have to
        differ in their odds of treatment before the inference flips?
        \srcp{DiPrete and Gangl 2004: unemployment insurance and re-employment}
  \item \textbf{Partial $R^2$ / robustness value} \srcp{Cinelli and Hazlett 2020}:
        express the required confounder strength as the share of residual
        variation in $D$ and in $Y$ it would have to explain, then benchmark it
        against an \emph{observed} covariate, ``the confounder would need to
        be three times as strong as parental education.''
  \item \textbf{$E$-value}: the minimum risk ratio a confounder would need with
        both treatment and outcome.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\alert{Report one.} It converts an untestable assumption into a quantitative
claim your reader can argue with.
\end{frame}

\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{A Workflow\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item State the estimand (ATE? ATT? ATO? on which population?) before touching
        data.
  \item Draw the DAG. Decide what to condition on, and what \emph{not} to.
  \item Estimate the propensity score. Look at overlap. Trim or redefine if needed
        and say so.
  \item Match or weight. Check balance with standardized mean differences and
        density plots, \alert{never} with $t$-tests.
  \item Estimate with a doubly robust estimator; report matching, weighting, and
        regression alongside.
  \item Get standard errors that respect the design: bootstrap the whole
        estimation pipeline for weighting and regression estimators, and cluster
        where treatment was assigned. \alert{Do not bootstrap nearest-neighbour
        matching}, the ordinary bootstrap is invalid for it
        \srcp{Abadie and Imbens 2008}; use their analytic variance instead.
  \item Report a sensitivity analysis.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\section[Anatomy]{The Anatomy of Adjustment}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What OLS Does With the Cell Effects: Goal and Plan}
Slide 6 asserted that OLS on $D_i$ and a saturated $X_i$ returns a
variance-weighted average of the $\tau(x)$. Now the derivation. From slide 5,
\begin{align*}
  \tau(x) &= \E[Y_i\mid D_i = 1,X_i = x] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i = 0,X_i = x] \\
  \text{ATE} &= \textstyle\sum_x \tau(x)\,\Pr(X_i = x), \qquad
  \text{ATT} = \textstyle\sum_x \tau(x)\,\Pr(X_i = x \mid D_i = 1) .
\end{align*}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Rewrite the ATT weights in terms of $e(x)$: one line of Bayes' rule.
  \item Write the OLS coefficient by Frisch--Waugh--Lovell.
  \item Simplify its numerator.
  \item Read off the weights, and compare them with matching's.
  \item Two consequences, the continuous case, and Angrist (1998) as the
        empirical check.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anatomy of the Matching Estimand}
Apply Bayes' rule to the ATT weights:
\begin{align*}
  \Pr(X_i = x \mid D_i = 1)
  = \frac{\Pr(D_i = 1\mid X_i = x)\,\Pr(X_i=x)}{\Pr(D_i=1)} .
\end{align*}
\pause
Substituting,
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;
  \text{ATT}
  = \frac{\sum_x \tau(x)\;\Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i=x)\;\Pr(X_i=x)}
         {\sum_x \Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i=x)\;\Pr(X_i=x)}\;}
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\alert{The matching-based ATT weights each cell effect in proportion to the
probability of treatment in that cell}; that is, in proportion to the
propensity score $e(x)$. Cells where treatment is common count for more, which
is exactly what ``the effect on the treated'' should mean.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anatomy of Regression Adjustment: Setup\adv}
Now do the same accounting for regression. To avoid conflating the weighting
question with a functional-form question, specify a model
\emph{saturated in $X_i$}:
\begin{align*}
  Y_i = \sum_x \mathds{1}(X_i = x)\,\alpha_x \;+\; \tau_R\, D_i \;+\; \epsilon_i .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
By Frisch--Waugh--Lovell (Week 1), the coefficient on $D_i$ equals the slope
of $Y_i$ on the part of $D_i$ the cell dummies cannot predict. Because the
model is saturated, that projection is the cell mean of $D_i$, $e(X_i)$, so
\begin{align*}
  \check D_i \equiv D_i - e(X_i)
  = \begin{cases} 1 - e(X_i) & \text{treated} \\ -\,e(X_i) & \text{untreated}\end{cases}
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  \tau_R = \frac{\Cov(Y_i,\check D_i)}{\Var(\check D_i)} .
\end{align*}
$\check D_i$ is the \emph{surprise} in treatment given the cell: large for a
treated unit in a cell where treatment is rare. Next: rewrite the covariance
so that the cell effects $\tau(x)$ appear.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anatomy of Regression Adjustment: the Covariance\adv}
Two facts about $\check D_i$ do all the work: its mean is zero, and it is a
function of $(D_i, X_i)$ only.
\begin{align*}
  \E[\check D_i]
  &= \E[D_i] - \E\big[\E[D_i\mid X_i]\big] = 0
  && \text{\footnotesize iterated expectations} \\
  \Cov(Y_i,\check D_i)
  &= \E[\check D_i Y_i] - \E[\check D_i]\,\E[Y_i] = \E[\check D_i Y_i]
  && \text{\footnotesize definition; second term is } 0 \\
  &= \E\big[\,\E[\check D_i Y_i \mid D_i, X_i]\,\big]
  && \text{\footnotesize iterate on } (D_i,X_i) \\
  &= \E\big[\,\check D_i\,\E[Y_i \mid D_i, X_i]\,\big]
  && \text{\footnotesize $\check D_i$ is fixed once $(D_i,X_i)$ are} \\
  \Var(\check D_i)
  &= \E[\check D_i^{\,2}]
  && \text{\footnotesize mean zero}
\end{align*}
The last step is the one to see: inside $\E[\,\cdot\mid D_i,X_i]$, anything
built from $D_i$ and $X_i$ is a constant and comes out. So
\begin{align*}
  \tau_R = \frac{\E\big[\check D_i\,\E[Y_i\mid D_i,X_i]\big]}{\E[\check D_i^{\,2}]} ,
\end{align*}
and the numerator now contains $\E[Y_i\mid D_i,X_i]$, which is where $\tau(x)$
lives. Next frame.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anatomy of Regression Adjustment: the Numerator\adv}
Because $D_i$ is binary, the conditional expectation decomposes exactly:
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_i\mid D_i,X_i]
  &= \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0,X_i]
   + D_i\Big(\E[Y_i\mid D_i=1,X_i]-\E[Y_i\mid D_i=0,X_i]\Big) \\
  &= \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0,X_i] + D_i\,\tau(X_i) .
\end{align*}
\pause
Substitute into the numerator:
\begin{align*}
  &\E\Big[(D_i - \E[D_i\mid X_i])\big(\E[Y_i\mid D_i=0,X_i] + D_i\tau(X_i)\big)\Big] \\
  &\quad= \underbrace{\E\Big[(D_i-\E[D_i\mid X_i])\,\E[Y_i\mid D_i=0,X_i]\Big]}_{=\,0
     \text{ (a function of } X_i \text{ only)}}
   + \E\Big[(D_i-\E[D_i\mid X_i])\,D_i\,\tau(X_i)\Big] \\[2pt]
  &\quad= \E\Big[(D_i - \E[D_i\mid X_i])^2\,\tau(X_i)\Big] ,
\end{align*}
where the last step uses $\E[(D_i-\E[D_i\mid X_i])D_i \mid X_i]
= \Var(D_i\mid X_i)$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Anatomy of Regression Adjustment: the Result}
Putting numerator and denominator together and applying iterated expectations:
\begin{align*}
  \tau_R
  = \frac{\E\big[(D_i-\E[D_i\mid X_i])^2\,\tau(X_i)\big]}
         {\E\big[(D_i-\E[D_i\mid X_i])^2\big]}
  = \boxed{\;\frac{\E\big[\sigma_D^2(X_i)\,\tau(X_i)\big]}{\E\big[\sigma_D^2(X_i)\big]}\;}
\end{align*}
where $\sigma_D^2(x) \equiv \Var(D_i\mid X_i = x)$. Since $D_i$ is binary,
$\sigma^2_D(x) = e(x)\big(1-e(x)\big)$, so
\begin{align*}
  \tau_R = \frac{\sum_x \tau(x)\, e(x)\big(1-e(x)\big)\,\Pr(X_i=x)}
                  {\sum_x e(x)\big(1-e(x)\big)\,\Pr(X_i=x)} .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\alert{Regression weights each cell by the \emph{conditional variance} of
treatment.} Compare with matching, which weighted by $e(x)$ itself. Cells with
$e(x)\approx 0.5$ dominate the regression, and cells that are nearly all treated
or nearly all control contribute almost nothing \emph{to $\tau_R$}. Matching
behaves differently at the top end, which is the next slide.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Two Consequences Worth Stating Plainly}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{The two weightings answer different questions} even under
        identical assumptions and with the same saturated specification. Matching
        is a \emph{method}, not an estimand, and can be pointed at the ATE or the
        ATT by changing the cell weights; the ATT weighting above is the one that
        necessarily differs from $\tau_R$. Neither $\tau_R$ nor the ATT is
        ``the'' ATE unless effects are homogeneous.
  \item \textbf{The regression weight $e(x)(1-e(x))$ vanishes at $e(x)\in\{0,1\}$},
        so cells with no comparison units drop out of $\tau_R$ silently.
        \alert{Positivity failures do not produce an error message; they produce a
        quietly redefined estimand.}
  \item Matching is different: a cell with $e(x)\approx1$ carries a \emph{large}
        ATT weight. But at $e(x)=1$ there is no control unit, so $\tau(x)$ is
        simply not identified; the estimator has nothing to average, and the
        honest response is to say which cells you dropped.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{With continuous covariates}
The anatomy still holds, as an integral:
\begin{align*}
  \tau_R = \frac{\int \tau(x)\,e(x)\big(1-e(x)\big)f(x)\,dx}
                  {\int e(x)\big(1-e(x)\big)f(x)\,dx} .
\end{align*}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

bin $(x-h,x+h)$, and positivity becomes

arms \emph{near} every $x$. A simulation \srcp{figs/w6\_fuzzy.R}:

\section[Heckman]{Selection on Unobservables: Heckman}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Today Could Not Touch\adv}
Slide 4's gap, baseline selection plus selection on the effect, was made zero
all day by conditioning on $X$. If units select on something unmeasured, it is
not zero, and no diagnostic today can see it.
Heckman (1978, 1979): instead of assuming it away, model \emph{who gets
treated} and \emph{how their unobservables relate to $Y$}, then compute it.
\begin{align*}
  D_i &= \mathds{1}\big[\,Z_i'\gamma + u_i > 0\,\big]
      && \text{\footnotesize selection rule: a probit, Week 4} \\
  Y_i &= X_i'\beta + \tau D_i + \epsilon_i
      && \text{\footnotesize outcome, one constant effect } \tau
\end{align*}
with $(u_i,\epsilon_i)$ bivariate normal, $\Var(u_i)=1$,
$\Var(\epsilon_i)=\sigma^2$, $\Cov(u_i,\epsilon_i)=\rho\sigma$.
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\rho\neq0$ \emph{is} selection on unobservables: the same unmeasured
        thing pushes a unit into treatment and moves its outcome.
  \item $Z_i$ contains $X_i$ and, ideally, one variable that shifts selection
        but not $Y_i$; why ``ideally'' comes in two frames.
  \item A constant $\tau$ drops selection on the effect: Heckman's
        simplification, not a result.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Gap Has a Formula\adv}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.63\textwidth}
\small
Bivariate normality gives $\E[\epsilon_i\mid u_i]=\rho\sigma\,u_i$
(slope $\Cov/\Var$). Write $c_i=Z_i'\gamma$, so $D_i=1$ means $u_i>-c_i$.
\begin{align*}
  \E[u_i\mid u_i>-c_i]
    &= \frac{\int_{-c_i}^{\infty} u\,\phi(u)\,du}{\Pr(u_i>-c_i)}
    && \text{\footnotesize conditional mean} \\
    &= \frac{\phi(-c_i)}{\Phi(c_i)} = \frac{\phi(c_i)}{\Phi(c_i)}
    && \text{\footnotesize } \phi'(u)=-u\,\phi(u)
\end{align*}
Below $-c_i$ the same integral gives $-\phi(c_i)/[1-\Phi(c_i)]$.
Multiply both by $\rho\sigma$ and subtract:
\begin{align*}
  &\E[\epsilon_i\mid D_i = 1,Z_i]-\E[\epsilon_i\mid D_i = 0,Z_i] \\
  &\quad= \rho\sigma\Big[\frac{\phi(c_i)}{\Phi(c_i)}+\frac{\phi(c_i)}{1-\Phi(c_i)}\Big]
   = \rho\sigma\,\frac{\phi(c_i)}{\Phi(c_i)\,[1-\Phi(c_i)]}
\end{align*}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.35\textwidth}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/heckman_bias.pdf}
\vskip1pt
{\footnotesize The gap at $\rho\sigma=.5$ by treated share: never zero,
smallest at one half.}
\vskip4pt
\begin{flushleft}\small
\textbf{Worked number.} $\rho=.5$, $\sigma=1$, $c=0$ (half treated):
$.5\times.399/.25=0.80$. With $\tau=1$ the naive gap should be $1.80$;
$10^6$ simulated draws give $1.797$.
\end{flushleft}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heckman's Two Steps\adv}
The omitted variable in the OLS of $Y$ on $X$ and $D$ is a \emph{known
function} of $c_i$ times one unknown constant $\rho\sigma$. Estimate the
function, then put it into the regression.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Probit of $D_i$ on $Z_i$ gives $\hat\gamma$ and $\hat c_i=Z_i'\hat\gamma$.
  \item Build the correction term, one expression for both groups:
        $\hat\lambda_i = D_i\,\phi(\hat c_i)/\Phi(\hat c_i)
        -(1-D_i)\,\phi(\hat c_i)/[1-\Phi(\hat c_i)]$,
        so that $\E[\epsilon_i\mid D_i,Z_i]=\rho\sigma\,\lambda_i$.
  \item OLS of $Y_i$ on $X_i$, $D_i$, $\hat\lambda_i$. The coefficient on $D_i$
        is $\hat\tau$; the coefficient on $\hat\lambda_i$ estimates $\rho\sigma$,
        and its test against zero is a test of selection on unobservables.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip2pt
Simulation, $n=2{,}000$, 1,000 replications, true $\tau=1$, $\rho\sigma=.5$:
OLS on $X$ and $D$ averages $1.574$; the two-step averages $1.004$, with
$0.498$ on $\hat\lambda_i$.
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item Step-2 OLS standard errors are wrong: $\hat\lambda_i$ is estimated and
        $\epsilon_i$ is heteroskedastic given $D_i$. Bootstrap both steps, or
        fit both equations by maximum likelihood (\texttt{sampleSelection::treatReg}).
  \item Wage version (Heckman 1979): $Y_i$ observed only when $D_i=1$. Same
        recipe on the $D_i=1$ rows, where $\hat\lambda_i=\phi/\Phi$ is the
        ``inverse Mills ratio''.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{center}\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}

Estimator of $\tau=1$ & mean & SD & mean & SD \\
\midrule

$\chi^2_1$ shocks, same $\rho$. \texttt{figs/w6\_heckman.R}}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{An exclusion restriction.} With $Z=X$, $\hat\lambda_i$ is a smooth
        function of $X_i$ and $D_i$: regressing it on them gives $R^2=.999$
        (vs.\ $.667$ with $Z$). Only the curvature of $\phi/\Phi$ identifies
        $\tau$, and the SD is 23 times larger. The excluded variable must move
        treatment and have no direct effect on $Y$: \alert{an instrument},
        next week's word.
  \item \textbf{Bivariate normality.} The gap formula is the normal formula. With
        an excluded variable, skewed errors cost little here ($0.96$ for $1$);
        without one, only the normal shape identifies $\tau$, and it returns
        $-0.17$. \srcp{Puhani 2000}
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heckman in Sociological Research\adv}
\textbf{Mare and Winship (1984, \emph{ASR}).} Employment among out-of-school
young men, black and white, 1964--1981. That population is itself selected,
by school enrollment and military enlistment, differently by race and over
time; enrollment and enlistment become the selection equation, and part of the
employment trend is composition.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Manski, Sandefur, McLanahan, and Powers (1992, \emph{JASA}).} Family
structure in adolescence and high school graduation: a nonintact family may
hurt, or an unobserved process may produce both. Parametric selection-model
estimates are reported next to nonparametric bounds (Week 5), so the reader
sees how much of the answer the normality assumption supplies.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Mulligan and Rubinstein (2008, \emph{QJE}).} The wage version, in
economics but on our question. Selection of women into full-time work turned
from negative in the 1970s to positive in the 1990s (exclusion restriction:
young children by marital status); holding composition fixed, most of the
apparent narrowing of the gender wage gap is a change in who works.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Critiques, and the Bridge to Week 7\adv}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Berk (1983, \textit{ASR})} brought it to sociology: who is
        arrested, employed, or surveyed is a social outcome, so samples are
        selected on what we study.
  \item \textbf{Winship and Mare (1992, \textit{Annual Review})} survey its uses
        in labor markets, schooling, courts, and mobility: results are sensitive
        to how selection is assumed to occur.
  \item \textbf{Stolzenberg and Relles (1997, \textit{ASR})} decompose the bias so
        its likely size can be judged before correcting.
  \item \textbf{Bushway, Johnson, and Slocum (2007)} review criminology's
        sentencing studies: the two-step run routinely with the same regressors
        in both equations, the $R^2=.999$ row.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\textbf{What survives.} The gap is real and has a shape. Fixing it needs a
variable that moves treatment and nothing else. Next week keeps that variable,
drops normality and the constant $\tau$, and pays with a different estimand.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Looking Ahead}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Everything today but the last section rested on \alert{conditional
        ignorability}: the claim that we measured every confounder. Matching,
        weighting, and AIPW make that assumption easier to implement; they do
        not make it more likely to be true. Heckman trades it for normality and
        an exclusion restriction.
  \item The anatomy results are the transferable lesson: \emph{every} adjustment
        method is a weighting of cell-level effects, and knowing the weights tells
        you what your number means.
  \item \textbf{Weeks 7--11} abandon ignorability and look for special structure
 (an instrument, a discontinuity, parallel trends) that identifies an
        effect \emph{despite} unobserved confounding.
  \item \textbf{Week 13} returns to today's AIPW estimator and asks what changes
        when the covariate set is large enough that logit and OLS will not do.
\end{itemize}
