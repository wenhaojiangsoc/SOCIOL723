\section[Sharp RD]{Sharp Regression Discontinuity}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Setup and Identification\core}
A \alert{running variable} (or forcing variable) $X_i$ determines treatment
deterministically:
\begin{align*}
  D_i = \mathds{1}\{X_i \geq c\} .
\end{align*}
Write $m_d(x) = \E[Y_i(d)\mid X_i = x]$ for the two potential-outcome curves.
\vskip2pt
\begin{block}{Continuity assumption \srcp{Hahn, Todd, and van der Klaauw 2001}}
$m_1(x)$ and $m_0(x)$ are continuous in $x$ at $x = c$.
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
Then
\begin{align*}
  \tau_{RD} = \lim_{x\downarrow c}\E[Y_i\mid X_i = x]
            - \lim_{x\uparrow c}\E[Y_i\mid X_i = x]
            = \E\big[Y_i(1)-Y_i(0)\ \big|\ X_i = c\big] .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Not assumed: anything about the shape of $\E[Y\mid X]$ away from
        the cutoff, or ignorability anywhere else.
  \item \alert{Positivity fails at every $x$}: $\Pr(D=1\mid X=x)$ is $0$ or $1$.
        Identification comes from continuity, not overlap, which is why the
        estimand is local to $c$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Curves, Each Seen on One Side}

\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The observed regression $\E[Y_i\mid X_i=x]$ follows $m_0$ to the left of $c$
        and $m_1$ to the right; it jumps at $c$ by $m_1(c) - m_0(c)$.
  \item The dashed halves are never observed. Away from $c$ the gap between the
        curves, $\tau(x)$, is not identified without further assumptions.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification, Step by Step}
Above the cutoff every unit is treated, so $Y_i = Y_i(1)$ there:
\begin{align*}
  \lim_{x\downarrow c}\E[Y_i\mid X_i=x]
  &= \lim_{x\downarrow c}\E[Y_i(1)\mid X_i=x]
   && \text{\footnotesize $D_i = 1$ for $x \geq c$; consistency} \\
  &= \lim_{x\downarrow c} m_1(x) = m_1(c)
   && \text{\footnotesize continuity of $m_1$ at $c$} \\[3pt]
  \lim_{x\uparrow c}\E[Y_i\mid X_i=x]
  &= \lim_{x\uparrow c}\E[Y_i(0)\mid X_i=x]
   && \text{\footnotesize $D_i = 0$ for $x < c$; consistency} \\
  &= m_0(c)
   && \text{\footnotesize continuity of $m_0$ at $c$}
\end{align*}
Subtract:
\begin{align*}
  \lim_{x\downarrow c}\E[Y_i\mid X_i=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c}\E[Y_i\mid X_i=x]
  = m_1(c) - m_0(c) = \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\mid X_i = c].
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Continuity of $m_1$ is used only from the right and of $m_0$ only
        from the left: we need the \emph{unseen} side of each curve to meet
        the seen side at $c$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Continuity Rules Out}
Continuity fails when something other than $D$ changes at $c$.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{A second treatment at the same cutoff.} An age threshold that
        grants a pension and also lowers a tax; a district boundary where
        schools, police, and taxes all change. The jump is the effect of the
        bundle.
  \item \textbf{Sorting.} If people who would do well anyway can place
        themselves just above $c$, the units just above differ from those just
        below in $Y_i(0)$ itself, and $m_0$ jumps.
  \item \textbf{Selection of the cutoff on outcomes.} An agency that sets $c$ where
        it expects a change, after seeing the data, builds the jump in.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
Continuity is a statement about $Y_i(0)$ and $Y_i(1)$, so it cannot be tested
directly. It has \alert{two testable implications}: the density of $X$ and the
distribution of pre-determined covariates should not jump at $c$. Both come
from the next frame.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Continuity Should Hold \srcp{Lee 2008}}
Let $W_i$ be a unit's type (everything fixed before $X$ is realized) and let
$X_i$ have density $f(x\mid w)$ given $W_i = w$. By Bayes' rule
\begin{align*}
  f(w\mid x) &= \frac{f(x\mid w)\,f(w)}{f(x)}
   && \text{\footnotesize density of types among units with $X_i=x$} \\
  m_d(x) &= \int \E[Y_i(d)\mid W_i=w]\, f(w\mid x)\,dw
   && \text{\footnotesize $Y_i(d)$ is a function of type}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \alert{If every type's density $f(x\mid w)$ is continuous at $c$}, then
        $f(w\mid x)$ is continuous, so $m_0$, $m_1$, and the mean of any
        pre-determined covariate are continuous at $c$.
  \item This is \textbf{imprecise control}: units may influence $X$ (study harder,
        campaign more), but a random component they cannot steer remains.
        Near $c$, treatment is as good as randomly assigned.
  \item It fails when some type can place itself exactly: then $f(x\mid w)$
        jumps at $c$ for that type, and so does $f(x)$, the density of $X$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Estimand Is Local, and That Matters\core}
$\tau_{RD}$ is the average effect \alert{for units at the cutoff}. Nothing more.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item A scholarship RD tells you about students at the eligibility margin, not
        about the strongest or weakest applicants.
  \item Extrapolating away from $c$ requires a functional-form assumption of
        exactly the kind RD was designed to avoid.
  \item Compare with the LATE from Week 7: both are estimands you did not choose,
        determined by the design rather than by the question. State them plainly.
  \item In exchange, RD is often the most credible observational design there is.
        The identifying variation is visible in a scatterplot.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\srct{Lee (2008)}'s reformulation is helpful: if agents cannot perfectly control
$X_i$, then treatment near the cutoff is \emph{as good as randomly assigned}, and
the design is a local experiment.
\end{frame}

\section[Estimation]{Estimation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimation: Local Linear Regression\core}
Fit separate linear regressions on each side of the cutoff, using only
observations within a bandwidth $h$, weighted by a kernel:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau = \hat\mu_+ - \hat\mu_-,
  \qquad
  (\hat\mu_+,\hat\beta_+) = \argmin \sum_{i: c \leq X_i < c+h}
  K\!\left(\frac{X_i - c}{h}\right)\big(Y_i - \mu - \beta(X_i - c)\big)^2 ,
\end{align*}
and symmetrically below.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Local linear, not global polynomial.} High-order global
        polynomials give bizarre weights to distant observations and are now
        strongly discouraged \srcp{Gelman and Imbens 2019}. Use degree 1 or 2.
  \item \textbf{Triangular kernel} (weights falling linearly to zero at $h$) is
        the standard choice and is boundary-optimal.
  \item Always let the slope differ on the two sides; forcing a common slope
        imposes a restriction you have no reason to believe.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{The Estimator, Written Out}
The RD estimate is the treatment coefficient in a \alert{kernel-weighted, fully
interacted local linear regression} on the observations within the bandwidth:
\begin{align*}
  \min_{\mu,\,\tau,\,\beta_-,\,\beta_+}
  \sum_{i:\,|X_i - c|\leq h} K\!\left(\frac{X_i-c}{h}\right)
  \Big(Y_i - \mu - \tau D_i - \beta_-(X_i-c)(1-D_i) - \beta_+(X_i-c)D_i\Big)^2 ,
\end{align*}
with $D_i = \mathds 1\{X_i \geq c\}$ and $\hat\tau$ the estimate.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \alert{Centre the running variable at $c$}, so that the intercept shift
        $\tau$ is evaluated at the cutoff rather than at $X = 0$.
  \item \alert{Interact the slope with $D_i$.} Forcing a common slope imposes a
        restriction you have no reason to believe, and it lets data on one side
        contaminate the estimate on the other.
  \item \textbf{Triangular kernel} $K(u) = (1-|u|)_+$ weights observations
        linearly down to zero at the boundary and is minimax optimal at a
        boundary point. A uniform kernel is simpler and usually similar.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Not Compare Means Near the Cutoff?}
Take the mean of $Y$ within $h$ on each side (a local constant fit). Each OLS
line passes through its side's means, so with $\bar x_\pm$ the mean distance to $c$:
\begin{align*}
  \bar Y_+ - \bar Y_-
  &= (\hat\mu_+ + \hat\beta_+ \bar x_+) - (\hat\mu_- + \hat\beta_- \bar x_-)
   && \text{\footnotesize the line through the means} \\
  &= \hat\tau_{\text{linear}} + \underbrace{\hat\beta_+\bar x_+ - \hat\beta_-\bar x_-}_{\text{slope}\,\times\,\text{distance}}
   && \text{\footnotesize regroup}
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
Senate, $h = 17.75$, uniform kernel:
\begin{align*}
  10.91 = 7.09 + 0.260 \times 7.96 + 0.226 \times 7.75 = 7.09 + 2.07 + 1.75 .
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \alert{The bias of comparing means is of order $h$}: the means sit at
        $\pm h/2$ on average, not at $c$, and any slope turns that distance
        into a gap. Here it inflates the effect by half.
  \item A boundary is the worst place for a local mean: data lie on one side
        only, so the slope cannot cancel. Local linear removes the first-order
        term.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Bias of Local Linear Is of Order $h^2$}
Above $c$, write $u_i = (X_i - c)/h \in [0,1]$; take $u$ uniform (a uniform kernel, flat density) and expand
\begin{align*}
  m_1(c + hu) &= m_1(c) + m_1'(c)\,hu + \tfrac12 m_1''(c)\,h^2u^2 + \dots
   && \text{\footnotesize Taylor} .
\end{align*}
The line fits the first two terms exactly. The bias is the intercept of the
best line through $u^2$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{slope} &= \frac{\Cov(u,u^2)}{\Var(u)} = \frac{\tfrac14 - \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13}{\tfrac1{12}} = 1
   && \text{\footnotesize $\E u^k = 1/(k+1)$} \\
  \text{intercept} &= \E u^2 - 1\cdot\E u = \tfrac13 - \tfrac12 = -\tfrac16
   && \text{\footnotesize line through the means} \\
  \E[\hat\mu_+] - m_1(c) &\approx \tfrac12 m_1''(c)\,h^2 \cdot\big(-\tfrac16\big) = -\tfrac{1}{12}\,m_1''(c)\,h^2
   && \text{\footnotesize scale by $\tfrac12 m_1''h^2$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \alert{Bias $= B h^2$}, with $B$ set by the curvature on each side:
        $B = -\tfrac1{12}\,(m_1''(c) - m_0''(c))$. Other kernels change only the
        constant $\tfrac1{12}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Variance Is of Order $1/(nh)$}
With $f(c)$ the density of $X$ at $c$, about $m = n f(c) h$ observations fall
in $[c, c+h]$. The fitted intercept is a prediction at the \emph{edge} of the data:
\begin{align*}
  \Var(\hat\mu_+)
  &= \frac{\sigma^2}{m}\left(1 + \frac{(0 - \bar u)^2}{\Var(u)}\right)
   && \text{\footnotesize variance of an OLS prediction at $u=0$} \\
  &= \frac{\sigma^2}{m}\left(1 + \frac{1/4}{1/12}\right) = \frac{4\,\sigma^2}{m}
   && \text{\footnotesize $u$ uniform on $[0,1]$} \\
  \Var(\hat\tau) &= \frac{4\,(\sigma_+^2 + \sigma_-^2)}{n f(c)\, h} \equiv \frac{V}{nh}
   && \text{\footnotesize two independent sides}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \alert{Extrapolating to the edge costs a factor of 4}: a mean at the
        centre of the window would have variance $\sigma^2/m$.
  \item The variance falls as the window widens, the bias rises: that is the
        bandwidth problem.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\vskip-10pt
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Wide} $h$: more data, lower variance, but the linear
        approximation is worse far from $c$, bias.
  \item \textbf{Narrow} $h$: less bias, but few observations, variance.
  \item Choose $h$ by minimizing estimated MSE (Imbens--Kalyanaraman; the
        Calonico--Cattaneo--Titiunik version in \texttt{rdrobust}).
  \item \alert{The MSE-optimal bandwidth leaves a bias that invalidates the
        conventional interval.} Use the CCT \emph{robust bias-corrected}
        interval, which \texttt{rdrobust} reports by default.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The MSE-Optimal Bandwidth}
Put the two previous frames together:
\begin{align*}
  \text{MSE}(h) &= B^2 h^4 + \frac{V}{nh}
   && \text{\footnotesize bias$^2$ + variance} \\
  0 &= 4B^2h^3 - \frac{V}{nh^2}
   && \text{\footnotesize derivative in $h$ set to zero} \\
  h^{*} &= \left(\frac{V}{4B^2}\right)^{1/5} n^{-1/5}
   && \text{\footnotesize solve}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Wider} when the noise $V$ is large or the density $f(c)$ is low;
        \textbf{narrower} when the curves bend sharply at $c$ ($B$ large).
  \item $B$ and $V$ are unknown: a pilot fit estimates $m''$, $\sigma^2$, and
        $f(c)$ and plugs them in \srcp{Imbens and Kalyanaraman 2012; Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik 2014}.
  \item $h^{*}$ shrinks slowly: ten times the data narrows it by $10^{1/5} = 1.6$.
  \item Senate: $\hat h^{*} = 17.75$ points on both sides.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the Optimal Bandwidth Breaks the Confidence Interval\adv}
At $h^{*}$, the first-order condition fixes the ratio of bias to standard error:
\begin{align*}
  4B^2h^{*5} = \frac{V}{n}
  \ \ \Longrightarrow\ \
  \underbrace{B^2 h^{*4}}_{\text{bias}^2} = \frac14\,\underbrace{\frac{V}{n h^{*}}}_{\text{variance}}
  \ \ \Longrightarrow\ \
  |\text{bias}| = \tfrac12\,\text{se} .
  && \text{\footnotesize divide by $4h^{*}$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The bias does not vanish relative to the standard error as $n$
        grows: both shrink like $n^{-2/5}$.
  \item $\hat\tau \pm 1.96\,\widehat{\text{se}}$ is centred half a standard error
        off: coverage $\Pr(|N(\tfrac12, 1)| \leq 1.96) = 0.921$, lower when $h$ is
        estimated. Covering 95\% needs 2.18, not 1.96 \srcp{Armstrong and Kolesár 2020}.
  \item The best $h$ for a \emph{point estimate} is not the best $h$ for an \emph{interval}.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
\textbf{Repairs}: a smaller $h$ (undersmoothing); subtract an estimate of the
bias (robust bias correction); or bound the bias and widen by the bound (honest intervals).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Robust Bias Correction}
\srct{Calonico, Cattaneo, and Titiunik (2014)}:
\vskip-9pt
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau^{\,bc} &= \hat\tau_h - \hat B\,h^2
   && \text{\footnotesize $\hat B$: local quadratic, pilot bandwidth $b$} \\
  \Var(\hat\tau^{\,bc}) &= \Var(\hat\tau_h) + h^4\Var(\hat B) - 2h^2\Cov(\hat\tau_h,\hat B)
   && \text{\footnotesize $\hat B$ adds noise}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The \alert{robust} interval centres on $\hat\tau^{\,bc}$ and uses the second line;
        ``bias-corrected'' keeps the old standard error and undercovers.
  \item \textbf{Identity}: with $b = h$, $\hat\tau^{\,bc}$ and its robust s.e. are the local
        quadratic estimate at $h$ and its s.e.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Honest Intervals \srcp{Armstrong and Kolesár 2018, 2020}}
Assume a bound on curvature, $|m_d''(x)| \leq M$ near $c$. For a given $h$ the
estimator's weights are known, so its \alert{largest possible bias} $\bar b$ is
computable. Then
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau \pm \text{cv}_{0.95}\!\big(\bar b/\widehat{\text{se}}\big)\cdot\widehat{\text{se}},
  \qquad
  \text{cv}_{0.95}(t) = \text{the 95th percentile of } |N(t,1)| .
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Coverage is at least 95\% for every curve with $|m''| \leq M$: the
        interval is widened by exactly enough to absorb the worst bias.
  \item $h$ is chosen to make the interval shortest; nothing is bias-corrected.
  \item The price is choosing $M$. A rule of thumb uses a global quartic fit;
        report how the interval changes with $M$.
  \item The same logic handles a \textbf{discrete running variable}, where
        continuity-based asymptotics do not apply \srcp{Kolesár and Rothe 2018}.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
Senate (\texttt{RDHonest}, $\hat M = 0.114$): $h = 9.84$, estimate 8.03, s.e.
1.85, $\bar b = 1.13$, $\text{cv} = 2.27$, interval [3.82, 12.24].
\end{frame}

weights $=$ row 1 of $(\bm{X}'\bm{W}\bm{X})^{-1}\bm{X}'\bm{W}$.

\begin{frame}{Adding Covariates \srcp{Calonico, Cattaneo, Farrell, and Titiunik 2019}}
Add pre-determined $Z_i$ (lagged vote, say) to the local regression, additively,
same kernel and bandwidth. By the omitted-variable formula (Week 1):
\begin{align*}
  \tilde\tau = \hat\tau_Y - \hat\tau_Z'\,\hat\gamma
   && \text{\footnotesize exact, for weighted least squares}
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
{\small $\hat\tau_Y$: RD estimate for $Y$;\ \ $\hat\tau_Z$: RD estimates with each $Z$
as the outcome;\ \ $\hat\gamma$: coefficients on $Z$ in the regression that includes $Z$.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item If $Z$ is continuous at $c$, $\tau_Z = 0$ and $\tilde\tau$ estimates the
        \alert{same} $\tau_{RD}$; the gain is precision when $Z$ predicts $Y$.
  \item If $\tilde\tau$ moves far from $\hat\tau_Y$, then $\hat\tau_Z$ is
        large: a covariate jumps at the cutoff. \alert{That is a failed balance
        test, not an improvement.}
  \item Do not interact $Z$ with $D_i$; never use post-treatment $Z$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Flexible Covariate Adjustment}
\textbf{Goal}: more precision from many covariates without adding bias.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Linear adjustment is one choice of a function $\eta(Z_i)$ subtracted
        from $Y_i$ before the RD:
        \begin{align*}
          \tau_{RD} = \lim_{x\downarrow c}\E[Y_i - \eta(Z_i)\mid X_i=x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c}\E[Y_i - \eta(Z_i)\mid X_i=x],
        \end{align*}
        true for \emph{any} $\eta$ when $Z$ is continuous at $c$: the
        $\eta$ terms cancel at the cutoff.
  \item So $\eta$ can be estimated by machine learning, on held-out folds,
        to predict $Y$ as well as possible; the estimand does not change
        \srcp{Noack, Olma, and Rothe 2026}.
  \item The precision gain is largest when $Z$ explains much of $Y$ near
        the cutoff, such as a lagged outcome.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize Week 13 returns to cross-fitting.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why RD Needs Several Times the Data of an Experiment}
Compare $\Var(\hat\tau)$ in RD with a coin-flip experiment, same $n$, same
$\sigma^2$, model $Y_i = \alpha + \tau D_i + \beta X_i + \epsilon_i$:
\begin{align*}
  \Var(\hat\tau_{RD}) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{n\Var(D)\,(1-\rho^2_{DX})}
   && \text{\footnotesize FWL: only the part of $D$ not explained by $X$ counts} \\
  \Var(\hat\tau_{\text{exp}}) &= \frac{\sigma^2}{n\Var(D)}
   && \text{\footnotesize $D$ independent of $X$} \\
  \text{ratio} &= \frac{1}{1-\rho^2_{DX}}
   && \text{\footnotesize $\rho_{DX} = \text{corr}(\mathds 1\{X\geq c\}, X)$}
\end{align*}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Covariates, Power, and What to Report}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Covariates are for precision only.} They must be
        \emph{pre-determined}, they should enter additively (not interacted with
        $D$), and the estimate should barely move when you add them. If it moves
        a lot, the design is in trouble, not improved
        \srcp{Calonico et al.\ 2019}.
  \item \textbf{Effective sample size}: report the number of observations
        \emph{inside the bandwidth} on each side. An RD with $n = 100{,}000$ often
        has 400 observations doing the work, and the standard errors should be
        read accordingly.
  \item \textbf{Power}: RD is data-hungry (previous frame); plan with
        \texttt{rdpower} \srcp{Cattaneo, Titiunik, and Vazquez-Bare 2019}.
  \item \textbf{Discrete running variables and heaping}: rounded ages, coarse
        test-score grids, or income bunched at round numbers do not invalidate the
        design, but they do invalidate the continuous-$X$ asymptotics behind
        bandwidth selection and bias correction. Use a discrete-score method and
        say so.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Threat: Manipulation\core}
Continuity fails if units can \emph{precisely} control which side of the cutoff
they land on, retaking a test until you pass, reporting income just under a
threshold, a firm holding hiring just below a regulatory headcount.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \alert{Imprecise control is fine.} \srct{Lee (2008)}'s insight is that if
        agents cannot control $X$ to the last decimal, assignment near the cutoff
        is as good as random even though agents influence $X$.
  \item \textbf{Density test} \srcp{McCrary 2008; Cattaneo, Jansson, and Ma 2020}:
        is the density of $X$ continuous at $c$? A spike on the advantaged side is
        direct evidence of sorting.
  \item \textbf{Covariate smoothness}: run the RD with \emph{pre-determined
        covariates} as outcomes. They should show no jump. This is the single
        most persuasive check, and it is the RD analogue of a balance table.
  \item \textbf{Donut hole}: drop observations immediately around $c$, where
        manipulation would concentrate, and see whether the estimate survives.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Sorting Does}
Simulation: true effect of crossing $= 0$; 88 of the units landing in $[-2, 0)$,
those with high unobserved $u$, move to $[0, 2)$ \srcp{figs/w9\_sims.R}.
\vskip-2pt

\end{center}

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item RD estimate (truth 0): 0.04 without sorting, \alert{2.89} with it.
  \item Density test $p$-value: 0.071 without sorting, 0.00001 with it.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
{\footnotesize The movers bring their high $u$ with them: $m_0$ now jumps at $c$, and the
density shows the hole and the spike that produced it.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Density Test, and the Senate}
\textbf{The test.} Estimate the density of $X$ at $c$ separately from each side
and compare:
\begin{align*}
  T = \frac{\hat f_+(c) - \hat f_-(c)}{\widehat{\text{se}}} \ \approx\ N(0,1)
  \ \text{ under no sorting.}
\end{align*}
\srct{McCrary (2008)} bins $X$ and runs local linear regressions on the bin
counts; \srct{Cattaneo, Jansson, and Ma (2020)} fit a local polynomial to the
empirical distribution function, with no bins (\texttt{rddensity}).
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Senate}: $T = -0.76$, $p = 0.45$; 49 elections lost by less than 2
points, 47 won by less than 2 \srcp{figs/w9\_senate.R}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item A pass is weak evidence: sorting in \emph{both} directions leaves the
        density smooth while changing who sits on each side.
  \item A failure is strong evidence, unless the spike has a mechanical cause
        such as rounding (next frames).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Close Elections Under Scrutiny}
\textbf{The challenge} \srcp{Caughey and Sekhon 2011}: in U.S.\ House races
1942--2008 decided by less than 0.5 points, the party already holding the seat
won 59 of 85 (69\%). Bare winners also outspent their opponents more often.
Imbalance \emph{grew} closer to the cutoff, the opposite of what Lee's argument
predicts.
\vskip4pt
\textbf{The reply} \srcp{Eggers, Fowler, Hainmueller, Hall, and Snyder 2015}:
over 40,000 close races (other House periods, statewide, state-legislative,
mayoral, and nine other countries), the incumbent-party advantage in bare wins
\alert{appears nowhere else}.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Precise control is possible where recounts, lawyers, and
        certification are contested; such places need balance checks that
        go beyond the density.
  \item The lesson is not ``close elections are fine'' or ``are not'': the
        design is checked application by application, and the checks have
        found failures.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heaping: a Cautionary Tale}
\textbf{The design} \srcp{Almond, Doyle, Kowalski, and Williams 2010}: newborns
under 1,500 g are classified ``very low birth weight'' and get more care.

\alert{heap} at round numbers (1,500 g; whole ounces), and heaped births are
disproportionately from poorer, less healthy pregnancies. The heap sits exactly
at the cutoff, on the treated side.

\begin{frame}{When Sorting Is Present: Bounds \srcp{Gerard, Rokkanen, and Rothe 2020}}
Suppose sorting only pushes units \emph{up} past $c$. The density then has an
excess just above $c$, and the excess is the movers:
\begin{align*}
  \pi = \frac{f_+(c) - f_-(c)}{f_+(c)} = 1 - \frac{f_-(c)}{f_+(c)}
   && \text{\footnotesize share of units just above $c$ who moved there}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The non-movers just above $c$ are comparable to the units just below.
        We do not know \emph{which} units above are movers, only how many.
  \item \textbf{Worst cases}: drop the top $\pi$ share of outcomes above $c$,
        or the bottom $\pi$ share. The two trimmed means bound
        $\E[Y_i(1)\mid X_i = c, \text{non-mover}]$, and subtracting the mean
        just below bounds the effect.
  \item The trimming logic of bounds for sample attrition \srcp{Lee 2009}. The
        bounds widen with $\pi$; a density test that fails by a little costs a little.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Other Checks and Common Errors}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Placebo cutoffs}: estimate at fake thresholds away from $c$, using
        only data on one side so the real discontinuity is not picked up.
        Significant ``effects'' there mean your procedure finds jumps everywhere.
  \item \textbf{Bandwidth sensitivity}: plot $\hat\tau$ against $h$ over a wide
        range with robust intervals. A result that exists only at one bandwidth is
        not a result.
  \item \alert{Do not use a high-order global polynomial}
        \srcp{Gelman and Imbens 2019}. The implied weights on distant
        observations are large, oscillating, and indefensible; estimates wander
        with the degree.
  \item \alert{Do not control for post-treatment covariates}, and do not interact
        covariates with $D$ unless you mean to estimate heterogeneity.
  \item \alert{Do not report only the conventional interval} at an MSE-optimal
        bandwidth.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Second Framework \srcp{Cattaneo, Frandsen, and Titiunik 2015}}
\textbf{Goal}: treat a small window around $c$ as an experiment, and analyse
it as one.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Assumption}: there is a window $W_0 = [c - w,\, c + w]$ in which
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item which units fall above $c$ is as if decided by a coin flip, and
  \item $Y_i(d)$ does not depend on $X_i$ inside the window.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Then the difference in means inside $W_0$ estimates the effect, and a
        \textbf{Fisher randomization test} gives exact $p$-values:
        reshuffle who is above $c$ among the window's units, recompute, compare.
  \item \textbf{Continuity} extrapolates two curves to one point; \textbf{local
        randomization} assumes a whole window is an experiment. Condition 2 is
        stronger, and it is why $w$ must be small \srcp{Sekhon and Titiunik 2017}.
  \item Natural when $X$ is discrete with few values near $c$ (ranks, ages
        in years), where curve fitting has little to work with.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Choosing the Window, Then Testing}
\textbf{Window}: in nested windows, test balance on pre-determined covariates;
take the largest window in which the smallest $p$-value stays above 0.15
(\texttt{rdlocrand::rdwinselect}). Senate, nine covariates \srcp{figs/w9\_senate.R}:
\vskip2pt

\end{frame}

\section[Fuzzy]{Fuzzy RD}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When the Rule Is Not Followed\core}
Often crossing the cutoff changes the \emph{probability} of treatment rather than
determining it: eligibility is not take-up.
\begin{align*}
  \lim_{x\downarrow c}\Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i=x) - \lim_{x\uparrow c}\Pr(D_i=1\mid X_i=x)
  \ \in\ (0,1) .
\end{align*}
The fuzzy RD estimator is a ratio of two sharp RD estimates:
\begin{align*}
  \tau_{FRD} = \frac{\text{jump in } Y \text{ at } c}{\text{jump in } D \text{ at } c}
  = \frac{\lim_{x\downarrow c}\E[Y\mid x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c}\E[Y\mid x]}
         {\lim_{x\downarrow c}\E[D\mid x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c}\E[D\mid x]} .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \alert{This is exactly the Wald estimator of Week 7}, with
        $\mathds 1\{X\geq c\}$ as the instrument, estimated locally.
  \item The numerator alone is the \textbf{intention-to-treat} effect of crossing
        the cutoff, and it is worth reporting on its own, it needs no
        first-stage assumption.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the Ratio Estimates}
Let $D_i(1)$, $D_i(0)$ be unit $i$'s treatment just above and just below $c$
(compliers: $D_i(1)=1$, $D_i(0)=0$). As in Week 7:
\begin{align*}
  \text{jump in } Y
  &= \E\big[Y_i(D_i(1)) - Y_i(D_i(0))\mid X_i = c\big]
   && \text{\footnotesize continuity, for each type} \\
  &= \E\big[(D_i(1) - D_i(0))\,(Y_i(1) - Y_i(0))\mid X_i = c\big]
   && \text{\footnotesize zero unless $D_i(1) \neq D_i(0)$} \\
  &= \Pr(C\mid c)\,\tau_C(c) - \Pr(\text{defier}\mid c)\,\tau_{\text{def}}(c)
   && \text{\footnotesize split by type} \\
  &= \Pr(C\mid c)\,\tau_C(c)
   && \text{\footnotesize monotonicity: no defiers} \\
  \text{jump in } D &= \Pr(C\mid c)
   && \text{\footnotesize same steps with $D$}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The ratio is $\tau_C(c)$: the effect for \alert{compliers at the cutoff}
        \srcp{Hahn, Todd, and van der Klaauw 2001}.
  \item ``Continuity for each type'' is the new assumption: the share of each
        type and its mean outcomes do not jump at $c$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimation: Local 2SLS}
Run the sharp RD twice, same kernel and same $h$, and divide:
\begin{align*}
  Y_i &= \alpha_Y + \beta_Y Z_i + \gamma_Y (X_i - c) + \eta_Y Z_i (X_i - c) + \epsilon_i \\
  D_i &= \alpha_D + \beta_D Z_i + \gamma_D (X_i - c) + \eta_D Z_i (X_i - c) + v_i,
  \qquad \hat\tau = \hat\beta_Y / \hat\beta_D ,
\end{align*}
with $Z_i = \mathds 1\{X_i \geq c\}$. Numerically the same: 2SLS of $Y$ on $D_i$
and $D_i(X_i - c)$, instrumented by $Z_i$ and $Z_i(X_i - c)$, controlling for $(X_i - c)$.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item One bandwidth for both regressions; \texttt{rdrobust(y, x, fuzzy = d)}
        chooses it for the ratio.
  \item The standard error is the IV standard error of Week 7, computed locally.
  \item Report $\hat\beta_D$, the first stage, in the main table.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fuzzy RD Inherits Everything From IV\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Exclusion}: crossing the cutoff must affect $Y$ only through $D$.
        If the threshold also triggers something else, a different school, a
        different caseworker, a label, exclusion fails.
  \item \textbf{Monotonicity}: no one is treated \emph{because} they fell below
        the cutoff.
  \item \textbf{The estimand is doubly local}: the effect for \alert{compliers at
        the cutoff}. Not the ATE, not the ATT, and not the effect for everyone
        near the cutoff.
  \item \textbf{Weak first stage} is a real risk: if take-up jumps by only a few
        percentage points, you are dividing by a small noisy number and all the
        Week 7 pathologies return. Report the size of the jump in $D$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Weak First Stage: Anderson--Rubin at the Cutoff}
The Week 7 idea carries over. To test $H_0: \tau = \tau_0$, build
\begin{align*}
  \tilde Y_i = Y_i - \tau_0 D_i ,
  \qquad
  \text{jump in } \tilde Y = \text{jump in } Y - \tau_0\cdot\text{jump in } D ,
\end{align*}
which is zero exactly when $\tau_0$ is the ratio. Run a \emph{sharp} RD on
$\tilde Y_i$; keep every $\tau_0$ it does not reject.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item No division, so a small jump in $D$ does not distort the test; the set
        can be wide or unbounded, and then it should be.
  \item \srct{Feir, Lemieux, and Marmer (2016)}: the usual fuzzy-RD $t$-test
        over-rejects when the jump in take-up is small; invert a test with
        the null-restricted standard error instead.
  \item \srct{Noack and Rothe (2024)}: the same construction with the bias of each
        sharp RD bounded as in honest intervals; valid with weak first stages,
        discrete running variables, and donuts.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Dell and Querubín (2018)}
\textbf{The question.} Does force from above win hearts and minds? The effect of
U.S.\ bombing on insurgency, local government, and civic life in South Vietnam,
and of the Army's firepower doctrine against the Marines' ``hearts and minds''.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} The Hamlet Evaluation System (HES), 1969--1973: 169 monthly
and quarterly questions on security, politics, and the economy, answered by
U.S.\ advisers for nearly every hamlet. Outcomes: indices built from HES
answers, armed-forces records of operations and attacks, and public opinion
surveys.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Two designs.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Bombing}: a fuzzy RD at the rounding thresholds of an algorithm
        that scored hamlet security; air strikes were targeted on the rounded score.
  \item \textbf{Doctrine}: a boundary RD at the line between the Marines' I Corps
        and the Army's II Corps.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
{\footnotesize Numbers in this section are from NBER Working Paper 22395 (2016);
the QJE version is \emph{QJE} 133(2): 701--764.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Score Nobody Saw}
\small
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item The HES questions feed 19 submodels. Each starts from a flat prior over
        five security classes, A (5) to E (1), and updates by Bayes' rule using
        a matrix of conditional probabilities.
  \item The \alert{posterior mean}, a continuous number between 1 and 5, is
        \alert{rounded}: 3.49 becomes C, 3.51 becomes B. Rounded submodel scores
        are combined into one letter grade per hamlet.
  \item Air strikes were planned weekly by quota, with the hamlet grade among
        the inputs. Just below a rounding threshold, a hamlet is graded one letter
        worse than a near-identical one just above.
  \item The continuous scores were never printed; the conditional probabilities
        were classified. The authors rebuilt them from archived tapes and
        reproduce every rounded score.
\end{itemize}
\vskip1pt
\textbf{Running variable}: distance from the continuous score to the nearest of
four thresholds (A/B, B/C, C/D, D/E); \textbf{instrument}: being below it.
Hamlets near each threshold: 35\%, 46\%, 16\%, 3\%.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dell and Querubín: the Estimating Equation}
For hamlet $h$ first near a threshold in quarter $t$ (each hamlet once):
\begin{align*}
  y_{h,t+n} = \gamma\,\text{below}_{ht}
  &+ \sum_{d=1}^{4} \Big(\delta_d D_{htd} + D_{htd}\,f_d(\text{dist}_{ht})
  + D_{htd}\,f_d(\text{dist}_{ht})\,\text{below}_{ht}\Big) \\
  &+ \alpha_t + X_{ht}'\beta + \epsilon_{ht}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $D_{htd} = 1$ if threshold $d$ is the nearest: a separate intercept and
        separate local linear slopes on each side of each threshold. This is
        the multi-cutoff pooling of the next section.
  \item $\alpha_t$: quarter fixed effects; $X_{ht}$: indicators for every
        answer that entered the score, for precision.
  \item Local linear, Imbens--Kalyanaraman bandwidth; standard errors clustered
        by village.
  \item First stage: $y$ = bombing; second stage instruments bombing with
        $\text{below}_{ht}$. The estimate is a LATE for hamlets bombed
        \emph{because} they were rounded down.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Extensions]{Extensions}

\begin{frame}{Several Cutoffs: What Pooling Estimates}
Group $j$ faces cutoff $c_j$ (with probability $p_j$). Pool by centring,
$\tilde X_i = X_i - c_{J_i}$, and run one RD at $\tilde X = 0$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{jump at } \tilde X = 0
  &= \sum_j \Pr(J_i = j \mid \tilde X_i = 0)\,\tau_j
   && \text{\footnotesize group jumps, mixed} \\
  \Pr(J_i = j\mid\tilde X_i = 0) &= \frac{f_j(c_j)\,p_j}{\sum_k f_k(c_k)\,p_k}
   && \text{\footnotesize Bayes' rule; $f_j$: density in group $j$}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The pooled effect is a weighted average of cutoff-specific effects,
        weights $\propto$ how many units sit at each cutoff
        \srcp{Cattaneo, Keele, Titiunik, and Vazquez-Bare 2016}.
  \item In Dell and Querubín, 46\% of hamlets sit near the B/C threshold and 3\%
        near D/E: the pooled estimate is mostly about mid-grade hamlets.
  \item Report the cutoff-specific estimates when you can; they show whether
        one $\tau$ is a fair summary.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Away From the Cutoff}
\textbf{Goal}: $\tau(x)$ for $x \neq c$, which continuity alone cannot deliver.
\vskip2pt
\textbf{With two cutoffs} $c_1 < c_2$, at $x \in (c_1, c_2)$ group 1 is treated
and group 2 is not. Assume the two control curves are parallel:
$m_{0,1}(x) - m_{0,2}(x) = \Delta$ for all $x$.
\begin{align*}
  \Delta &= m_{0,1}(c_1) - m_{0,2}(c_1)
   && \text{\footnotesize both observed untreated at $c_1$ (group 1 from the left)} \\
  \tau_1(x) &= m_{1,1}(x) - \big(m_{0,2}(x) + \Delta\big)
   && \text{\footnotesize the unseen $m_{0,1}(x)$ replaced}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
{\footnotesize \srcp{Cattaneo, Keele, Titiunik, and Vazquez-Bare 2021}; the assumption is Week 10's parallel trends, with $x$ for time.}
\vskip4pt
\textbf{Other routes}:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Conditional independence}: given other predictors $W$, $Y(d)$
        does not depend on $X$; then $W$, not $X$, does the work away from $c$,
        and the assumption is testable on each side \srcp{Angrist and Rokkanen 2015}.
  \item \textbf{The slope of the effect at $c$}, $\tau'(c)$, is identified from
        the change in slopes; it answers ``what if the threshold moved a
        little?'' \srcp{Dong and Lewbel 2015}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Boundaries and Two Scores}
\textbf{Geographic RD}: the running variable is location $(\text{lat}, \text{lon})$;
the cutoff is a line \srcp{Keele and Titiunik 2015}.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The estimand is $\tau(b)$ at each boundary point $b$; one number pools
        them, weighted by where units live along the line.
  \item \textbf{Distance to the boundary} as a single running variable mixes
        points that are close to the line but far from each other; boundary
        segment fixed effects, as in Dell and Querubín, compare neighbours.
  \item Recent work estimates $\tau(b)$ point by point, with location-based or
        distance-based local polynomials and valid intervals
        \srcp{Cattaneo, Titiunik, and Yu 2025; \texttt{rd2d}}.
  \item \alert{Compound treatment is the rule}: borders separate taxes,
        schools, police, and history at once. Name the bundle.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Two scores} (pass both a reading and a math test): the treated region is
a corner; each edge of the boundary has its own effect
\srcp{Papay, Willett, and Murnane 2011; Reardon and Robinson 2012}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Regression Kink \srcp{Card, Lee, Pei, and Weber 2015}}
A benefit $B_i = b(X_i)$ follows a known schedule that is continuous but
\emph{changes slope} at $c$ (a benefit cap, a tax bracket). With $Y_i = y(B_i, X_i, U_i)$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{d}{dx}\E[Y_i\mid X_i = x]
  &= \E\Big[\frac{\partial y}{\partial B}\Big|\, x\Big]\, b'(x)
   + \text{smooth terms}
   && \text{\footnotesize chain rule} \\
  \underbrace{\lim_{x\downarrow c}\tfrac{d}{dx}\E[Y\mid x] - \lim_{x\uparrow c}\tfrac{d}{dx}\E[Y\mid x]}_{\text{kink in } Y}
  &= \E\Big[\frac{\partial y}{\partial B}\Big|\, c\Big]\,
     \underbrace{\big(b'(c^+) - b'(c^-)\big)}_{\text{kink in } B}
   && \text{\footnotesize subtract}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Effect $=$ kink in $Y$ / kink in $B$: the RD ratio with slopes for levels.
  \item Slopes are estimated far less precisely than levels; local quadratic
        fits and robust intervals are standard.
  \item Austrian unemployment benefits: higher benefits lengthen joblessness.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RD in Time}
The running variable is the date; the treatment starts at $t^{*}$ for everyone
\srcp{Hausman and Rapson 2018}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{No one sorts into a date}, but other things happen on dates:
        holidays, seasons, a second event. Check for coincident events.
  \item \textbf{Observations are not independent}: one time series,
        autocorrelated; inference must account for it.
  \item \textbf{Windows tend to be wide}: daily data with few days near
        $t^{*}$ invite long windows, and then the RD is an interrupted time
        series with a polynomial trend.
  \item \textbf{Effects evolve}: anticipation before $t^{*}$, fading or
        building after. The jump at $t^{*}$ is the short-run effect only.
  \item \textbf{Placebo dates} and a comparison group (Week 10) are the main defences.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Repeated Cutoffs: Dynamic RD}
\textbf{Goal}: the effect of a treatment $k$ years later when units that barely
miss can try again (a district loses a bond vote, proposes again, passes).
\vskip2pt
The RD of ``barely won at $t$'' on $Y_{t+k}$ is an intention-to-treat effect,
$\text{ITT}_k$. Let $\pi_j$ be the RD jump in the chance of a bond at $t+j$
($\pi_0 = 1$) and $\theta_k$ the effect of a bond $k$ years after it passes:
\begin{align*}
  \text{ITT}_k &= \sum_{j=0}^{k} \pi_j\,\theta_{k-j}
   && \text{\footnotesize every bond induced at $t+j$ acts for $k-j$ years} \\
  \theta_k &= \text{ITT}_k - \sum_{j=1}^{k} \pi_j\,\theta_{k-j}
   && \text{\footnotesize solve from $\theta_0 = \text{ITT}_0$ upward}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
{\small Hypothetical numbers: $\text{ITT}_0 = 10$, $\text{ITT}_1 = 12$, $\pi_1 = -0.4$ (losers
pass a year later): $\theta_1 = 12 - (-0.4)(10) = 16$. The ITT understates the bond's effect.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dynamic RD: Applications and Assumptions}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item California school bonds and house prices \srcp{Cellini, Ferreira, and Rothstein 2010};
        Wisconsin operating vs.\ capital referenda \srcp{Baron 2022}.
  \item Assumes $\theta_k$ does not depend on a unit's history; \srct{Hsu and Shen (2024)}
        identify the effects under weaker assumptions.
  \item Cumulative effect: $\sum_k \theta_k$. The ITT path is the effect of a close win,
        retries included; Bol et al.'s \texteuro180,000 below is of that kind.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Bol, de Vaan, and van de Rijt (2018)}
\textbf{The question.} Merton's Matthew effect: does early success cause later
success, or do repeated winners just have more ability? Cumulative advantage
is a cornerstone claim of stratification research and hard to separate from
selection.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} The Dutch science foundation's early-career ``Veni'' grant
(\texteuro250,000): review scores and decisions for recent PhDs, followed for
up to 16 years.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The design.} A hard threshold: every winner scored above every
non-winner. Running variable: the applicant's \alert{rank} relative to the
funding line in each competition; compare the two ranks just above (+1, +2)
with the two just below ($-1$, $-2$), with exact tests. A sharp RD with a
discrete running variable, analysed as a local experiment.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{Outcomes.} Later grants (the mid-career ``Vidi'' and three others),
applications for them, publications and citations, a full professorship.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reading and Reporting an RD\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{The picture first.} Binned means of $Y$ against $X$, with the
        fitted local polynomials and the cutoff. If the jump is not visible, be
        sceptical of the number.
  \item The bandwidth, how it was chosen, and the effective $n$ on each side.
  \item Robust bias-corrected confidence intervals.
  \item The density test and the covariate-smoothness checks.
  \item A bandwidth-sensitivity plot and placebo cutoffs.
  \item For fuzzy designs, the size of the jump in treatment take-up, and the
        intention-to-treat effect alongside.
  \item An explicit statement that the estimand is local to the cutoff.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking Ahead}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item RD buys extraordinary credibility at the cost of a very local estimand.
        The identifying variation is visible in a scatterplot, and the main
        threats (sorting, bandwidth dependence) are checkable.
  \item Note the family resemblance: sharp RD is a local experiment; fuzzy RD is
        local IV. The tools recombine rather than multiply.
  \item \textbf{Next week}: designs that use variation over \emph{time} rather
        than across a threshold.
  \item Note again how the estimand was chosen by the design rather than by us.
        Saying so plainly is part of reporting an RD honestly.
\end{itemize}
