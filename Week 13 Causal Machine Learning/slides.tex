\begin{frame}{Who Gains Most From College?}
Brand and Xie (2010, Week 5) asked whether the returns to college are larger for those
most or least likely to go. Two theories predict opposite answers:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Positive selection}: those most likely to go gain most (they sort on gains).
  \item \textbf{Negative selection}: those least likely to go gain most (they have the fewest
        other routes out of low-wage work).
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
What answering it takes:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Not one average effect, but the effect for each \emph{kind} of youth.
  \item ``Kind'' is described by many pre-college variables: parents' income and schooling,
        siblings, ability, race, expectations, school, \dots
  \item Nobody knows in advance which of them, or which combinations, matter.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
{\small Brand, Xu, Koch, and Geraldo (2021) answer it with a causal forest on the NLSY79.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From One Effect to Many\core}
Each person has two potential outcomes; their difference is the person's effect (Week 5):
\begin{align*}
  \tau_i &= Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)
   && \text{\footnotesize never observed for anyone} \\
  \tau(x) &= \E\big[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid X_i = x\big]
   && \text{\footnotesize the CATE: average effect among people with $X_i = x$} \\
  \text{ATE} &= \E\big[\tau(X_i)\big]
   && \text{\footnotesize average the CATEs over everyone}
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The college question is about $\tau(x)$: is the effect larger when $x$ describes a
        disadvantaged youth?
  \item Identification is Week 6's: if $D_i$ is as good as random given $X_i$, then
        within each value of $x$,
  \[
    \tau(x) = \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1, X_i = x] - \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0, X_i = x] = m_1(x) - m_0(x).
  \]
  \item The problem is estimation: with 20 or more covariates, almost every $x$ is held by one
        person. Week 6's cells run out.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Old Way: Subgroups Chosen by the Researcher}
Split the sample by a variable you chose (mother's schooling, say) and estimate the
effect in each part.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item Transparent, and fine for one or two variables chosen \emph{before} looking.
  \item \textbf{Which variables?} 22 covariates and their combinations: mother's schooling
        \emph{and} siblings \emph{and} ability. Two values each already make $2^{22} = 4$
        million cells.
  \item \textbf{Forking paths}: try twenty splits and report the one that ``works.'' With no
        true differences, each test is significant 5\% of the time; at least one of twenty, 64\%.
  \item \textbf{Goal}: let the data choose the splits, then estimate each subgroup's effect
        so that the choosing does not contaminate the estimate.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Recap: Week 12's Tree Cuts the Squared Error\core}
A node $P$ ($n$ people, mean $\bar Y_P$) is split into a left child $L$ ($n_L$, $\bar Y_L$) and a right child $R$ ($n_R$, $\bar Y_R$).
The tree keeps the split with the largest drop in the sum of squared errors. For any group,
\begin{align*}
  \text{SSE} = \textstyle\sum_i (Y_i - \bar Y)^2 &= \textstyle\sum_i Y_i^2 - n\,\bar Y^2
   && \text{\footnotesize expand the square; $\textstyle\sum_i Y_i = n\bar Y$}
\end{align*}
Subtract the children's SSE from the parent's:
\begin{align*}
  \text{SSE}_P - \text{SSE}_L - \text{SSE}_R
  = \big(\textstyle\sum_{P} Y_i^2 - n\bar Y_P^2\big) - \big(\textstyle\sum_{L} Y_i^2 - n_L\bar Y_L^2\big)
     - \big(\textstyle\sum_{R} Y_i^2 - n_R\bar Y_R^2\big)
\end{align*}
\vskip-14pt
\begin{align*}
  &= n_L\bar Y_L^2 + n_R\bar Y_R^2 - n\,\bar Y_P^2
   && \text{\footnotesize same people: $\textstyle\sum_P = \sum_L + \sum_R$} \\
  &= n_L(\bar Y_L - \bar Y_P)^2 + n_R(\bar Y_R - \bar Y_P)^2
   && \text{\footnotesize $n\bar Y_P = n_L\bar Y_L + n_R\bar Y_R$}
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The tree picks the split whose children's means sit \emph{farthest from the parent's mean}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Tree for Effects: Put $\hat\tau$ Where $\bar Y$ Was\core}
Now the target is the effect. $\tau_i$ is never seen, but a group's average effect can be estimated by
comparing its treated and untreated members:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau_\ell = \bar Y_{\ell,\,D=1} - \bar Y_{\ell,\,D=0}
  \qquad \text{(difference in means among the people in leaf $\ell$)}
\end{align*}
For now, assume $D$ is as good as random inside each leaf (relaxed after the example). In the drop in SSE, put $\hat\tau$
where $\bar Y$ was:
\begin{align*}
  \Delta
  &= n_L\,(\hat\tau_L - \hat\tau_P)^2 + n_R\,(\hat\tau_R - \hat\tau_P)^2
   && \text{\footnotesize larger $\Delta$: more different effects} \\
  &\approx \tfrac{n_L\,n_R}{n}\,(\hat\tau_L - \hat\tau_R)^2
   && \text{\footnotesize plug in $\hat\tau_P \approx (n_L\hat\tau_L + n_R\hat\tau_R)/n$}
\end{align*}
{\scriptsize ($\approx$: exact when both children have the parent's share treated.)}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item A \textbf{causal tree} \srcp{Athey and Imbens 2016} keeps the split with the largest $\Delta$. Children with
        the parent's effect: $\Delta = 0$.
  \item A tiny child's $\hat\tau$ is noisy and can sit far from $\hat\tau_P$ by chance, so each child must keep a
        minimum number of treated and untreated people (\texttt{grf}: \texttt{min.node.size}).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Before the Tree: Control for $X$\core}
The split by hand compared graduates and non-graduates as if $D$ were random. It is not: a difference
in means is OLS of $Y$ on $D$ without $X$, confounded as in Week 1.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The fix}: control for $X$ \emph{before} growing any tree \srcp{Athey, Tibshirani, and Wager 2019}.
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item Two ordinary regression forests (Week 12) predict $\hat m(x) \approx \E[Y\mid X = x]$ and
        $\hat e(x) \approx \E[D\mid X = x]$; each person is predicted by trees that did not draw them.
  \item Residualize once, for everyone, never again inside a leaf:
        $\tilde Y_i = Y_i - \hat m(X_i)$, $\ \tilde D_i = D_i - \hat e(X_i)$.
  \item Wherever a $\hat\tau$ appears, in the splitting rule $\Delta$ and in every leaf, use the residual-on-residual
        slope in place of the difference in means:
        \[ \hat\tau_\ell = \frac{\sum_{i\in\ell}\tilde D_i\,\tilde Y_i}{\sum_{i\in\ell}\tilde D_i^2} . \]
\end{enumerate}
\vskip-2pt
{\small Week 1's FWL: this slope is the coefficient on $D$ controlling for $X$. So every $\hat\tau$ from here on is
estimated holding $X$ fixed, with forests doing the controlling. Section 2 calls this DML.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Growing the Whole Tree\core}
Start with everyone in one node. At every node:
\vskip2pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item For \textbf{every} covariate $X_j$ and \textbf{every} cut $s$ between its observed values: send
        $X_{ij} \leq s$ left and the rest right; compute $\hat\tau_L$ and $\hat\tau_R$ (residual slopes)
        and $\Delta(j, s)$.
  \item Keep the $(j, s)$ with the largest $\Delta$. Skip any cut that leaves a child with too few
        treated or untreated people; if no cut is left, the node is a leaf.
  \item Repeat inside each child.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip4pt
\textbf{Prediction.} A person with covariates $x$ follows the splits to one leaf. Their estimated effect
$\hat\tau(x)$ is the slope $\hat\tau_\ell$ in that leaf.
\vskip4pt
{\small In the toy: the root tries mother's schooling ($\Delta = 0.08$) and siblings at 3+ ($\Delta = 0$), and splits on
mother's schooling. A youth whose mother finished high school gets $\hat\tau(x) = -0.15$; one whose
mother did not gets $-0.35$.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Catch: the Search Inflates What It Finds\core}
The rule trusts $\hat\tau_L$ and $\hat\tau_R$. If the same people choose the split and estimate the
leaves: a simulation with \alert{no heterogeneity}, everyone's effect $-0.15$; 400 people,
20 noise covariates; 2,000 draws \srcp{figs/w13\_toy.R, C}.
\vskip1pt
Each draw: on half A, pick the covariate whose split makes the two leaves' effects differ
most; estimate both leaves on half A (\emph{adaptive}) and on half B (\emph{honest}).
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
\toprule
& estimated on A (chose the split) & estimated on B (did not) \\
\midrule

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item The search keeps the split whose noise happened to separate the leaves most; the same
        noise sits in the leaf estimates. Selection on the outcome (Week 5), inside one algorithm.
  \item On fresh data, the noise is new and averages out.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{From One Tree to an Honest Forest\core}
One tree is unstable: another sample gives another tree (Week 12). Grow many and average
\srcp{Wager and Athey 2018}. For each tree $t = 1, \dots, T$ (2,000 or more):
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item Draw a \textbf{subsample}: half of the $n$ people, \textbf{without replacement}.
  \item Split the subsample in two (honesty): \textbf{half A} grows the tree, \textbf{half B} estimates each leaf.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize,
  bx/.style={draw,minimum height=0.55cm,inner sep=1pt,align=center}]
  \node[bx,draw=gray,fill=gray!8,minimum width=10cm] (all) at (0,0) {all $n$ people};
  \node[bx,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,minimum width=5cm] (sub) at (-2.5,-1.0) {subsample of tree $t$: $n/2$ people};
  \node[bx,draw=gray,dashed,minimum width=5cm,text=gray] (out) at (2.5,-1.0) {not drawn for tree $t$};
  \node[bx,draw=dukeblue,fill=dukeblue!12,minimum width=2.5cm,text width=2.3cm] (A) at (-3.75,-2.0) {half A: grows};
  \node[bx,draw=accent,fill=accent!15,minimum width=2.5cm,text width=2.3cm] (B) at (-1.25,-2.0) {half B: estimates};
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=1.6mm]}] (sub.north |- all.south) -- (sub.north);
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=1.6mm]}] (A.north |- sub.south) -- (A.north);
  \draw[-{Stealth[length=1.6mm]}] (B.north |- sub.south) -- (B.north);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-10pt
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau(x) = \frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T \hat\tau_t(x),
  \qquad \hat\tau_t(x) = \text{half B's slope $\hat\tau_\ell$ in $x$'s leaf of tree $t$} .
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Subsample Half the People, Without Replacement?}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item \textbf{Why not with replacement} (Week 12's bootstrap)? It draws some people twice: one copy
        could land in half A, the other in half B, and the same noise would choose and estimate.
  \item \textbf{Why not everyone?} Without replacement, drawing all $n$ gives every tree the same
        people. Trees must see different people for their average to be more stable than one tree.
  \item \textbf{Why a subsample of exactly $n/2$?} It behaves like a new sample. Take the mean of
        $Y = 1, 3, 5, 7$:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item across samples, its variance is $s^2/n = 6.67/4 = 1.67$;
    \item across the six possible subsamples of two, the means are $2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6$: variance
          $(4 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 1 + 4)/6 = 1.67$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item How much an estimate moves from one subsample to another is how much it would move from
        one sample to another. This holds at $n/2$, not at other sizes.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Every Person Gets an Effect Estimate: AIPW\core}
The forest's own estimate for person $i$ is $\hat\tau(X_i)$: over the trees grown \emph{without} person $i$,
the average of the leaf slope $\hat\tau_\ell$ where $X_i$ falls.
\vskip4pt
A person's effect does not have to be estimated leaf by leaf. Week 6's AIPW already gives every person a
number, and it needs only three predictions, $\hat m_1$, $\hat m_0$, $\hat e$:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau_i^{\text{AIPW}} = \underbrace{\hat m_1(X_i) - \hat m_0(X_i)}_{\text{g-computation}}
  + \underbrace{\frac{D_i\big(Y_i - \hat m_1(X_i)\big)}{\hat e(X_i)}
  - \frac{(1-D_i)\big(Y_i - \hat m_0(X_i)\big)}{1 - \hat e(X_i)}}_{\text{weighted residual: the correction}}
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Week 6 predicted $m_1$, $m_0$, $e$ with OLS and logit. Now forests predict them; each person is
        predicted by trees grown without them.
  \item Week 6: right on average at every $x$ if the outcome models or the propensity is right. Very noisy
        for one person: like an observed $\tau_i$.
  \item \alert{Use}: every summary below is a mean or an OLS slope with $\hat\tau_i^{\text{AIPW}}$ as the outcome.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Is the Spread Real? The Calibration Test\core}
Honesty stops the splits from exaggerating differences, but each leaf's estimate is still noisy.
\vskip2pt
\textbf{Check}: regress the AIPW estimate on the forest's prediction, centered at its mean $\bar\tau$:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau_i^{\text{AIPW}} = c + b\,\big(\hat\tau(X_i) - \bar\tau\big) + \text{error}
\end{align*}
\vskip-8pt
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.64\linewidth}
\small
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Slope $b$}: when the forest predicts a larger effect, is the AIPW estimate larger?
        $b = 1$: the differences are right in size. $b = 0$: they are noise. Test $b > 0$.
  \item \textbf{Intercept $c$}: the regressor is centered, so $c$ is the mean AIPW estimate, the ATE.
        \texttt{grf} prints $a = c/\bar\tau$; $a = 1$: the forest's average agrees.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.34\linewidth}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=2.6cm,y=1.5cm]
  \draw[->,gray] (0,0) -- (1.08,0) node[below left,font=\scriptsize,text=black] {forest's $\hat\tau(X_i)$};
  \draw[->,gray] (0,0) -- (0,1.1) node[above,font=\scriptsize,text=black] {mean of $\hat\tau_i^{\text{AIPW}}$};
  \foreach \x/\y in {0.1/0.14,0.3/0.27,0.5/0.52,0.7/0.66,0.9/0.92} \fill[accent] (\x,\y) circle (1.6pt);
  \draw[accent,thick] (0.05,0.05) -- (1,1) node[right,font=\scriptsize] {$b = 1$};

  \draw[dukeblue,thick] (0.05,0.5) -- (1,0.5) node[right,font=\scriptsize] {$b = 0$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

{\small If $b$ is not significant, report pre-specified groups, not individual $\hat\tau(X_i)$
\srcp{\texttt{test\_calibration}, in residual form}.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Effects in Pre-Specified Groups}
When the calibration test is weak, the person-level $\hat\tau(X_i)$ cannot be reported. Groups chosen
\emph{before} looking can:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau_g = \frac1{n_g}\sum_{i\in g}\hat\tau_i^{\text{AIPW}} ,
  \qquad \text{s.e.} = \frac{\text{sd of } \hat\tau_i^{\text{AIPW}} \text{ in } g}{\sqrt{n_g}} .
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Each $\hat\tau_i^{\text{AIPW}}$ is right on average at every $x$, so their mean in $g$ estimates the group's
        average effect; the s.e.\ is the usual one for a mean.
  \item No forest prediction enters, and no search: the groups come from theory (parents' income,
        mother's schooling, the propensity of college).
  \item The difference between two groups has s.e.\ $\sqrt{\text{s.e.}_1^2 + \text{s.e.}_2^2}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Would Targeting Help? The TOC Curve}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.54\linewidth}

first \srcp{Yadlowsky et al.\ 2025}. For each $q$:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt\small
  \item Serve the top $q$ of the ranking.
  \item Average $\hat\tau_i^{\text{AIPW}}$ over all of them.
  \item Subtract the ATE, what a random $q$ would get.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip-4pt

\begin{frame}{Overlap: Who Can We Learn About?}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The propensity $\hat e(x)$ here is predicted by a regression forest of $D$ on $X$, not a logit
        (Week 6). 38\% of the sample has $\hat e < 0.05$, a less than one-in-twenty chance of finishing
        college by 25 given their background.
  \item For them, $\tau(x)$ rests on very few graduates who look like them: the estimate
        leans on extrapolation (Week 6).
  \item The ATE ($-0.154$) averages over everyone, including these; the ATT ($-0.111$)
        averages over graduates, who are well represented.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
{\small Negative selection, larger effects for the least likely, is a claim about the region of
weakest overlap. Report effects there with that caveat, or restrict to
$0.05 \leq \hat e \leq 0.95$.}

\begin{frame}{Reporting Heterogeneous Effects: a Checklist\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item State the identifying assumption and show overlap; say where $\hat e$ is near 0 or 1.
  \item Report the average effect first (ATE and ATT), from the doubly robust scores.
  \item Test before reading: the calibration test, with its $p$-value, whatever it says.
  \item Report effects for a \emph{short, pre-specified} list of groups, from the scores.
  \item Label data-found subgroups (trees) as exploratory; estimate them honestly and
        check stability across random splits.
  \item Interpret importance as ``used by the forest,'' never as ``causes the heterogeneity.''
  \item For targeting, report a held-out TOC curve and AUTOC, and compare with a simple rule.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{frame}{Back to One Number}
Often the question is a single effect, with many controls:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Do poorer countries grow faster than richer ones, given 60 characteristics of each
        country?
  \item Does a training program raise earnings, given ten years of earnings history?
  \item Does price lower demand, given everything written about the product?
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
The \alert{partially linear model}: one effect, everything else flexible.
\begin{align*}
  Y_i &= \tau\, D_i + g(X_i) + \epsilon_i, \qquad \E[\epsilon_i \mid D_i, X_i] = 0 \\
  D_i &= e(X_i) + v_i, \qquad\qquad\ \ \E[v_i \mid X_i] = 0
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $\tau$: the target. $g$ and $e$: \textbf{nuisance functions}, needed but not of interest;
        any shape, $X$ possibly large. For binary $D$, $e(X)$ is the propensity score.
  \item Under ignorability given $X$, $\tau$ is causal (a weighted average of the $\tau(x)$
        when effects vary; AIPW targets the ATE).
  \item Week 1: linear $g$, $e$, few controls. Now: estimate $g$ and $e$ by ML.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Week 1's FWL, Written as a Normal Equation\core}
OLS of $Y$ on $D$ and $X$. Let $\tilde D_i$ be the residual from regressing $D$ on $X$, and $\tilde Y_i$ the residual
from regressing $Y$ on $X$. The OLS residual is uncorrelated with every regressor, hence with $\tilde D_i$:
\begin{align*}
  0 &= \textstyle\sum_i \tilde D_i\,\big(Y_i - \hat\tau D_i - X_i'\hat\beta\big)
   && \text{\footnotesize $\tilde D_i$ is $D_i$ minus a combination of $X_i$} \\
  &= \textstyle\sum_i \tilde D_i\,\big(Y_i - \hat\tau D_i\big)
   && \text{\footnotesize $\tilde D_i$ is uncorrelated with $X_i$} \\
  &= \textstyle\sum_i \tilde D_i\,\big(\tilde Y_i - \hat\tau\,\tilde D_i\big)
   && \text{\footnotesize $Y_i - \tilde Y_i$ and $D_i - \tilde D_i$ are linear in $X_i$}
\end{align*}
Solve the last line:
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau = \frac{\sum_i \tilde D_i\,\tilde Y_i}{\sum_i \tilde D_i^2} .
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The normal equation says: choose $\tau$ so that what is left of $Y$, after $X$ and $\tau D$, is uncorrelated
        with what is left of $D$ after $X$.
  \item $X$ enters only through two predictions, of $Y$ and of $D$. Here both are linear.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{DML: the Same Equation, as a Moment Condition}
Replace the two linear predictions (``double'') by any functions of $X$: $\ell(x) = \E[Y_i\mid X_i = x]$,
$e(x) = \E[D_i\mid X_i = x]$; together $\eta = (\ell, e)$. A \textbf{moment condition} is a population average
that is zero at the true parameter:
\begin{align*}
  \E\big[\psi(W_i; \tau, \eta)\big] = 0, \qquad
  \psi(W_i; \tau, \eta) = \Big(Y_i - \ell(X_i) - \tau\,\big(D_i - e(X_i)\big)\Big)\big(D_i - e(X_i)\big) .
\end{align*}
$\psi$ is the \textbf{score}; $W_i = (Y_i, D_i, X_i)$. It holds at the true $\tau$ in $Y_i = \tau D_i + g(X_i) + \epsilon_i$:
\begin{align*}
  \ell(X_i) &= \tau\,e(X_i) + g(X_i)
   && \text{\footnotesize take $\E[\,\cdot\mid X_i]$ of the model} \\
  Y_i - \ell(X_i) - \tau\big(D_i - e(X_i)\big) &= \epsilon_i
   && \text{\footnotesize subtract: $g$ drops out} \\
  \E\big[\psi\big] = \E\big[\epsilon_i\,(D_i - e(X_i))\big] &= 0
   && \text{\footnotesize $\E[\epsilon_i\mid D_i, X_i] = 0$}
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
\textbf{Estimator}: replace $\E$ by the sample average and $\eta$ by ML predictions $(\hat\ell, \hat e)$; solve for $\tau$:
$\hat\tau = \sum_i\tilde D_i\tilde Y_i / \sum_i\tilde D_i^2$. FWL's normal equation was this, with linear predictions.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Neyman Orthogonality}
The score, with predictions $\eta = (\ell, e)$:
\begin{align*}
  \psi(W_i; \tau, \eta) = \Big(Y_i - \ell(X_i) - \tau\,\big(D_i - e(X_i)\big)\Big)\big(D_i - e(X_i)\big) .
\end{align*}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{block}{Proved \srcp{Chernozhukov et al.\ 2018}}
This score is \textbf{Neyman orthogonal}: at the true $\ell$ and $e$,
$\ \partial_\eta\, \E\big[\psi(W_i; \tau, \eta)\big] = 0$.
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
\textbf{In plain language}: if $\ell$ and $e$ are estimated with a small error, the moment condition does not
move, and so $\hat\tau$ is not biased by it.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Why}: both $Y$ and $D$ are residualized. An error in $\hat\ell$ or $\hat e$ is a function of $X$, and it
        enters multiplied by a residual that $X$ cannot predict. On average the product is zero: FWL's logic.
  \item \textbf{What is left}: (error in $\hat\ell$) $\times$ (error in $\hat e$), far smaller than either error.
  \item \textbf{Not every score has this}: multiply by $D_i$ in place of $D_i - e(X_i)$, and an error in
        $\hat\ell$ biases $\hat\tau$ one for one.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why We Care: Errors in the Predictions Barely Move $\hat\tau$}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.56\linewidth}
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/orth_surfaces.pdf}
{\scriptsize Mean of the score at the true $\tau = 0.5$ when the outcome prediction is off by $a$ and $\hat e$ by $b$
\srcp{figs/w13\_surfaces.R}. Left: DML's score, flat at $(0, 0)$. Right: the score that multiplies by $D$, a tilted plane.}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\linewidth}
\small
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item ML predictions are biased on purpose, and improve with $n$ more slowly than a standard error
        shrinks (Week 12).
  \item Tilted plane: that bias passes to $\hat\tau$ one for one. The interval is centered in the wrong
        place, however large $n$.
  \item Flat surface: only error $\times$ error passes. Errors of 0.1 move the score by 0.005, not 0.05.
  \item So $\hat\tau$ has the usual standard error and 95\% interval, as if $\ell$ and $e$ were known.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cross-Fitting: Why\core}
Orthogonality covers prediction errors that do not depend on person $i$'s own noise.
If $\hat\ell$ and $\hat e$ are trained on person $i$, they partly memorize $i$: \alert{overfitting bias}.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item Extreme case: a deep tree gives $\hat e(X_i) = D_i$. Every $\tilde D_i = 0$: nothing is left to compare.
  \item Milder: a flexible $\hat\ell$ is pulled toward $Y_i$ itself, so $\tilde Y_i$ has lost part of $i$'s own
        $\epsilon_i$ and part of the effect. The residuals are no longer what $X$ fails to predict.
  \item The fix is Week 12's test set: predict each person from a model that never saw them. Then the
        prediction error is a function of $X_i$ only, unrelated to $\epsilon_i$.
  \item A single train--test split would waste half the people. Rotate the roles instead, so that everyone is
        predicted once and everyone is used to train.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
{\small The causal forest did the same job: each person was predicted by trees that did not draw them.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Cross-Fitting: How\core}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1.55cm,y=0.38cm,every node/.append style={scale=0.85}]
  \foreach \r in {1,...,5} {
    \foreach \c in {1,...,5} {
      \ifnum\r=\c \node[foldres] at (\c,-\r) {predict};
      \else \node[foldfit] at (\c,-\r) {fit}; \fi }
    \node[font=\scriptsize,anchor=east] at (0.45,-\r) {round \r}; }
  \foreach \c in {1,...,5} \node[font=\scriptsize] at (\c,-0.2) {fold \c};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item Split the people into $K = 5$ folds (whole clusters together, if clustered).
  \item Round $k$: fit $\hat\ell$ and $\hat e$ on the four other folds, with any ML method; predict for fold $k$;
        form $\tilde Y_i$, $\tilde D_i$ there.
  \item After five rounds everyone has residuals from models that never saw them. Regress $\tilde Y$ on $\tilde D$
        once, on everyone: $\hat\tau = \sum_i\tilde D_i\tilde Y_i/\sum_i\tilde D_i^2$.
  \item Standard error: the robust one from that regression. Repeat over several random splits; report the median.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip-2pt
{\small \srct{Chernozhukov et al.\ (2018)}. The held-out predictions also tell how well each learner predicts: used next.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An Example: Do Poor Countries Catch Up?}
\textbf{The question.} The convergence hypothesis (Solow): among countries with the same characteristics,
poorer ones grow faster. In $Y_i = \tau D_i + g(X_i) + \epsilon_i$, convergence is $\tau < 0$.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} Barro and Lee (1994): 90 countries \srcp{\texttt{hdm::GrowthData}}.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $Y$: growth of GDP per capita over a decade (log change; mean 0.045, sd 0.051).
  \item $D$: log GDP per capita at the start, 1965.
  \item $X$: 60 country characteristics (schooling, trade openness, investment, government spending,
        political instability, population) and their squares: \alert{120 controls}.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The problem.} 120 controls, 90 countries \srcp{figs/w13\_growth.R, 1}:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item No controls: $0.001$. Rich and poor countries differ in everything else.
  \item OLS \alert{cannot be estimated}: 122 coefficients from 90 countries. The fit is perfect, every
        residual is 0, and no standard error exists.
  \item DML: let a learner decide how much of the 120 to use. Which learner?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which Learner? Judge by Held-Out Predictions\core}
Four learners, 5 folds \srcp{figs/w13\_growth.R, 2}. Out-of-fold $R^2$: share of variance predicted for
countries the model never saw; below 0 is worse than predicting the mean.
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figs/growth_learners.pdf}
\end{center}
\vskip-8pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item Best for $Y$: forest (0.17); the lasso is below 0 ($-0.15$). Best for $D$: lasso (0.83).
        Growth is hard to predict; initial GDP is easy.
  \item The four estimates agree: $-0.033$ to $-0.042$, every interval below 0.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Which Estimate to Report\core}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Drop learners that predict worse than the mean} on held-out data. Here: the lasso for $Y$.
  \item \textbf{Pick the best learner for each prediction separately}; they need not be the same. Forest for $Y$,
        lasso for $D$:
        \[ \hat\tau = -0.045 \ \ (\text{s.e. } 0.014), \qquad 95\% \text{ interval } [-0.073,\ -0.018] . \]
  \item \textbf{Check that the answer is not fragile.}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item Other learners that predict: ridge $-0.033$, forest $-0.041$, boosting $-0.042$.
    \item Redrawing the random folds 20 times: $-0.053$ to $-0.007$.
    \item Stacking (average the learners, weights from held-out fit): $-0.051$ (0.015) \srcp{figs/w13\_growth\_stack.R}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Convergence: a country 10\% richer in 1965 grew about 0.005 less. OLS could not be estimated.
  \item Choose by prediction, never by $\hat\tau$. An omitted confounder is still omitted.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Difference-in-Differences: Recap, and Where $X$ Enters\core}
Week 10's 2$\times$2: two groups, before and after. With $\Delta Y_i = Y_{i,\text{after}} - Y_{i,\text{before}}$,
\begin{align*}
  \hat\tau = \overline{\Delta Y}_{\text{treated}} - \overline{\Delta Y}_{\text{control}}
  \qquad \text{(OLS of $\Delta Y_i$ on $D_i$)} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Differencing removes every stable difference between units. The assumption left is
        \textbf{parallel trends}: without treatment, both groups would have changed by the same amount.
  \item It fails when the groups differ in $X$ that predict the \emph{change}. Then assume it only among
        units with the same $X$ (conditional parallel trends):
  \[
    \E[\Delta Y_i(0)\mid D_i = 1, X_i] = \E[\Delta Y_i(0)\mid D_i = 0, X_i] .
  \]
  \item That is selection on observables with $\Delta Y$ as the outcome: $\Delta Y_i = \tau D_i + g(X_i) + \epsilon_i$. So DML
        applies as it stands: predict $\Delta Y$ and $D$ from pre-treatment $X$ (cross-fitted), then regress
        residual on residual.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{When the Confounder Is Written Down}
Some confounders are not columns but text or images:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The effect of price on demand must hold product quality fixed; quality is in the
        description \srcp{Bajari et al.\ 2025}.
  \item The effect of being censored on a blogger's later posting must hold the \emph{content}
        of the censored post fixed \srcp{Roberts, Stewart, and Nielsen 2020}.
  \item The effect of nearby violence on test scores must hold neighbourhood quality fixed;
        street images may record it.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
The plan:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item Turn each text into a list of numbers, a \textbf{representation} $W_i$: principal
        components $\to$ autoencoders $\to$ word vectors (Week 12) $\to$ BERT.
  \item Put $W_i$ in the role of $X$ in Section 2: $Y_i = \tau D_i + g(W_i) + \epsilon_i$, with
        $g$ an unknown function of 768 numbers. DML handles the rest.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\subsection{Principal Components}
\begin{frame}{Feature Extraction: Principal Components\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep6pt
  \item We want to turn complex objects, such as text or images, into a relatively small set of
        numerical features.
  \item Principal components are an early, classic example of such features (\emph{embeddings}).
  \item Principal components are weighted sums of the original variables, chosen to be the
        ``best'' at reproducing the data: each new direction is orthogonal to the previous ones.
  \item A small number of principal components often captures most of the variation in the
        original variables, and so gives a useful low-dimensional summary.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Principal Components: the Setup\core}
A sample of $n$ observations $W_1, \dots, W_n$ of a centered vector with $d$ entries,
$W_i = (W_{i1}, \dots, W_{id})'$, each entry with mean 0. Their covariance matrix:
\[
  \Sigma_n = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n W_i W_i' \qquad (d \times d).
\]
\textbf{With $d = 2$, written out:}
\begin{align*}
  \Sigma_n &= \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n
  \begin{pmatrix} W_{i1} \\ W_{i2} \end{pmatrix}
  \begin{pmatrix} W_{i1} & W_{i2} \end{pmatrix}
  = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \begin{pmatrix} W_{i1}^2 & W_{i1}W_{i2} \\ W_{i2}W_{i1} & W_{i2}^2 \end{pmatrix}
   && \text{\footnotesize multiply} \\
  &= \begin{pmatrix} \Var(W_1) & \Cov(W_1, W_2) \\ \Cov(W_1, W_2) & \Var(W_2) \end{pmatrix}
   && \text{\footnotesize means are 0}
\end{align*}
{\small Diagonal: each variable's variance. Off the diagonal: how the variables move together.
For standardized SEI and prestige: 1, 1, and their correlation 0.82.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What $\Sigma_n$ Describes, and Why Decompose It\core}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.44\linewidth}

\begin{frame}{Principal Directions and Components\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The \textbf{eigenvectors} $c_k$ (each of length 1) and \textbf{eigenvalues} $\lambda_k$ of
        $\Sigma_n$ solve
  \[
    \Sigma_n c_k = \lambda_k c_k, \qquad k = 1, \dots, d .
  \]
        Software (\texttt{prcomp}) finds them. $c_1$ is the direction in which the data vary
        most; $\lambda_k$ is the variance along $c_k$; the $\lambda_k$ add up to the total variance.
  \item Keep the $K$ largest ($K \ll d$): $C_K = [c_1, \dots, c_K]$, a $d \times K$ matrix.
  \item \textbf{Project} $W_i$ on each principal direction. With $d = 2$, written out:
  \[
    X_{ki} = c_k' W_i = c_{k1} W_{i1} + c_{k2} W_{i2} :
  \]
        a weighted sum of the variables, the $k$-th \textbf{principal component}.
  \item Person $i$'s compressed features: $X_i^K = (X_{1i}, \dots, X_{Ki})' = C_K' W_i$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Reconstruction\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item $X_i^K$ is the compressed feature vector of the original $W_i$.
  \item Approximate $W_i$ by projecting $X_i^K$ back to the $d$-dimensional space:
  \[
    \hat W_i = C_K X_i^K .
  \]
  \item With $d = 2$ and $K = 1$, written out: $\hat W_i = c_1 X_{1i}$, i.e.\
        $\hat W_{i1} = c_{11} X_{1i}$ and $\hat W_{i2} = c_{12} X_{1i}$.
        Green person: $\hat W_i = (0.707 \times 1.24,\ 0.707 \times 1.24)' = (0.87, 0.87)'$.
  \item The choice of $K$ sets how much of the original information (variance) is kept:
        the share $\sum_{k \leq K} \lambda_k \big/ \sum_k \lambda_k$.
  \item A common use: compressing images (next).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Change What the Network Predicts\core}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item With encoder $e(\cdot)$ and decoder $d(\cdot)$, an autoencoder minimizes the
        rebuilding loss $\E\big[\text{loss}\big(W,\ d(e(W))\big)\big]$. It must rebuild
        \emph{everything}: font, word order, typos, lighting.
  \item Keep the encoder, but predict another target $A$:
        $\E\big[\text{loss}\big(A,\ f(e(W))\big)\big]$. The code keeps what predicts $A$:
  \begin{center}\small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.0}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
  \toprule
  model & trained to predict & the code ends up holding \\
  \midrule
  autoencoder & the input itself & whatever varies most \\
  word2vec & the words near each word & what a word's company says \\
  BERT & words hidden from a sentence & meaning in context \\
  Gebru et al.\ CNN & a car's make and model & visual traits of cars \\
  \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \end{center}
  \item The task is a choice: for price effects, a description's code should hold product
        type and quality, not its wording.
  \item All but the last are \textbf{self-supervised} (slide on the three kinds): the
        target comes free from the data, so they train on billions of words.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Words in Context}

\begin{frame}{From Word Vectors to Words in Context}
Week 12's word2vec gives every word one list of numbers, learned by predicting its neighbours.
Two problems for a whole text:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{One vector per word}: \emph{bank} gets the same numbers in ``river bank'' and
        ``bank loan''; \emph{set} in ``a Lego set'' and ``set the table''.
  \item \textbf{No order}: averaging word vectors gives ``dog bites man'' and ``man bites dog''
        the same text vector.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The fix: let each word's vector depend on the other words in its sentence.
  \item Recurrent networks do it by reading one word at a time; \textbf{attention} does it by
        letting every word look at every other word at once. Attention is what BERT, and every
        large language model since, is built from.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Attention: Look at Every Word at Once\core}
\begin{quote}\small
The survey asked parents about the new school. \textbf{It} was badly underfunded.
\end{quote}
\vskip-2pt
A reader links \emph{It} to \emph{school}, eight words back; a recurrent network must
carry that link through every word between. \textbf{Self-attention}
\srcp{Vaswani et al.\ 2017} lets each word look at all others directly. Each word gets
three lists of $D$ numbers, weighted sums of its word vector with learned weights:
a \textbf{query} $q$ (what it looks for), a \textbf{key} $k$ (what it offers), a
\textbf{value} $v$ (what it passes on). For word $i$:
\begin{align*}
  \text{score}_{ij} &= \frac{\sum_{d} q_{id} k_{jd}}{\sqrt D}
   && \text{\footnotesize how well word $j$ answers word $i$} \\
  \text{weight}_{ij} &= \frac{\exp(\text{score}_{ij})}{\sum_{j'}\exp(\text{score}_{ij'})}
   && \text{\footnotesize softmax: positive, sums to 1 over $j$} \\
  \text{new vector}_{i} &= \textstyle\sum_j \text{weight}_{ij}\, v_{j}
   && \text{\footnotesize weighted average of the values}
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

$(0, 2)$, bank $(0.5, 0.5)$; values: the $(0, 0)$, river $(1, 0)$, loan $(0, 1)$, bank
$(0.5, 0.5)$ \srcp{figs/w12\_repr.R, G; hypothetical}:

\begin{frame}{Position Vectors: Telling Attention the Word Order\core}
Attention is a weighted average over a sentence's words, and an average ignores order:
``dog bites man'' and ``man bites dog'' contain the same three vectors. \textbf{Fix}: add to each
word's vector a \textbf{position vector}, one learned list of numbers per slot $1, \dots, 512$:
\begin{align*}
  h^{(0)}_i = \underbrace{\text{word vector of word } i}_{\text{what the word is}}
  + \underbrace{\text{position vector of slot } i}_{\text{where it stands}} .
\end{align*}
Toy, two numbers: dog $(1, 0)$, man $(0, 1)$, bites $(0, 0)$; positions $(0.2, 0)$, $(0, 0)$, $(0, 0.2)$
(hypothetical):
\begin{center}\small

\toprule
& slot 1 & slot 2 & slot 3 \\
\midrule
``dog bites man'' & dog $(1.2, 0)$ & bites $(0, 0)$ & man $(0, 1.2)$ \\
``man bites dog'' & man $(0.2, 1)$ & bites $(0, 0)$ & dog $(1, 0.2)$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
{\small The same word enters with different numbers in different slots, so queries, keys, and every later
vector can depend on order. BERT: 768 numbers per word and per slot, all learned.}
\end{frame}

\begin{column}{0.58\linewidth}
\small
For word $i$ with input vector $h_i$ (768 numbers):
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Attention.} $q_i, k_i, v_i$: weighted sums of $h_i$'s entries (hidden units without
        a bend). $a_i = \sum_j \text{weight}_{ij} v_j$. 12 heads of 64 numbers side by side: 768.
  \item \textbf{Add, rescale.} $h_i + a_i$: the word plus what it learned from the others; rescale
        its 768 numbers to mean 0, sd 1.
  \item \textbf{Week 12's network}: 768 inputs, 3,072 hidden units with a ReLU-like bend, 768
        outputs; the \emph{same} weights for every word.
  \item \textbf{Add, rescale} again: word $i$'s input to the next layer.
\end{enumerate}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip4pt
{\small Step 1 is the only place words see each other; step 3 is the only bend. BERT stacks 12 such
layers: each word's vector is refined by its context 12 times.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{BERT: Learn by Filling In Blanks\core}
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.47\linewidth}
\begin{center}

\begin{column}{0.51\linewidth}
\small
\textbf{BERT} \srcp{Devlin et al.\ 2019}: a stack of attention layers, trained on books
and Wikipedia.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Training task}: hide 15\% of the words behind \texttt{[MASK]} and predict
        them from \emph{both} sides; also predict whether sentence B followed sentence A.
  \item Nobody labels anything: the hidden words are the labels.
  \item \textbf{Output}: a vector for every word, read in its sentence. The vector of
        the extra first token \texttt{[CLS]} (768 numbers) serves as the vector of the
        whole text.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{From the Last Layer to a Prediction, and Back}
Training BERT on ``The survey was \texttt{[MASK]} in 2020'':
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep5pt
  \item \textbf{Forward.} Word $+$ position vectors $\to$ 12 layers $\to$ a 768-number vector $h^{(12)}$ at the
        \texttt{[MASK]} slot, built from every other word in the sentence.
  \item \textbf{Predict.} Score every word $w$ of the 30,522-word vocabulary,
        $\text{score}_w = \sum_d u_{wd}\, h^{(12)}_d$, and take a softmax: Week 12's word2vec output, a
        multinomial logit over the vocabulary.
  \item \textbf{Loss.} $-\log P(\text{the hidden word})$, e.g.\ $-\log P(\text{fielded})$.
  \item \textbf{Backward.} Backpropagation (Week 12) sends the error through all 12 layers: it
        updates the query, key, and value weights, the feed-forward weights, the word vectors, and
        the position vectors.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip4pt
{\small Repeat over billions of words: 110 million weights in BERT-base. What makes a good guess at the
blank (who did what, in which sense of each word) is what the vectors end up holding.}
\end{frame}


\subsection{Text as a Control}

\begin{frame}{Running Example: Price and Demand for Toys}
\textbf{The question.} How much does demand fall when price rises: the price elasticity
$\tau$ in
\[
  \underbrace{\log q_i}_{Y_i} = \tau\,\underbrace{\log p_i}_{D_i} + g(W_i) + \epsilon_i .
\]
\vskip-2pt
\textbf{The confounder.} Better toys cost more \emph{and} sell more. Quality, brand, and type
are in the description, not in a column.
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The data.} 8,003 toys on Amazon UK with at least one review (PromptCloud, used in
\srct{Chernozhukov et al.\ 2024}'s notebooks):
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $D_i$: log price (s.d.\ 1.13). $Y_i$: log share of all reviews, a proxy for sales (s.d.\ 1.25).
  \item Text: ``name $|$ manufacturer $|$ description''.
  \item $W_i$: BERT (\texttt{bert-base-uncased}), first 128 tokens, average of the last layer's
        word vectors: 768 numbers per toy, no fine-tuning \srcp{Data/build\_amazon\_toys\_bert.py}.
  \item Also: the category tree (``Hobbies $>$ Model Trains''): 6 large categories, 236 subcategories.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why Still So Small? What the Text Cannot Hold}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item Controlling for the description removes confounding by \emph{what the product is}. It
        cannot remove confounding by what is not written.
  \item \srct{Chernozhukov et al.\ (2024, Ch.\ 10)} redo this with proprietary panel data on toy cars:
        multimodal embeddings (text, images, tables), fine-tuned to predict price and quantity.
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item Cross-sectional estimates with rich embeddings: $-0.1$ to $-0.3$, still implausibly small.
    \item The key confounder is \textbf{last period's sales}: popular products build visibility
          and price accordingly. Adding lagged quantity and price: $-0.69$
          (90\% CI about $[-0.78, -0.54]$) for sales rank.
    \item Embeddings then matter for \emph{heterogeneity}: elasticities differ across product
          types.
  \end{itemize}
  \item By category, from our DML residuals: games $-0.20$ (0.05), hobbies $-0.06$ (0.03),
        die-cast vehicles $0.00$ (0.03).
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
{\small The lesson of the whole day, in one example: a better $g(W)$ fixes functional form; it cannot
add a confounder that $W$ does not contain.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Make the Representation Predict the Nuisances}
Two ways to use a pretrained BERT for $\hat\ell(W)$ and $\hat e(W)$ \srcp{Chernozhukov et al.\ 2024, Ch.\ 10}:
\vskip3pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Feature extraction} (what we did): freeze BERT, take its 768 numbers, feed them to
        a lasso or forest.
  \item \textbf{Fine-tuning}: put a regression layer on top of BERT and update \emph{all} BERT's weights
        to predict $\log p$ (one copy) and $\log q$ (another copy), with early stopping on a
        validation set.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Orthogonality says the bias is (error in $\hat e$) $\times$ (error in $\hat\ell$): better
        nuisance predictions mean a smaller remaining bias. So train the representation to predict
        \emph{the nuisances}, not to rebuild the text.
  \item Cross-fitting still applies: fine-tune on folds that exclude the products being
        predicted. Fine-tuning a 110-million-weight model memorizes easily.
  \item Same structure as Week 12's ``change what the network predicts.''
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Generative AI for Causal Inference}
\par{\footnotesize\color{gray}Imai and Nakamura (2026), \textit{PNAS} 123(36): e2530532123}\vskip4pt
\textbf{The question.} How to adjust for a confounder that is a text or image, without
choosing a representation by hand or fine-tuning one on a small sample?
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The idea.} Ask an open-source generative model to \emph{reproduce} each document
(``You are a text generator who simply repeats the input text''), and keep its internal state:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $R_i$: the hidden state at the last token. LLaMA 3 (8 billion weights): 4,096 numbers.
        Stable Diffusion for images: 16,384 numbers instead of 786,432 pixels.
  \item The model writes the document out of $R_i$, deterministically: so $R_i$ holds
        \emph{everything} in the document, including any confounder in it.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The catch.} Adjusting for all of $R_i$ breaks overlap: it also predicts treatment
almost perfectly (Week 6). Keep only the part of $R_i$ that matters for the outcome.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Imai and Nakamura: the Estimator}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Deconfounder}: a network maps $R_i$ to a few numbers $f(R_i)$, which feed two
        heads, $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$, predicting $Y$ for the untreated and treated. Fit by least squares:
  \[
    \min\ \frac1n\sum_i \Big(Y_i - \mu_{D_i}\big(f(R_i), Z_i\big)\Big)^2 .
  \]
        No head predicts $D$: what predicts only treatment is discarded.
  \item \textbf{Propensity}: a second network, $\hat\pi\big(f(R_i), Z_i\big)$.
  \item \textbf{ATT}: Week 6's ATT AIPW score, with $\hat\mu_0$ and $\hat\pi$, 2-fold
        cross-fitting; standard errors from the score.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Generative AI supplies the representation; DML supplies the inference.
  \item Assumptions: ignorability given $Z$ and the confounding features of the text;
        overlap given $f(R)$; the model reproduces the text.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Imai and Nakamura: Censorship on Weibo}
Reanalysis of \srct{Roberts, Stewart, and Nielsen (2020)}: 75,324 Weibo posts by 4,155 users.
Does being censored change what a user does in the next four weeks? Confounders: the post's text
(LLaMA 3), date, and the user's prior four weeks. ATT, s.e.\ clustered by user (Tables S3, S4 of the arXiv v4):
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
outcome over the next 4 weeks & GPI, LLaMA 3 & text matching \\
\midrule
censorship rate & 0.012 (0.000) & 0.004 (0.001) \\
missing-post rate & 0.065 (0.003) & 0.050 (0.016) \\
number of posts & $-16.2$ (2.60) & 11.5 (41.2) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt\small
  \item Text matching (topic model $+$ coarsened exact matching) keeps 879 posts; GPI uses all.
  \item Censored users post about 16 fewer times in the next month: evidence of
        \textbf{self-censorship}, which the original analysis could not detect.
  \item Adding a list of 60 sensitive keywords as a control barely moves GPI's estimate
        (8\% for posts); it moves text matching's by more than 500\%.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Imai and Nakamura: What to Take Away}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item The pipeline is today's: a representation of the text, flexible nuisance functions,
        an orthogonal score, cross-fitting.
  \item What is new is where $W$ comes from: an open-source model's own state, which by
        construction contains the whole document.
  \item \textbf{Cautions they report}
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
    \item Tuning matters: in their simulation an over-sized deconfounder lets overlap fail
          again (coverage 0.76 instead of 0.97).
    \item Representations differ: a Sentence-BERT representation reverses the sign of the
          censorship effect.
    \item Reproduction can fail; larger models are not always better.
  \end{itemize}
  \item For sociology: posts, speeches, court records, photos can enter a causal analysis as
        controls (or as treatments), with standard errors.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where This Leaves the Course}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{One idea, three appearances.} FWL (Week 1) $\to$ double robustness (Week 6)
