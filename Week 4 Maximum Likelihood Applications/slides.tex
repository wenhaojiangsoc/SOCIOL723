\section[The GLM]{The Generalized Linear Model}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Putting Covariates Inside the Likelihood}
Last week the parameter $\theta$ was a single number. Now we let it depend on
covariates. Three ingredients:
\vskip4pt
\begin{block}{The GLM recipe}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Random component}: a distribution for $Y_i$ with mean
        $\mu_i = \E[Y_i\mid X_i]$ --- normal, Bernoulli, Poisson, \dots
  \item \textbf{Systematic component}: a \emph{linear predictor}
        $\eta_i = X_i'\bm\beta$.
  \item \textbf{Link function}: $g(\mu_i) = \eta_i$, connecting the two.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The linear predictor is always linear. All the nonlinearity lives in the
        link, whose job is to map $\mu_i$ from its natural range (a probability in
        $[0,1]$, a rate in $[0,\infty)$) onto the whole real line.
  \item This is why we can keep using everything from Weeks 1--2 about $X'\beta$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Standard Family}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.35}
\begin{tabular}{llll}
\toprule
Outcome & Distribution & Link $g(\mu)$ & \texttt{R} \\
\midrule
Continuous & Normal & identity: $\mu$ & \texttt{glm(..., gaussian)} $=$ \texttt{lm} \\
Binary & Bernoulli & logit: $\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}$ & \texttt{binomial(link="logit")} \\
Binary & Bernoulli & probit: $\Phi^{-1}(\mu)$ & \texttt{binomial(link="probit")} \\
Count & Poisson & log: $\log\mu$ & \texttt{poisson(link="log")} \\
Count, overdispersed & Neg.\ binomial & log & \texttt{MASS::glm.nb} \\
Nominal, $J$ categories & Multinomial & multi-logit & \texttt{nnet::multinom} \\
Ordered, $J$ categories & --- & cumulative logit & \texttt{MASS::polr} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip6pt
The \emph{canonical} link for each distribution (logit for the binomial, log for
the Poisson) makes the algebra especially clean, but you are free to choose
another --- probit is not canonical and nobody minds.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How \texttt{glm()} Actually Fits\adv}
Every GLM log-likelihood has score equations of the form
\begin{align*}
  \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{\partial \mu_i}{\partial \eta_i}\,
  \frac{Y_i - \mu_i}{V(\mu_i)}\, X_i = \zero ,
\end{align*}
where $V(\mu)$ is the variance function implied by the distribution.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Compare with OLS's normal equations $\sum_i X_i(Y_i - X_i'\bm\beta) = \zero$:
        the difference is the weight $\frac{\partial\mu/\partial\eta}{V(\mu)}$.
  \item So GLMs are fitted by \alert{iteratively reweighted least squares}: run a
        weighted regression, update the weights from the new fitted values, repeat.
        This is Fisher scoring in disguise.
  \item Practical consequence: \texttt{glm()} usually converges in 4--6
        iterations, and failure to converge is a real signal (often separation).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Binary]{Binary Outcomes}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Start With the Linear Probability Model}
Just run OLS with a $0/1$ outcome: $\Pr(Y_i = 1\mid X_i) = X_i'\bm\beta$.
\vskip4pt
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{In its favour}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item Coefficients are marginal effects, directly on the probability scale.
  \item Everything from Weeks 1--2 applies: FWL, fixed effects, IV, clustering.
  \item Robust to functional form in the sense of being the best linear
        approximation to the CEF.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{Against}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item Fitted probabilities can fall outside $[0,1]$.
  \item Errors are \emph{necessarily} heteroskedastic:
        $\Var(Y_i\mid X_i)=p_i(1-p_i)$. (Use robust SEs --- Week 2.)
  \item Constant marginal effects are implausible near 0 and 1.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip6pt
\begin{block}{A reasonable position}
For a causal contrast with a binary treatment, the LPM is often fine and easier
to interpret. For prediction, extrapolation, or when the probabilities are near
the boundary, you want logit or probit.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Latent-Variable Motivation}
Imagine an unobserved continuous propensity $Y_i^{*}$ --- a taste for voting, a
level of labour-market attachment:
\begin{align*}
  Y_i^{*} = X_i'\bm\beta + \epsilon_i ,
  \qquad
  Y_i = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } Y_i^{*} > 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}
\end{align*}
Then
\begin{align*}
  \Pr(Y_i = 1\mid X_i) = \Pr(\epsilon_i > -X_i'\bm\beta) = F(X_i'\bm\beta)
\end{align*}
for $F$ the CDF of $-\epsilon_i$.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $\epsilon_i \sim$ \textbf{logistic} $\Rightarrow$
        $F(z) = \frac{e^{z}}{1+e^{z}} = \Lambda(z)$: the \alert{logit} model.
  \item $\epsilon_i \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ $\Rightarrow$ $F(z) = \Phi(z)$: the
        \alert{probit} model.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
The choice between them is a choice about the tails of an unobservable. It almost
never matters empirically.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Logit Likelihood}
With $p_i \equiv \Lambda(X_i'\bm\beta) = \dfrac{\exp(X_i'\bm\beta)}{1+\exp(X_i'\bm\beta)}$,
the Bernoulli log-likelihood from last week becomes
\begin{align*}
  \loglik(\bm\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\, Y_i \log p_i + (1-Y_i)\log(1-p_i)\Big] .
\end{align*}
\pause
Substituting and simplifying (using $\log\frac{p_i}{1-p_i} = X_i'\bm\beta$ and
$\log(1-p_i) = -\log(1+e^{X_i'\bm\beta})$):
\begin{align*}
  \loglik(\bm\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\, Y_i\, X_i'\bm\beta
  - \log\big(1+e^{X_i'\bm\beta}\big)\Big] .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
This is globally concave in $\bm\beta$, so the maximum is unique whenever it
exists --- a genuinely nice property that probit shares.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Logit Score}
Differentiate. The key fact is $\dfrac{\partial \Lambda(z)}{\partial z} = \Lambda(z)\big(1-\Lambda(z)\big)$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial\loglik}{\partial\bm\beta}
  = \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ Y_i X_i - \frac{e^{X_i'\bm\beta}}{1+e^{X_i'\bm\beta}}X_i\right]
  = \sum_{i=1}^n \big(Y_i - p_i\big)X_i \ \overset{!}{=}\ \zero .
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{Look at what this says}
$\sum_i (Y_i - \hat p_i)X_i = \zero$: the residuals $Y_i - \hat p_i$ are
orthogonal to the regressors. \alert{Exactly the OLS normal equations}, with the
fitted probability in place of the fitted value.
\end{alertblock}
\vskip3pt
There is no closed-form solution because $p_i$ depends on $\bm\beta$
nonlinearly --- hence the numerical optimization from last week. The information
matrix\adv{} is $\Info(\bm\beta) = \sum_i p_i(1-p_i)X_iX_i'$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[shrink=10]{Logit Versus Probit}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1]
  \draw[->] (-4.4,0) -- (4.6,0) node[right]{\small $X'\beta$};
  \draw[->] (0,0) -- (0,2.9) node[above]{\small $\Pr(Y=1)$};
  \draw[gray,dashed] (-4.4,2.5) -- (4.4,2.5) node[right]{\scriptsize 1};
  \draw[dukeblue,very thick,smooth,samples=90,domain=-4.3:4.3,variable=\x]

  \draw[accent,very thick,dash pattern=on 4pt off 2pt,smooth,samples=90,domain=-4.3:4.3,variable=\x]
    plot ({\x},{2.5*(1/(1+exp(-1.702*\x)))});
  \node[dukeblue] at (3.0,1.6) {\scriptsize logit};
  \node[accent] at (1.5,2.2) {\scriptsize probit, rescaled};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-6pt
{\footnotesize (The sketched ``probit'' curve is the standard logistic
approximation $\Lambda(1.702x)$ to $\Phi(x)$; the two agree to within $0.01$
everywhere, which is itself the point of the slide.)}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The two curves are nearly indistinguishable after rescaling: logit
        coefficients are roughly $1.6$--$1.8$ times probit coefficients.
  \item Choose logit if you want odds ratios; probit if you want a normal latent
        variable (useful for bivariate probit and selection models).
  \item \alert{Marginal effects and predicted probabilities from the two are
        essentially identical.} Do not agonize over this choice.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Scale Is Not Identified --- and Why It Matters}
In the latent-variable setup, only $\bm\beta/\sigma_\epsilon$ is identified: we
normalize $\sigma_\epsilon$ (to $\pi/\sqrt3$ for logit, to 1 for probit) because
the data cannot tell us anything about it.
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{The consequence \srcp{Allison 1999; Mood 2010}}
Adding a covariate changes the residual variance and therefore \emph{rescales all
the coefficients}, even ones whose true effect is unchanged:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item You cannot compare logit coefficients \alert{across nested models} the way
        you compare OLS coefficients.
  \item You cannot compare logit coefficients \alert{across groups or samples}
        with different residual variance.
  \item ``The coefficient shrank when I added controls'' may be pure rescaling
        rather than confounding.
\end{itemize}
\end{alertblock}
\vskip3pt
\textbf{The fix}: compare \emph{marginal effects} or \emph{predicted
probabilities}, which are on the probability scale and do not suffer from this.
\end{frame}

\section[Interpretation]{Interpretation}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Scales, Three Meanings}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Scale & Quantity & Interpretation of $\beta_j$ \\
\midrule
Log-odds & $\log\frac{p}{1-p} = X'\bm\beta$ & additive: linear, but nobody thinks
  in log-odds \\
Odds & $\frac{p}{1-p} = e^{X'\bm\beta}$ & multiplicative: $e^{\beta_j}$ is the
  \emph{odds ratio} \\
Probability & $p = \Lambda(X'\bm\beta)$ & \alert{what we care about}, but the
  effect is not constant \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The model is linear on the first scale and nonlinear on the third. That
        tension is the whole interpretation problem.
  \item \alert{An odds ratio is not a risk ratio}, and it is not a probability
        difference. An odds ratio of 2 can correspond to a probability change from
        $0.01$ to $0.02$ or from $0.33$ to $0.50$.
  \item Report probabilities or marginal effects. Report odds ratios only if your
        audience genuinely works in odds.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Marginal Effects}
For a \emph{continuous} $X_j$, the marginal effect on the probability is
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\partial \Pr(Y_i=1\mid X_i)}{\partial X_{ij}}
  = \underbrace{\lambda(X_i'\bm\beta)}_{\text{density at } X_i}\ \beta_j ,
  \qquad \lambda(z) = \Lambda(z)\big(1-\Lambda(z)\big) .
\end{align*}
\vskip-2pt
{\footnotesize For a \emph{binary or categorical} $X_j$ a derivative is
meaningless; report the average \emph{discrete change}
$\frac1n\sum_i[\hat p_i(X_j{=}1) - \hat p_i(X_j{=}0)]$ instead. This is what
\texttt{avg\_comparisons()} does automatically.}
\alert{It depends on $X_i$}, so there is no single ``the'' marginal effect. Two
standard summaries:
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Marginal effect at the mean (MEM)}:
        $\lambda(\bar X'\bhat)\,\hat\beta_j$. Fast, but evaluated at a
        hypothetical person who may not exist (mean of a dummy $=$ $0.53$ female).
  \item \textbf{Average marginal effect (AME)}:
        $\frac1n\sum_i \lambda(X_i'\bhat)\,\hat\beta_j$. Computed for every real
        respondent, then averaged. \alert{This is the default you should use.}
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
\srct{Hanmer and Kalkan (2013)} make the case for the AME (``observed-value'')
approach over the MEM (``average-case'') approach.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Predicted Probabilities Done Right}
Most readers want: \emph{what is the predicted probability at $X_j = a$ versus
$X_j = b$, holding everything else at realistic values?}
\vskip2pt
\begin{block}{The observed-value approach \srcp{Hanmer and Kalkan 2013}}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item Take the whole sample as it is.
  \item Set $X_j = a$ for \emph{everyone}, leaving all other covariates at their
        actual values. Compute $\hat p_i$ for each $i$ and average.
  \item Repeat with $X_j = b$.
  \item Report both, and their difference.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item This targets a population-level quantity, and is exactly the
        \emph{g-computation} estimator we meet again in Week 6.
  \item Contrast with fixing every other covariate at its mean, which describes a
        person who may not exist.
  \item \texttt{R}: \texttt{marginaleffects::avg\_predictions()},
        \texttt{avg\_slopes()}, \texttt{avg\_comparisons()}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Uncertainty for These Quantities}
Predicted probabilities and marginal effects are \emph{nonlinear} functions of
$\bhat$, so you cannot read their standard errors off the regression table. Three
options:
\vskip3pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Delta method} (Week 2): linearize and use
        $\nabla g(\bhat)'\widehat{\Var}(\bhat)\nabla g(\bhat)$. Fast, analytic,
        and the default in \texttt{marginaleffects}.
  \item \textbf{Simulation} \srcp{King, Tomz, and Wittenberg 2000}: draw
        $\tilde{\bm\beta}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal N\big(\bhat, \widehat{\Var}(\bhat)\big)$
        for $s = 1,\dots,S$, compute the quantity of interest at each draw, and
        take percentiles. Trivial to implement, handles any nonlinearity.
  \item \textbf{Bootstrap}: resample the data, refit, recompute. Slowest, makes
        the fewest assumptions about the shape of the sampling distribution.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip3pt
\alert{Do not} compute a confidence interval on the log-odds scale and then
transform its endpoints if what you want is an interval for a
\emph{difference} in probabilities.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Interactions in Nonlinear Models}
In a logit, the effect of $X_1$ on the probability depends on $X_2$ \emph{even
with no product term in the model}: the curve is steepest in the middle and flat
at the ends.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item So a significant $\hat\beta_{12}$ on the log-odds scale is neither
        necessary nor sufficient for interaction on the probability scale
        \srcp{Ai and Norton 2003}.
  \item Conversely, \srct{Berry, DeMeritt, and Esarey (2010)} show that omitting
        the product term imposes a very specific --- and usually unmotivated ---
        form of interaction.
  \item \textbf{Practical advice}: include the product term if your theory says
        the latent-scale effects interact, and then \alert{report the interaction
        you care about as a difference in average marginal effects}, with an
        interval.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\texttt{marginaleffects::avg\_comparisons(m, variables = "x1", by = "x2")}.
\end{frame}

\section[More Outcomes]{Multinomial, Ordered, and Count Outcomes}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Multinomial Logit}
For an unordered outcome with $J$ categories, pick a base category $J$ and model
\begin{align*}
  \Pr(Y_i = j \mid X_i) = \frac{\exp(X_i'\bm\beta_j)}{1 + \sum_{m=1}^{J-1}\exp(X_i'\bm\beta_m)} ,
  \qquad \bm\beta_J \equiv \zero .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item You estimate $J-1$ coefficient vectors --- interpretation burden grows
        fast. Report predicted probabilities by category, not coefficient tables.
  \item Every coefficient is relative to the omitted base category. Changing the
        base changes every number without changing the model.
  \item \textbf{IIA}: the ratio $\Pr(Y=j)/\Pr(Y=m)$ does not depend on what other
        options exist --- the ``red bus / blue bus'' problem. Testable in
        principle (Hausman--McFadden), but the tests behave poorly; think
        substantively instead.
  \item Alternatives when IIA is untenable: nested logit, multinomial probit,
        mixed logit.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ordered Logit}
For an ordered outcome, one latent variable and $J-1$ cutpoints:
\begin{align*}
  Y_i^{*} = X_i'\bm\beta + \epsilon_i ,
  \qquad
  Y_i = j \ \text{ if } \ \tau_{j-1} < Y_i^{*} \leq \tau_j .
\end{align*}
This gives the cumulative-logit form
$\Pr(Y_i \leq j \mid X_i) = \Lambda(\tau_j - X_i'\bm\beta)$.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Only \emph{one} $\bm\beta$ regardless of $J$: far more parsimonious than
        multinomial logit. That parsimony is the \alert{proportional odds
        assumption} --- the effect of $X$ is the same at every cutpoint.
  \item Test it with a Brant test, or by comparing to a generalized ordered logit
        that relaxes it (\texttt{VGAM}, \texttt{ordinal::clm}).
  \item If proportional odds fails badly and the categories are genuinely ordered,
        a partial proportional odds model is usually the right compromise.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Poisson Regression for Counts}
\begin{align*}
  Y_i \mid X_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu_i), \qquad \log\mu_i = X_i'\bm\beta .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Log link keeps $\mu_i > 0$; coefficients are \emph{semi-elasticities}:
        $e^{\beta_j}$ is the multiplicative change in the expected count, so
        $\beta_j = 0.10$ means about $10\%$ more events.
  \item \textbf{Exposure}: if units are observed for different lengths of time or
        have different populations at risk, model the \emph{rate} by adding an
        \texttt{offset(log(exposure))} --- a coefficient constrained to 1.
  \item The Poisson imposes $\Var(Y_i\mid X_i) = \E[Y_i\mid X_i]$. That is a
        strong restriction and it is usually violated.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
Useful fact: with a log link, Poisson regression is \emph{consistent for the
conditional mean even when the Poisson distribution is wrong}, provided the mean
is correctly specified. Pair it with robust standard errors and it is a
workhorse for non-negative outcomes.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Overdispersion}
In practice $\Var(Y_i\mid X_i) > \E[Y_i\mid X_i]$ almost always --- unobserved
heterogeneity makes some units systematically more event-prone.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Consequence: coefficients stay roughly right, but \alert{standard errors
        are far too small}, sometimes by a factor of two or three.
  \item \textbf{Quasi-Poisson}: keep the Poisson mean, multiply the variance by a
        dispersion parameter $\phi$ estimated from the data. Simple; inflates the
        standard errors by $\sqrt{\hat\phi}$.
  \item \textbf{Negative binomial}: model the heterogeneity explicitly as a gamma
        mixture, giving $\Var(Y) = \mu + \mu^2/\theta$. A genuine likelihood, so
        AIC and LR tests apply. \texttt{MASS::glm.nb}.
  \item \textbf{Robust standard errors} on a Poisson fit are the pragmatic
        third option, and are consistent under any variance function.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
Test with a score test for overdispersion (\texttt{AER::dispersiontest}) --- a
nice example of a test that needs only the restricted model.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Excess Zeros}
Many sociological counts have far more zeros than any Poisson or negative
binomial allows: number of arrests, number of union grievances, hours of
volunteering.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Zero-inflated}: a mixture of a ``structural zero'' class (people
        who could never have a positive count) and a count process. Two equations,
        two sets of coefficients. \texttt{pscl::zeroinfl}.
  \item \textbf{Hurdle}: a binary model for zero versus positive, then a
        truncated count model for the positives. \texttt{pscl::hurdle}.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{Choose on substance, not fit}
Zero-inflation says some zeros are of a different \emph{kind}; a hurdle says
crossing from zero to one is a different \emph{process}. Ask which story your
theory tells before letting AIC decide. And check first whether a negative
binomial already accommodates the zeros --- often it does.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Practice]{Diagnostics and Practice}

\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Separation}
\vskip-2pt
If some linear combination of the covariates perfectly predicts the outcome ---
say, every respondent with a graduate degree voted --- then the likelihood has no
interior maximum. The coefficient marches off to $\pm\infty$.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Symptoms: enormous coefficients (10, 20, 40) with enormous standard
        errors; convergence warnings; results that change when you change
        \texttt{maxit}.
  \item Common with rare outcomes, small samples, and many dummy variables ---
        i.e.\ very common in practice.
  \item \textbf{Do not} respond by dropping the variable; that discards the
        strongest signal in the data.
  \item \textbf{Fix} \srcp{Zorn 2005}: \alert{Firth's penalized likelihood}, which
        adds a Jeffreys-prior penalty that guarantees finite estimates and also
        reduces small-sample bias. \texttt{logistf} or \texttt{brglm2}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Assessing Fit}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Deviance} $= 2\big(\loglik_{\text{saturated}} - \loglik_{\text{fitted}}\big)$:
        the GLM analogue of the residual sum of squares. The saturated term is
        common to nested models, so \emph{differences} in deviance are exactly LR
        tests.
  \item \textbf{Pseudo-$R^2$} (McFadden: $1 - \loglik_{\text{full}}/\loglik_{\text{null}}$).
        Interpretable only as a rank ordering, never as ``proportion of variance
        explained.'' Values of $0.2$--$0.4$ are considered excellent.
  \item \textbf{Classification accuracy}: needs a threshold, and is misleading
        with rare outcomes --- always compare against the null model that predicts
        the majority class.
  \item \textbf{ROC / AUC}: accuracy across all thresholds. $0.5$ is a coin flip,
        $1.0$ is perfect.
  \item \textbf{Predictive distribution checks}: simulate outcomes from the fitted
        model and compare their distribution to the data. This is the most
        informative diagnostic and the least often used.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Practical Protocol}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item Choose the distribution from the \emph{outcome}, and the link from
        convention. Do not agonize over logit versus probit.
  \item Fit, then check convergence and look for separation.
  \item Check overdispersion for counts; check proportional odds for ordered
        outcomes.
  \item \alert{Report average marginal effects or predicted probabilities}, with
        intervals from the delta method or simulation. Coefficient tables belong
        in an appendix.
  \item Never compare raw coefficients across nested logit models or across
        groups. Compare marginal effects.
  \item Use robust or clustered standard errors, exactly as in Week 2 --- the
        sandwich applies to \texttt{glm} objects too.
\end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Looking Ahead}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep5pt
  \item We now have both estimation engines: least squares and maximum
        likelihood. Everything in the rest of the course is one of them, applied
        to a cleverly chosen quantity.
  \item \alert{Notice what we have \emph{not} done}: nothing in Weeks 1--4 says
        any coefficient is a causal effect. We have been describing conditional
        distributions.
  \item \textbf{Week 5} changes the question. Potential outcomes and DAGs give us
        a language for saying what we \emph{want} to estimate, and what would have
        to be true to get it.
\end{itemize}
