\begin{frame}{The Pivot}
Weeks 1--4 were about \emph{estimation}: given a target parameter, how do we
estimate it and how uncertain are we?
\vskip3pt
Nothing we did licensed a causal claim. OLS estimates the best linear
approximation to a conditional expectation; maximum likelihood estimates the
parameters of a probability model. Neither says the word ``effect.''
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{Today's question is different}
\emph{What are we trying to estimate, and what would have to be true about the
world for our estimate to recover it?}
\end{alertblock}
\vskip3pt
Two languages answer it, and they are complementary: \textbf{potential outcomes}
(what would have happened) and \textbf{structural equations / directed acyclic
graphs} (how the world is wired). Everything in Weeks 6--13 applies one or both.
\end{frame}
\section[Potential Outcomes]{The Potential Outcomes Framework}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Worlds Per Person\core}
For a binary treatment $D_i \in \{0,1\}$, imagine that each unit $i$ has
\emph{two} outcomes written into it before treatment is assigned:
\begin{align*}
  Y_i(1) &= \text{the outcome if unit } i \text{ were treated,} \\
  Y_i(0) &= \text{the outcome if unit } i \text{ were not treated.}
\end{align*}
The \alert{individual treatment effect} is
$\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$.
\vskip6pt
We observe only one of them:
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;Y_i = D_i\,Y_i(1) + (1-D_i)\,Y_i(0)\;}
  \qquad\text{(the \emph{switching equation})}
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{block}{Holland's fundamental problem of causal inference}
$\tau_i$ is never observed for anyone. Causal inference is a \alert{missing data
problem}, and every method in this course is a strategy for filling in the
missing column.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Missing Data View}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
\begin{tabular}{cccccc}
\toprule
Unit & $D_i$ & $Y_i(0)$ & $Y_i(1)$ & $\tau_i$ & $Y_i$ (observed) \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 12 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 12 \\
2 & 0 & 7 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 7 \\
3 & 1 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 9  & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 9 \\
4 & 0 & 5 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip6pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Half the table is missing \emph{by construction}, not by bad luck. No
        larger sample fixes it.
  \item So we give up on $\tau_i$ and target \emph{averages}, which are
        estimable under assumptions.
  \item ``No causation without manipulation'' \srcp{Holland 1986}: if you cannot
        even imagine intervening to set $D_i$, the potential outcomes are not
        well defined. This is why ``the effect of race'' or ``the effect of
        gender'' needs careful restatement.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimands: Say Which Average You Want\core}
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATE} &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] && \text{average treatment effect} \\
  \text{ATT} &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] && \text{on the treated} \\
  \text{ATU} &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0] && \text{on the untreated} \\
  \text{CATE}(x) &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid X_i = x] && \text{conditional on covariates}
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $\text{ATE} = \pi\,\text{ATT} + (1-\pi)\,\text{ATU}$ where
        $\pi = \Pr(D_i=1)$.
  \item These differ whenever effects are heterogeneous \emph{and} correlated
        with selection, which is the normal case in sociology.
  \item Policy relevance differs too: ``should we expand this programme?''
        is about the ATU; ``was this programme worth running?'' is about the ATT.
  \item \alert{Later we will meet estimands nobody chose}: the LATE (Week 7),
        the RD effect at the cutoff (Week 9), and OLS's own variance-weighted
        average (Week 1).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Estimands, With Numbers}
Four units, effects that differ, and treatment correlated with the effect:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{cccccc}
\toprule
Unit & $D_i$ & $Y_i(0)$ & $Y_i(1)$ & $\tau_i$ & $Y_i$ observed \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & 4 & 10 & 6 & 10 \\
2 & 1 & 6 & 14 & 8 & 14 \\
3 & 0 & 5 & 6  & 1 & 5 \\
4 & 0 & 7 & 10 & 3 & 7 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATE} &= \tfrac14(6+8+1+3) = 4.5, &
  \text{ATT} &= \tfrac12(6+8) = 7, &
  \text{ATU} &= \tfrac12(1+3) = 2 .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The naive comparison is $\tfrac12(10+14) - \tfrac12(5+7) = 12 - 6 = 6$
, which equals none of them.
  \item Check the decomposition: ATT $+$ selection bias
        $= 7 + \big(\tfrac12(4+6) - \tfrac12(5+7)\big) = 7 - 1 = 6$. \checkmark
  \item ATE $= \pi\,$ATT $+ (1-\pi)\,$ATU $= 0.5(7)+0.5(2) = 4.5$. \checkmark
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why the Naive Comparison Fails\core}
The quantity we can compute from data is the difference in observed means. Add
and subtract $\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1]$:
\begin{align*}
  \underbrace{\E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{naive comparison}}
  &= \E[Y_i(1)\mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0] \\[3pt]
  &= \underbrace{\E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1]}_{\text{ATT}} \\
  &\quad + \underbrace{\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0]}_{\alert{\text{selection bias}}} .
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Selection bias asks: \emph{would the treated and untreated have differed
        even with no treatment?} Almost always yes.
  \item Note that it involves \alert{only} $Y_i(0)$, a quantity about the
        world as it would have been. No amount of data on $Y_i$ can settle it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Randomization Solves It\core}
If $D_i$ is assigned by a coin flip, then $D_i$ is independent of everything
about the unit, including its potential outcomes:
\begin{align*}
  \big(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)\big) \indep D_i .
\end{align*}
Then $\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1] = \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0] = \E[Y_i(0)]$, the selection
bias term is zero, and
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_i\mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0] = \text{ATE} = \text{ATT} .
\end{align*}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Randomization does not make the groups identical, it makes them
        \emph{exchangeable}, so that any difference is chance.
  \item Note what randomization is doing: it is an assumption made \emph{true by
        design} rather than argued for.
  \item Every observational design in this course is an attempt to buy some
        version of this independence with an argument instead of a coin.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{SUTVA: The Assumption Nobody States\core}
The framework quietly assumed that writing ``$Y_i(1)$'' makes sense. That
requires the \alert{stable unit treatment value assumption}:
\vskip1pt
\begin{block}{SUTVA has two parts}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{No interference}: unit $i$'s outcome does not depend on anyone
        else's treatment. Otherwise $Y_i$ is a function of the whole vector
        $(D_1,\dots,D_n)$ and each unit has $2^n$ potential outcomes.
  \item \textbf{No hidden versions of treatment}: ``$D_i = 1$'' means the same
        thing for everyone, or $Y_i(1)$ is ambiguous.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep1pt
  \item Sociology is full of interference: peer effects, neighbourhood effects,
        general equilibrium, diffusion. If everyone gets a college degree, the
        return to a degree changes.
  \item Hidden versions: ``college'' covers a community-college certificate and
        a Princeton BA; ``marriage'' in 1960 and in 2020.
  \item \alert{State SUTVA explicitly and defend it}, or redefine the treatment.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Consistency: the Assumption Hiding in the Notation}
The switching equation $Y_i = D_iY_i(1) + (1-D_i)Y_i(0)$ contains an assumption
so quiet it usually goes unnamed.
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{Consistency}
For a unit that actually received $d$, the observed outcome \emph{is} that unit's
potential outcome under $d$: $\ Y_i = Y_i(d)$ when $D_i = d$.
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Sounds like a tautology. It is not: it requires the treatment to be
        well enough defined that ``the outcome under $d$'' is a single quantity.
  \item It fails when treatment is heterogeneous in ways that matter, ``college''
        that is two years at a community college versus four at a research
        university, which is the hidden-versions half of SUTVA again.
  \item It also fails under \emph{anticipation}: if units respond to the
        assignment before it is realized, the observed outcome reflects both.
  \item \alert{Consistency is the bridge between the causal notation and the
        data.} Without it, the identification chain has no last step.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Randomization: What It Does and Does Not Buy}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Does}: makes $D_i$ independent of \emph{everything} about the
        unit, observed or not, so the selection-bias term is zero in expectation.
  \item \textbf{Does not}: make the two groups identical in your sample. Chance
        imbalance is real, and it is why we still adjust for pre-treatment
        covariates in experiments, for \emph{precision}, not for bias.
  \item \textbf{Does not}: rescue you from non-compliance, attrition, or
        interference. Randomizing the \emph{offer} identifies the effect of the
        offer (intention-to-treat), not the effect of the treatment, which is
        the LATE problem of Week 7.
  \item \alert{A balance table with $p$-values is not a check on randomization.}
        If assignment was truly random, any imbalance is chance by construction,
        and the $p$-values are uniform by definition. Report standardized
        differences instead, and use them to decide what to adjust for.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Identification Under Selection on Observables\core}
When randomization is unavailable, we assume it holds \emph{within cells of
observed covariates}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{block}{The four conditions}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Conditional independence} (unconfoundedness, ignorability):
        $\big(Y_i(0),Y_i(1)\big) \indep D_i \mid X_i$.
  \item \textbf{Positivity} (overlap): $0 < \Pr(D_i = 1\mid X_i = x) < 1$ for all
        $x$ with positive density.
  \item \textbf{Consistency}: $Y_i = Y_i(D_i)$, so the observed outcome \emph{is}
        the potential outcome for the treatment actually received.
  \item \textbf{SUTVA}, as above.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
Then the ATE is identified:
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATE} = \E\Big[\, \underbrace{\E[Y_i\mid D_i=1, X_i] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0, X_i]}_{\text{estimable from data}} \Big] .
\end{align*}
\vskip1pt
{\footnotesize\alert{Read the two lines carefully.} The left side is causal and
unobservable; the right side is a function of the observed joint distribution.
Assumptions 1--4 are the bridge, and they are not testable.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Positivity Is Really Saying\core}
$0 < \Pr(D_i=1 \mid X_i = x) < 1$: within every covariate cell, we must see
\emph{both} treated and untreated units.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item If no one with $X = x$ is ever treated, no data in the world tells you
        what would happen if they were. Extrapolation there is model, not
        evidence.
  \item Positivity fails silently when you add many covariates: with 10 binary
        controls there are 1{,}024 cells, and most will be empty or one-sided.
        \alert{This is the curse of dimensionality for causal inference}, and it
        is why Weeks 6 and 13 exist.
  \item Regression does not warn you. It fills empty cells by
        \emph{extrapolating the functional form}, quietly.
  \item Diagnose it: look at the distribution of the estimated propensity score
        in the two groups (Week 6).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Path Analysis to DAGs]{From Path Analysis to DAGs}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Causal Diagrams Are a Sociological Tradition}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The earliest formal treatment of causality in statistics was
        \textbf{Sewall Wright}'s \emph{path analysis} (1918, 1921).
  \item Sociology adopted it early. \textbf{Blau and Duncan}'s \textit{The
        American Occupational Structure} (1967) made path diagrams of status
        attainment, father's education and occupation $\to$ respondent's
        education $\to$ first job $\to$ current occupation, the discipline's
        central analytic image, and structural equation modelling grew out of it.
  \item But path analysis had \alert{no definition of a causal effect} and no rule
        for deciding which variables to include. The arrows were interpreted, not
        derived.
  \item \textbf{Judea Pearl} (1995) supplied the missing half: a formal graphical
        language, plus precise criteria, back-door, front-door, saying
        \emph{when} an effect is identified from observational data.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
So DAGs are not an import into sociology. They are our own tradition, finally
given its identification theory.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{What a DAG Is, and What It Leaves Out}
Write down how you think each variable is generated. For a treatment $D$, an
outcome $Y$, and a covariate $X$:
\begin{align*}
  X := \epsilon_X, \qquad
  D := f_D(X, \epsilon_D), \qquad
  Y := f_Y(D, X, \epsilon_Y) ,
\end{align*}
with the $\epsilon$'s mutually independent. Draw an arrow $A \to B$ whenever $A$
appears on the right of $B$'s equation.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.95,>=Stealth,thick,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1pt,minimum size=8mm,font=\small}]
  \node (D) at (0,0) {$D$}; \node (X) at (1.7,-1.4) {$X$}; \node (Y) at (3.4,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (D)--(Y); \draw[->,dukeblue] (X)--(D); \draw[->,dukeblue] (X)--(Y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item ``$:=$'' means \emph{is generated as}, not \emph{equals}: the equation is
        a claim about what would happen if we intervened, not a description of a
        correlation.
  \item \textbf{Acyclic}: no variable causes itself through a loop.
  \item \alert{The graph keeps only which arrows exist and drops the functional
        forms $f_D, f_Y$.} That loss is deliberate, everything below holds for
        \emph{any} functional form, linear or not.
  \item \alert{The assumptions are the \emph{missing} arrows}, not the drawn ones.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Same Variable Can Play Two Roles}
Suppose $X$ also causes $D$, and $D$ causes $M$ which causes $Y$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.9,>=Stealth,thick,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1pt,minimum size=8mm,font=\small}]
  \node (X) at (0,0) {$X$}; \node (D) at (2,0) {$D$};
  \node (M) at (4,0) {$M$}; \node (Y) at (6,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (X)--(D); \draw[->,dukeblue] (D)--(M); \draw[->,dukeblue] (M)--(Y);
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (X) to[bend right=28] (Y);
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (D) to[bend left=30] (Y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item If the target is the effect of $D$ on $Y$: $X$ is a \textbf{confounder}
        (control for it) and $M$ is a \textbf{mediator} (do not).
  \item If the target is the \emph{total} effect of $X$ on $Y$: $D$ is now a
        \textbf{mediator}, and controlling for it destroys the quantity you want.
  \item \alert{Nothing in the data distinguishes these cases.} The correct control
        set depends on which effect you want, so the estimand must be chosen
        before the specification.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
This is why ``I controlled for everything available'' is not a defensible
position. There is no control set that is correct for all questions at once.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Three Building Blocks\core}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1,>=Stealth,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1.6pt,minimum size=7mm,font=\small}]
  \node (a1) at (0,1.2) {$A$};  \node (b1) at (1.4,1.2) {$B$}; \node (c1) at (2.8,1.2) {$C$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue,thick] (a1)--(b1); \draw[->,dukeblue,thick] (b1)--(c1);
  \node[draw=none,fill=none,rectangle] at (1.4,0.3) {\footnotesize \textbf{chain} (mediator)};
  \node (b2) at (5.6,1.7) {$B$}; \node (a2) at (4.9,0.7) {$A$}; \node (c2) at (6.3,0.7) {$C$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue,thick] (b2)--(a2); \draw[->,dukeblue,thick] (b2)--(c2);
  \node[draw=none,fill=none,rectangle] at (5.6,0.0) {\footnotesize \textbf{fork} (confounder)};
  \node (a3) at (8.6,1.7) {$A$}; \node (c3) at (10.0,1.7) {$C$}; \node (b3) at (9.3,0.7) {$B$};
  \draw[->,accent,thick] (a3)--(b3); \draw[->,accent,thick] (c3)--(b3);
  \node[draw=none,fill=none,rectangle] at (9.3,0.0) {\footnotesize \textbf{collider}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Structure & $A$ and $C$ unconditionally & conditioning on $B$ \\
\midrule
Chain $A\to B\to C$ & associated & \alert{blocks} the path \\
Fork $A \leftarrow B \to C$ & associated & \alert{blocks} the path \\
Collider $A \to B \leftarrow C$ & \emph{independent} & \alert{opens} the path \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
The collider row is the one that surprises people, and it is the source of most
of the damage done by ``controlling for everything.''
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{$d$-Separation\core}
\begin{block}{Blocking}
A path between $D$ and $Y$ is \alert{blocked} by a set $Z$ if either
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item it contains a chain or fork whose middle node is \emph{in} $Z$, or
  \item it contains a collider that is \emph{not} in $Z$ and none of whose
        descendants is in $Z$.
\end{enumerate}
$D$ and $Y$ are \alert{$d$-separated} by $Z$ if $Z$ blocks every path between
them.
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $d$-separation is purely graphical; you can check it with a pencil.
  \item Its content is probabilistic: $d$-separation by $Z$ implies
        $D \indep Y \mid Z$ in \emph{every} distribution compatible with the graph.
  \item Note clause 2's tail: conditioning on a \emph{descendant} of a collider
        also opens the path, partially. Conditioning on a proxy for a collider is
        still conditioning on a collider.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Back-Door Criterion and the $do$-Operator\core}
\begin{block}{Back-door criterion \srcp{Pearl 1995}}
A set $Z$ satisfies the back-door criterion relative to the ordered pair $(D,Y)$
if
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $D$, and
  \item $Z$ blocks every path between $D$ and $Y$ containing an arrow into $D$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
If $Z$ satisfies it, the causal effect is identified by the
\alert{adjustment formula}:
\begin{align*}
  P\big(Y(d)\big) = P\big(Y \mid do(D=d)\big) = \int P(Y\mid D=d, Z=z)\, f(z)\,dz .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $do(D=d)$ means \emph{intervene} and set $D$ to $d$, as distinct from
        \emph{observing} $D=d$. Confusing the two is the entire problem.
  \item In words: average the observed conditional distribution of $Y$ among
        units with $D=d$ \emph{across strata of $Z$}, weighted by how common each
        stratum is in the whole population.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The First Law of Causal Inference\core}
\begin{alertblock}{Equivalence}
The DAG (with its structural equations) and the potential-outcomes framework
express the same underlying assumptions in two notations. A graph that satisfies
the back-door criterion \emph{delivers} conditional ignorability.
\end{alertblock}
\vskip3pt
Writing $D$ for the treatment, if $Z$ closes all back doors then
\begin{align*}
  Y(d) \indep D \mid Z ,
  \qquad
  \E[Y(d) \mid Z] = \E[Y(d)\mid D=d, Z] = \E[Y \mid D=d, Z] .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The last equality is \emph{consistency}: for units that actually received
        $d$, the potential outcome $Y(d)$ is the observed $Y$.
  \item The chain of three expressions is worth memorizing. The first is causal
        and unobservable; the last is estimable; the middle step is where the
        assumption lives.
  \item Equivalently: conditioning on $Z$ \emph{$d$-separates} the realized
        treatment $D$ from the potential outcome $Y(d)$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why This Beats ``Control for Anything Predictive''}
\srct{Wooldridge (2005)} notes the temptation: ``if `good' is taken to mean
`best fit', it is tempting to include anything in $X_i$ that helps predict
treatment.''
\vskip3pt
\begin{center}

  \item \textbf{Scenario 1} ($Z$ predicts only $D$): including $Z$ removes

\end{itemize}

\section[Colliders]{When Conditioning Goes Wrong}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{The Intuition First\core}
Admission to a selective college requires either high test scores or athletic
talent; the two are independent in the population.
\vskip1pt
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8,>=Stealth,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1pt,minimum size=7.5mm,font=\scriptsize}]
  \node (t) at (0,1.2) {test};  \node (a) at (3.2,1.2) {athl.};
  \node[draw=accent,fill=accent!12] (adm) at (1.6,0) {admit};
  \draw[->,dukeblue,thick] (t)--(adm); \draw[->,dukeblue,thick] (a)--(adm);
  \draw[<->,gray,dashed] (t) to[bend left=20] node[draw=none,fill=none,above,font=\scriptsize,text=gray] {independent} (a);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip1pt
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item In the population, test scores tell you nothing about athletic talent.
  \item \alert{Among admitted students}: someone with mediocre scores must have
        got in some other way, so they are probably a strong athlete. A
        \emph{negative} association appears out of nowhere.
  \item Berkson's paradox, the same mechanism behind ``selection bias'' and
        the birth-weight paradox.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Now the Algebra\adv}
Consider the structural model
\begin{align*}
  T := \epsilon_T, \qquad B := \epsilon_B, \qquad C := T + B + \epsilon_C ,
\end{align*}
with $\epsilon_T,\epsilon_B,\epsilon_C$ independent $\mathcal N(0,1)$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.85,>=Stealth,thick,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1pt,minimum size=7.5mm,font=\small}]
  \node (B) at (0,0) {$B$};  \node (T) at (2.6,0) {$T$};
  \node[draw=accent] (C) at (1.3,-1.5) {$C$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (B)--(C); \draw[->,dukeblue] (T)--(C);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item $B$ and $T$ are independent by construction:
        $\E[T] = 0$ and $\E[T\mid B=b] = 0$ for every $b$.
  \item There is \alert{no arrow} between them, and no common cause.
  \item Question: what is $\E[T \mid B, C]$?
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conditioning on the Collider\adv}
Because $C = T + B + \epsilon_C$, we can write $T = (C-B) - \epsilon_C$. So
$\E[T\mid B,C]$ is the linear projection of $T$ on $C-B$:
\begin{align*}
  \E[T \mid B, C]
  &= \frac{\Cov\big(T,\ C-B\big)}{\Var\big(C-B\big)}\,(C-B)
   = \frac{\Cov\big(T,\ T+\epsilon_C\big)}{\Var\big(T+\epsilon_C\big)}\,(C-B) \\[3pt]
  &= \frac{1}{1+1}\,(C-B) \;=\; \tfrac12 (C-B) .
\end{align*}
\pause
Read the coefficient on $B$ straight off that conditional expectation:
\begin{align*}
  \E[T \mid B, C] = \tfrac12 C \;-\; \tfrac12 B
  \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \frac{\partial\,\E[T\mid B,C]}{\partial B} = -\tfrac12 .
\end{align*}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Conditioning on the Collider: What It Means}
\begin{alertblock}{}
Controlling for $C$, the apparent effect of $B$ on $T$ is $-1/2$: entirely
spurious, since nothing in the model connects $B$ to $T$, and indeed
$\E[T\mid B] = 0$ for every $B$.
\end{alertblock}
\vskip6pt
This is \alert{collider bias}, the same object as Heckman's sample-selection
bias.
\vskip8pt
\begin{block}{Read the \emph{partial} coefficient, not the average}
Averaging $\tfrac12(C-b)$ over $C$ given $B=b$ gives zero, because
$\E[C\mid B=b]=b$. The bias lives in the comparison at \emph{fixed} $C$, which
is exactly what ``controlling for $C$'' does.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Damage Spreads to Ancestors}
Conditioning on a collider opens associations among its parents \alert{and all
of their ancestors}.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.8,>=Stealth,thick,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1pt,minimum size=7.5mm,font=\scriptsize}]
  \node (B) at (0,0) {$B$};  \node (T) at (2.6,0) {$T$};
  \node[draw=accent] (C) at (1.3,-1.5) {$C$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (B)--(C); \draw[->,dukeblue] (T)--(C);
  \draw[<->,accent,dashed] (B) to[bend left=35] (T);
  % right panel
  \node (X2) at (6.0,0) {$X$}; \node (B2) at (8.0,0) {$B$};
  \node (T2) at (10.6,0) {$T$}; \node (Y2) at (12.6,0) {$Y$};
  \node[draw=accent] (C2) at (9.3,-1.5) {$C$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (X2)--(B2); \draw[->,dukeblue] (B2)--(C2);
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (T2)--(C2); \draw[->,dukeblue] (Y2)--(T2);
  \draw[<->,accent,dashed] (X2) to[bend left=28] (Y2);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item On the right, $X$ and $Y$ are four steps apart and have no common cause.
        Conditioning on $C$ makes them dependent anyway.
  \item Practical consequence: a control variable can induce bias between
        variables it is not adjacent to. You cannot check for this by inspecting
        correlations, only by tracing paths.
  \item And conditioning on a \emph{descendant} of $C$ does a milder version of
        the same thing.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Birth-Weight Paradox}
A famous puzzle from a 1991 US study.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Infants born to smokers have a higher risk of low birth weight (LBW).
  \item Infants born to smokers have higher infant mortality.
  \item \alert{But among LBW infants, mortality is \emph{lower} for infants of
        smokers than for infants of non-smokers.}
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
The naive reading is that smoking is protective conditional on LBW, which
would be a remarkable public-health finding.
\vskip3pt
The plausible reading is that \textbf{low birth weight is a collider}. Both
smoking and unobserved competing risks (congenital abnormalities, severe
maternal illness) cause LBW, and those competing risks are far more lethal than
smoking. Among LBW babies, ``not a smoker'' becomes evidence for ``something
worse.''
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Birth-Weight Paradox, Structurally\adv}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.72,>=Stealth,thick,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1pt,minimum size=7.5mm,font=\scriptsize}]
  \node (S) at (0,1.6) {$S$};  \node[draw=accent] (B) at (2.0,0) {$B$};
  \node (Y) at (4.6,0) {$Y$};  \node[draw=gray,dashed] (U) at (0,-1.6) {$U$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (S)--(B); \draw[->,dukeblue] (S) to[bend left=20] (Y);
  \draw[->,gray] (U)--(B); \draw[->,gray] (U) to[bend right=20] (Y);
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (B)--(Y);
  % conditioned version
  \node (S2) at (8.4,1.6) {$S$};  \node[draw=accent,fill=accent!12] (B2) at (10.4,0) {$B$};
  \node (Y2) at (13.0,0) {$Y$};  \node[draw=gray,dashed] (U2) at (8.4,-1.6) {$U$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (S2)--(B2); \draw[->,dukeblue] (S2) to[bend left=20] (Y2);
  \draw[->,gray] (U2)--(B2); \draw[->,gray] (U2) to[bend right=20] (Y2);
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (B2)--(Y2);
  \draw[<->,accent,dashed] (S2) to[bend right=45] (U2);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{align*}
  Y := S + B + \kappa U + \epsilon_Y, \qquad
  B := S + U + \epsilon_B, \qquad
  S := \epsilon_S, \qquad U := \epsilon_U ,
\end{align*}
with independent $\mathcal N(0,1)$ shocks. Smoking has a \emph{harmful} total
effect: direct $1$, plus indirect $1\times1$ through birth weight, total
$\bm{+2}$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the Sign Flips\adv}
Projecting $Y$ on $S$ alone recovers the correct total effect of $+2$.
\vskip3pt
Projecting $Y$ on $S$ \emph{and} $B$, ``controlling for birth weight'', 
gives the conditional expectation
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y \mid S, B] \;=\; \big(1 - \kappa/2\big)\,S \;+\; \big(1 + \kappa/2\big)\,B .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The coefficient on $S$ is no longer the direct effect of $1$. It has
        acquired a $-\kappa/2$ term, which comes entirely from the induced
        negative association between $S$ and $U$ within strata of $B$.
  \item If the competing risks are much more lethal than smoking, $\kappa \gg 1$
, the coefficient on smoking becomes \alert{large and negative}. Smoking
        appears strongly protective.
  \item \alert{Nothing is wrong with the estimator.} The regression faithfully
        reports a conditional expectation. The error is in reading that
        conditional expectation as a causal effect.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Selection Bias Is Collider Bias\core}
The most common invisible collider in sociology is \emph{the sample itself}.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Wage regressions on employed workers}: employment is caused by
        both schooling and the unobserved determinants of wages. Restricting to
        the employed conditions on a collider.
  \item \textbf{Studies of college students, surviving firms, intact marriages,
        surviving states}: every one is a conditioning operation.
  \item \textbf{Panel attrition}: staying in the sample is an outcome.
  \item \textbf{``Among those who report X''}: reporting is behaviour.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{The practical rule}
Draw the selection mechanism into your DAG as a node you have conditioned on,
with a box around it. Then read off whether it opens anything. If your sample
was constructed by any process downstream of treatment or outcome, assume it did.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Controls]{Good Controls and Bad Controls}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Taxonomy\core}
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Variable & Role & Control for it? \\
\midrule
Common cause of $D$ and $Y$ & confounder & \textbf{yes}; this is the point \\
Pre-treatment predictor of $Y$ only & precision variable & \textbf{yes}, reduces variance \\
Pre-treatment predictor of $D$ only & instrument-like & \emph{no}, inflates variance,
  amplifies bias \\
Consequence of $D$ on the path to $Y$ & mediator & \alert{no}, removes part of the effect \\
Common effect of $D$ and $Y$ & collider & \alert{no}, creates spurious association \\
Consequence of $Y$ & outcome proxy & \alert{no} \\
Sample selection on a collider & implicit conditioning & \alert{no}, and often invisible \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item Full taxonomy in \srct{Cinelli, Forney, and Pearl (2024)}.
  \item Note the third row again: conditioning on a strong predictor of $D$ with
        no direct effect on $Y$ \emph{amplifies} whatever unmeasured confounding
        remains. ``More controls'' is not conservative.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Post-Treatment Variables, and How They Sneak In}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Explicitly adjusting for a post-treatment variable is almost always a
        mistake, and it is a mistake even when the coefficient ``looks better.''
  \item \alert{The greater danger is implicit conditioning}, through sample
        construction and variable definition rather than through the regression
        formula:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
    \item estimating the effect of education on wages using only \emph{employed}
          individuals, employment is post-treatment;
    \item studying occupational outcomes among people who \emph{have} an
          occupation;
    \item defining the outcome as a ratio whose denominator responds to
          treatment;
    \item restricting to respondents who completed a follow-up wave.
  \end{itemize}
  \item None of these appears in the model equation. All of them are conditioning.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Two Sharper Cases}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Mediators and the controlled direct effect}
\vskip1pt

$D \to M \leftarrow U \to Y$. Direct effects need \emph{stronger} assumptions
than total effects.}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{Consequences of the outcome}
\vskip1pt
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=0.72,>=Stealth,thick,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1pt,minimum size=7mm,font=\scriptsize}]

  \draw[->,dukeblue] (D)--(Y); \draw[->,dukeblue] (Y)--(Z);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip2pt
{\small $Z$ happens \emph{after} $Y$ and is caused by it. Controlling for it
partials out variation in the outcome itself and biases the estimate. Which
direction depends on the rest of the graph, so do not assume it is conservative.
Common as ``controlling for'' a downstream measure of the same construct.}
\end{column}

distinction is causal, and it must come from the DAG.

\section[Front Door]{The Front-Door Criterion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification Without Measuring the Confounder}
Sometimes no back door can be closed, but the \emph{mechanism} can be measured.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0,>=Stealth,thick,
  every node/.style={circle,draw=dukeblue,fill=white,inner sep=1pt,minimum size=8.5mm,font=\scriptsize}]
  \node[draw=gray,dashed] (u) at (2.4,1.5) {$U$};
  \node (d) at (0.0,0) {$D$};  \node (m) at (2.4,0) {$M$};  \node (y) at (4.8,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[->,dukeblue] (d)--(m); \draw[->,dukeblue] (m)--(y);
  \draw[->,gray,dashed] (u)--(d); \draw[->,gray,dashed] (u)--(y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\vskip-4pt
\begin{block}{The three conditions}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Complete mediation}: every directed path from $D$ to $Y$ passes
        through $M$.
  \item \textbf{No unblocked back door from $D$ to $M$}.
  \item \textbf{All back doors from $M$ to $Y$ are blocked by $D$}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
$U$ is unobserved and confounds $D$ and $Y$, yet the effect is identified.
That is a genuinely surprising result, and worth understanding rather than
memorizing.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why It Works: Two Identified Legs}
The trick is to split $D \to Y$ into two effects, each of which \emph{is}
identified.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Leg 1, $D \to M$}: by condition 2 there is no back door, so
        $P(M \mid do(D=d)) = P(M\mid D=d)$. We can read the effect of $D$ on $M$
        straight off the data.
  \item \textbf{Leg 2, $M \to Y$}: the only back door from $M$ to $Y$ is
        $M \leftarrow D \leftarrow U \to Y$, and conditioning on $D$ blocks it.
        So the effect of $M$ on $Y$ is identified by adjusting for $D$.
  \item \textbf{Compose}: the effect of $D$ on $Y$ is the effect of $D$ on $M$,
        pushed through the effect of $M$ on $Y$.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\alert{Neither leg requires observing $U$.} The confounder sits outside both
sub-problems, and that is the whole trick.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{The Front-Door Formula\adv}
\begin{align*}
  P\big(Y(d)\big) &= P\big(Y \mid do(D=d)\big) \\
  &= \sum_m P\big(Y \mid do(D=d), M=m\big)\, P\big(M=m \mid do(D=d)\big) \\[2pt]
  &= \sum_m \underbrace{P\big(Y \mid do(M=m)\big)}_{\text{leg 2}}\,
            \underbrace{P\big(M=m \mid D=d\big)}_{\text{leg 1}} \\[2pt]
  &= \sum_m P(M=m\mid D=d)\left[\sum_{d'} P\big(Y \mid M=m, D=d'\big)\,P(D=d')\right] .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Line 3 uses complete mediation (given $M$, $D$ no longer matters for $Y$)
        and condition 2.
  \item The bracket in line 4 is the back-door adjustment for leg 2, averaging
        over the \emph{marginal} distribution of $D$, note $d'$, not $d$.
  \item Everything on the right is estimable. $U$ never appears.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{A Sociological Application: the Age--Period--Cohort Problem}
To see what front-door reasoning actually buys, take a problem sociologists know
well, and one where the regression coefficient cannot even be \emph{defined}
uniquely: separating age, period, and cohort.
\vskip3pt
The standard APC regression is
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha + \beta_A A_i + \beta_P P_t + \beta_C C_i + \epsilon_{it} ,
\end{align*}
with $A$ = age, $P$ = calendar period, $C$ = birth cohort.
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{The identification problem}
$C = P - A$ exactly. The three regressors are \emph{perfectly collinear}, so
$\beta_A, \beta_P, \beta_C$ are not separately identified, for any candidate
solution there is a one-parameter family of alternatives fitting equally well.
\end{alertblock}
\vskip3pt
Decades of proposed fixes, constrained estimators, the intrinsic estimator,
arbitrary equality restrictions, amount to choosing a point on that family by
assumption. None of them is identification; they are conventions.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mechanism-Based Identification for APC}
Suppose we want the effect of \emph{historical period} on attitudes. Rather than
fight the collinearity, look for the mechanism.
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Period ($D$) affects exposure to new information, institutions, media
        environments, or policies ($M$).
  \item Those exposures shape individual attitudes ($Y$).
  \item If we can measure \emph{all} the channels $M$ through which period
        operates, the front-door formula identifies the period effect:
\end{itemize}
\begin{align*}
  P\big(Y \mid do(D)\big)
  = \sum_m P(M=m\mid D)\, \sum_{d'} P\big(Y\mid M=m, D=d'\big) P(D=d') .
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\alert{The collinearity is not the binding constraint} once we stop asking for a
coefficient in a linear model and start asking for a causal quantity with a
stated mechanism.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Critiques of Front-Door Identification}
Be sceptical of front-door claims, including your own.
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Complete mediation is unrealistic.} Every effect of $D$ on $Y$
        must pass through the \emph{observed} $M$. A single unmediated path
        breaks identification, and in the APC case period plausibly acts through
        many unmeasured channels at once.
  \item \textbf{No hidden confounding of the legs.} We still need $D \to M$
        unconfounded, and $M \to Y$ identified by adjusting for $D$ alone. If
        something unobserved drives both the mechanism and the outcome, the
        second leg fails.
  \item \textbf{Measurement.} $M$ must be measured well, and note \emph{where} the
        error bites. In leg 1, $M$ is the outcome, so classical error adds noise
        without attenuating $P(M\mid D)$. In leg 2, $M$ is the variable being
        intervened on and conditioned on, so error there biases that leg and
        therefore the composed estimate.
  \item \textbf{Sensitivity.} Because the assumptions are strong, report how the
        estimate moves as you allow a small unmediated path.
\end{itemize}
\vskip2pt
The front door is a real result and a rarely satisfied one. Its main value is
clarifying \emph{what you would need to know} to make a mechanism argument work.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Potential Outcomes and DAGs Are Complements}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Potential outcomes}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item Precise about the \emph{estimand}: ATE, ATT, LATE.
  \item Natural home for heterogeneity, effect distributions, and design-based
        inference.
  \item Clumsy for reasoning about long chains of variables.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{DAGs}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item Precise about \emph{identification}: which paths are open, what to
        condition on.
  \item Makes collider and mediator mistakes visible immediately.
  \item Silent about magnitude, heterogeneity, and which average you get.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip6pt
\begin{block}{Use both}
Draw the DAG to work out \emph{what to condition on}. Write the potential
outcomes to state \emph{what you are estimating}. The First Law says they are
the same theory; in practice each makes a different mistake easy to catch.
\end{block}
\end{frame}
\section[Estimands]{Defining a Well-Posed Estimand}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Standard Workflow Skips a Step\core}
\srct{Lundberg, Johnson, and Stewart (2021)} argue that most quantitative papers
jump straight from theory to a regression, leaving the target quantity implicit.
Their proposed sequence:
\vskip4pt
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Theoretical estimand.} A \emph{unit-specific quantity} and a
        \emph{target population}. ``The average difference in earnings at age 40
        between completing college and not, among US-born adults who completed
        high school.'' Stated before any data.
  \item \textbf{Empirical estimand.} What identification assumptions turn the
        theoretical estimand into a function of observable distributions? This is
        the back-door argument.
  \item \textbf{Estimation strategy.} Only now: regression, matching, weighting,
        IV, machine learning.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip4pt
\alert{Most methodological disputes are really disagreements at step 1 or 2}
being conducted as if they were about step 3.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Why This Discipline Pays}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item It forces you to notice when the estimand is chosen \emph{by the method}
        rather than by you: OLS's variance-weighted average (Week 1), the LATE
        for compliers (Week 7), the effect at the cutoff (Week 9).
  \item It makes ``the coefficient on $X_3$ in Model 4'', a quantity nobody
        can state in words, an unacceptable answer.
  \item It separates two very different objections to a paper: ``your estimand is
        uninteresting'' and ``your estimate does not recover your estimand.''
  \item It clarifies external validity: the target population is part of the
        estimand, so extrapolation is a stated claim rather than an implicit one.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{A habit worth forming}
Write the theoretical estimand as one sentence, in words, before you write any
code. You will be asked to do exactly this on the final exam, and in every paper
you referee afterwards.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[allowframebreaks,t]{Where This Leaves Us}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item Causal inference is a missing data problem. We target averages because
        individual effects are unknowable.
  \item The naive comparison equals the ATT plus selection bias. Every design in
        this course is an argument \emph{about} that term: either that adjustment
        drives it to zero (Week 6), or that some other comparison sidesteps it
        entirely, at the price of a narrower estimand (IV's LATE, Weeks 7--11).
  \item Conditional ignorability, positivity, consistency, and SUTVA identify the
        ATE from observable quantities. \alert{None of them is testable.}
  \item DAGs turn those assumptions into a picture you can check for colliders
        and mediators.
\end{itemize}
