\begin{frame}{From Estimation to Causal Questions}
Weeks 1--4 were about \emph{estimation}: given a target parameter, how do we
estimate it and how uncertain are we? OLS estimates the best linear
approximation to a conditional expectation; maximum likelihood estimates the
parameters of a probability model. Neither says the word ``effect.''
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{Today's question}
What are we trying to estimate, and what would have to be true about the
world for our estimate to recover it?
\end{alertblock}
\vskip4pt
Two languages answer it, and they are complementary:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Potential outcomes}: what would have happened to each unit
        under each treatment. Precise about the estimand.
  \item \textbf{Directed acyclic graphs}: how the variables cause one another.
        Precise about what to condition on.
\end{itemize}
Everything in Weeks 6--13 applies one or both.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification Is Not Estimation}
\begin{block}{Manski (1995)}
``Studies of identification seek to characterize the conclusions that could
be drawn if one could obtain an unlimited number of observations.
Identification problems cannot be solved by gathering more of the same kind
of data.''
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Estimation} (Weeks 1--4): uncertainty from a finite sample;
        it shrinks as $n$ grows.
  \item \textbf{Identification} (today): whether the quantity you want is
        pinned down by the distribution of what you observe, even with
        $n = \infty$. If not, only an assumption resolves the ambiguity.
  \item \textbf{Identification analysis}: state assumptions about how the
        data were generated and show they carry you from observed to
        potential outcomes. An \textbf{identification strategy} is a
        labelled source of variation in the treatment plus the technique
        that exploits it \srcp{Angrist and Krueger 1999}.
\end{itemize}
The rest of the course is identification analysis, design by design.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Worlds Per Person\core}
For a binary treatment $D_i \in \{0,1\}$, each unit $i$ has \emph{two}
outcomes written into it before treatment is assigned:
\begin{align*}
  Y_i(1) &= \text{the outcome if unit } i \text{ were treated,} \\
  Y_i(0) &= \text{the outcome if unit } i \text{ were not treated.}
\end{align*}
The \alert{individual treatment effect} is $\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$.
We observe only one of the two:
\begin{align*}
  \boxed{\;Y_i = D_i\,Y_i(1) + (1-D_i)\,Y_i(0)\;}
  \qquad\text{(the \emph{switching equation})}
\end{align*}
\begin{block}{Holland's fundamental problem of causal inference \srcp{Holland 1986}}
$\tau_i$ is never observed for anyone. Causal inference is a \alert{missing
data problem}, and every method in this course is a strategy for filling in
the missing column.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Missing Data View}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{cccccc}
\toprule
Unit & $D_i$ & $Y_i(0)$ & $Y_i(1)$ & $\tau_i$ & $Y_i$ (observed) \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 12 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 12 \\
2 & 0 & 7 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 7 \\
3 & 1 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 9  & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 9 \\
4 & 0 & 5 & \textcolor{accent}{?} & \textcolor{accent}{?} & 5 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip4pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Half the table is missing \emph{by construction}, not by bad luck. No
        larger sample fixes it.
  \item So we give up on $\tau_i$ and target \emph{averages}, which are
        estimable under assumptions.
  \item ``No causation without manipulation'' \srcp{Holland 1986}: if you
        cannot even imagine intervening to set $D_i$, the potential outcomes
        are not well defined. ``The effect of race'' or ``of gender'' needs
        restating as the effect of a manipulable treatment, such as the race
        signalled on a job application \srcp{VanderWeele and Robinson 2014}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimands: Say Which Average You Want\core}
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATE} &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)] && \text{average treatment effect} \\
  \text{ATT} &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 1] && \text{on the treated} \\
  \text{ATU} &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid D_i = 0] && \text{on the untreated} \\
  \text{CATE}(x) &= \E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \mid X_i = x] && \text{conditional on covariates}
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $\text{ATE} = \pi\,\text{ATT} + (1-\pi)\,\text{ATU}$, where
        $\pi = \Pr(D_i=1)$.
  \item They differ whenever effects are heterogeneous \emph{and} related to
        who gets treated, which is the normal case in sociology.
  \item ``Should we expand this program?'' asks about the ATU. ``Was it
        worth running?'' asks about the ATT.
  \item Later we meet estimands nobody chose: the LATE (Week 7), the effect
        at a cutoff (Week 9), OLS's own variance-weighted average (Week 6).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimands, With Numbers\core}
Four units, effects that differ, and treatment related to the effect:
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{tabular}{cccccc}
\toprule
Unit & $D_i$ & $Y_i(0)$ & $Y_i(1)$ & $\tau_i$ & $Y_i$ observed \\
\midrule
1 & 1 & 4 & 10 & 6 & 10 \\
2 & 1 & 6 & 14 & 8 & 14 \\
3 & 0 & 5 & 6  & 1 & 5 \\
4 & 0 & 7 & 10 & 3 & 7 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATE} &= \tfrac14(6+8+1+3) = 4.5, &
  \text{ATT} &= \tfrac12(6+8) = 7, &
  \text{ATU} &= \tfrac12(1+3) = 2 .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item The naive comparison is $\tfrac12(10+14) - \tfrac12(5+7) = 6$,
        which equals none of them.
  \item ATT $+$ selection bias
        $= 7 + \big(\tfrac12(4+6) - \tfrac12(5+7)\big) = 7 - 1 = 6$.
        \checkmark
  \item $\pi\,$ATT $+ (1-\pi)\,$ATU $= 0.5(7)+0.5(2) = 4.5 = $ ATE.
        \checkmark
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why the Naive Comparison Fails\core}
The quantity we can compute is the difference in observed means. By the
switching equation it is $\E[Y_i(1)\mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0]$;
add and subtract $\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1]$:
\begin{align*}
  \underbrace{\E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{naive comparison}}
  &= \underbrace{\E[Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1]}_{\text{ATT}} \\
  &\quad + \underbrace{\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0]}_{\alert{\text{selection bias}}}
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Selection bias asks: \emph{would the treated and untreated have
        differed even with no treatment?} Almost always yes.
  \item It involves \alert{only} $Y_i(0)$, the world as it would have been for
        the treated. No amount of data on $Y_i$ can settle it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Same Decomposition, Around the ATE}
Write $\tau = \text{ATE}$ and $\pi = \Pr(D_i = 1)$. Since
$\text{ATT} = \tau + (1 - \pi)\big(\text{ATT} - \text{ATU}\big)$,
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0]
  = \tau
  &+ \underbrace{\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{selection on the baseline } Y_i(0)} \\
  &+ \underbrace{(1-\pi)\big(\text{ATT} - \text{ATU}\big)}_{\text{selection on the effect } \tau_i} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Two ways to be wrong about the ATE: the treated would have done
        differently anyway, or the treated gain more from treatment. The
        four-unit table had both: baseline gap $-1$, effect gap $7 - 2 = 5$,
        so $6 = 4.5 - 1 + 0.5 \times 5$.
  \item The second term is why ``the treated are those who benefit most''
        is an identification problem, not a nuisance. Week 7's LATE is what
        remains estimable when it is present.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{In the Literature: Negative Selection \srcp{Brand and Xie 2010}}
Treatment: completing a four-year degree. Outcome: log hourly wage. Data:
NLSY79 ($n = 2{,}474$) and the Wisconsin Longitudinal Study ($n = 7{,}905$).
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Estimate each person's propensity to finish college from pre-college
        covariates (family background, measured ability, grades, aspirations);
        group people into balanced propensity strata; estimate the college
        effect within each stratum.
  \item The effect \emph{falls} with the propensity. NLSY men at ages 29--32:
        about $30\%$ higher wages in the lowest stratum, $10\%$ in the
        highest, $5$ points less per stratum. NLSY women at 37--40: $35\%$
        to $20\%$. Same sign in the Wisconsin cohort.
  \item In today's notation: $\tau_i$ is largest where $\Pr(D_i = 1 \mid X_i)$
        is smallest, so $\text{ATU} > \text{ATT}$. The comparative-advantage
        story from economics predicts the opposite ordering.
  \item Consequence: the treated are the wrong people to learn the ATE from,
        and a policy that expands college reaches the ATU population.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Regression on $D$ Is the Naive Comparison\core}
Write the switching equation as a regression, with $\tau = \text{ATE}$ and
$\alpha = \E[Y_i(0)]$:
\begin{align*}
  Y_i &= Y_i(0) + \tau_i D_i && \text{\footnotesize switching equation} \\
      &= \alpha + \tau D_i
         + \underbrace{\big(Y_i(0) - \alpha\big) + (\tau_i - \tau)\,D_i}_{\varepsilon_i}
         && \text{\footnotesize add and subtract } \alpha \text{ and } \tau D_i
\end{align*}
The error holds the unit's baseline and its excess gain. OLS of $Y$ on $D$
returns $\bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0$, the naive comparison, and
{\small
\begin{align*}
  \E[\varepsilon_i \mid D_i = 1] - \E[\varepsilon_i \mid D_i = 0]
  &= \underbrace{\E[Y_i(0) \mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0]}_{\text{baseline selection}}
  + \underbrace{(1-\pi)(\text{ATT}-\text{ATU})}_{\text{selection on the effect}}
\end{align*}}
using $\E[\tau_i - \tau \mid D_i = 1] = \text{ATT} - \tau = (1-\pi)(\text{ATT} - \text{ATU})$.
Weeks 1--4 treated $\E[\varepsilon_i \mid D_i] = 0$ as a modelling
assumption. Today it is a claim about the world: who gets treated is
unrelated to what would have happened to them either way.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Randomization Solves It\core}
If $D_i$ is assigned by a coin flip, it is independent of everything about
the unit, including its potential outcomes:
\begin{align*}
  \big(Y_i(0), Y_i(1)\big) \indep D_i .
\end{align*}
Then $\E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=1] = \E[Y_i(0)\mid D_i=0]$, the selection-bias term
is zero, and
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_i\mid D_i=1] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0] = \text{ATE} = \text{ATT} .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Randomization does not make the groups identical; it makes them
        \emph{exchangeable}, so any difference is chance. Experiments still
        adjust for pre-treatment covariates, for precision rather than bias.
  \item Randomizing an \emph{offer} identifies the effect of the offer, not
        of the treatment taken up (Week 7).
  \item Every observational design tries to buy some version of this
        independence with an argument instead of a coin.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimating the ATE in an Experiment \srcp{Neyman 1923}}
$n_1$ treated, $n_0$ controls, $D_i$ by coin flip. Estimator
$\hat\tau = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0$.
\begin{align*}
  \E[\bar Y_1] &= \E[Y_i(1) \mid D_i = 1] = \E[Y_i(1)]
      && \text{\footnotesize consistency, then } D_i \indep Y_i(1) \\
  \E[\hat\tau] &= \E[Y_i(1)] - \E[Y_i(0)] = \text{ATE}
      && \text{\footnotesize same for } \bar Y_0 \\
  \widehat{\Var}(\hat\tau) &= \frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_0^2}{n_0}
      && \text{\footnotesize } s_d^2 = \text{sample variance of } Y \text{ in arm } d
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Conservative: the true variance has a third term, $-\Var(\tau_i)/n$,
        which no data can estimate because no unit shows both outcomes.
        Exact when effects are constant.
  \item It equals the HC2 robust variance from OLS of $Y$ on $D$ (Week 2),
        so \texttt{lm} plus a robust SE is Neyman's estimator
        \srcp{Samii and Aronow 2012}.
  \item Simulated experiment, $n = 2{,}000$, ATE $= 4.03$: $\hat\tau = 4.08$,
        SE $= 0.116$. Over $2{,}000$ re-randomizations the estimates have SD
        $0.102$ and the SE averages $0.115$: conservative, as promised.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{SUTVA and Consistency: What the Notation Assumes\core}
Writing ``$Y_i(1)$'' at all assumes the \alert{stable unit treatment value
assumption}:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{No interference}: unit $i$'s outcome does not depend on anyone
        else's treatment. Otherwise each unit has $2^n$ potential outcomes.
  \item \textbf{No hidden versions of treatment}: ``$D_i = 1$'' means the
        same thing for everyone, or $Y_i(1)$ is ambiguous.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip2pt
\textbf{Consistency} is the link from notation to data: for a unit that
received $d$, the observed outcome \emph{is} its potential outcome under $d$,
$Y_i = Y_i(d)$ when $D_i = d$. It fails when the treatment has hidden
versions, and under anticipation.
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Sociology is full of interference: peer effects, neighbourhood
        effects, diffusion. If everyone gets a degree, the return to a degree
        changes.
  \item Hidden versions: ``college'' covers a community-college certificate
        and a Princeton BA; ``marriage'' in 1960 and in 2020.
  \item State SUTVA and defend it, or redefine the treatment.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Beyond a Binary Treatment}
Nothing above needs two arms. For a treatment $d$ in a set $\mathcal{D}$,
unit $i$ has $Y_i(d)$ for every $d$ and reveals $Y_i = Y_i(D_i)$.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Years of schooling: $Y_i(12), Y_i(16), \ldots$ ``The effect of
        college'' is $\E[Y_i(16) - Y_i(12)]$, one contrast among many. A
        regression slope on years averages all the contrasts with weights
        nobody chose.
  \item Continuous dose: $d \mapsto \E[Y_i(d)]$ is the dose-response curve.
        Positivity now asks for every dose inside every covariate cell.
  \item The estimands and the selection-bias decomposition go through one
        contrast at a time, so the course stays with $D_i \in \{0,1\}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification Under Selection on Observables\core}
Without randomization, assume it holds \emph{within cells of observed
covariates}.
\begin{block}{The four conditions}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{Conditional independence} (ignorability, unconfoundedness):
        $\big(Y_i(0),Y_i(1)\big) \indep D_i \mid X_i$.
  \item \textbf{Positivity} (overlap): $0 < \Pr(D_i = 1\mid X_i = x) < 1$
        wherever $x$ occurs.
  \item \textbf{Consistency}: $Y_i = Y_i(D_i)$.
  \item \textbf{SUTVA}: one unit's outcome does not depend on others'
        treatments, and ``treated'' means one thing for everyone.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
Then the ATE is identified:
\begin{align*}
  \text{ATE} = \E\Big[\, \underbrace{\E[Y_i\mid D_i=1, X_i] - \E[Y_i\mid D_i=0, X_i]}_{\text{estimable from data}} \Big] .
\end{align*}
The left side is causal and unobservable; the right side is a function of the
observed data. Conditions 1--4 are the bridge, and \alert{none is testable}.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Proof, Line by Line\core}
Fix a covariate value $x$ and work inside the cell $X_i = x$:
{\footnotesize
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1, X_i = x] &= \E[Y_i(1) \mid D_i = 1, X_i = x]
      && \text{\footnotesize consistency} \\
  &= \E[Y_i(1) \mid X_i = x]
      && \text{\footnotesize conditional independence} \\
  \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0, X_i = x] &= \E[Y_i(0) \mid X_i = x]
      && \text{\footnotesize same two steps} \\
  \text{CATE}(x) &= \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1, X_i = x] - \E[Y_i \mid D_i = 0, X_i = x]
      && \text{\footnotesize subtract} \\
  \text{ATE} &= \textstyle\sum_x \text{CATE}(x)\,\Pr(X_i = x)
      && \text{\footnotesize iterated expectations}
\end{align*}}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Positivity enters in line 1: $\E[Y_i \mid D_i = 1, X_i = x]$ exists
        only if someone with $X_i = x$ is treated.
  \item Line 5 weights cells by how common they are; the ATT weights them by
        $\Pr(X_i = x \mid D_i = 1)$ instead.
  \item In missing-data language: the unobserved potential outcome is
        missing at random given $X_i$. Week 6 is line 4 when $X$ has too
        many cells to take means.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Positivity Is Saying\core}
$0 < \Pr(D_i=1 \mid X_i = x) < 1$: within every covariate cell we must see
\emph{both} treated and untreated units.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item If no one with $X = x$ is ever treated, no data in the world says
        what would happen if they were. Extrapolation there is model, not
        evidence.
  \item Positivity fails silently as covariates accumulate: with 10 binary
        controls there are 1{,}024 cells, and most are empty or one-sided.
        This is the curse of dimensionality for causal inference, and why
        Weeks 6 and 13 exist.
  \item Regression does not warn you. It fills empty cells by extrapolating
        the functional form.
  \item Diagnose it by looking at the estimated propensity score in the two
        groups (Week 6).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section[Path Analysis to DAGs]{From Path Analysis to DAGs}

\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Causal Diagrams Are a Sociological Tradition}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item The first formal treatment of causality in statistics was
        \textbf{Sewall Wright}'s path analysis \srcp{Wright 1921}.
  \item Sociology adopted it early. \textbf{Blau and Duncan} (1967) drew
        status attainment as a path diagram, father's education and
        occupation $\to$ respondent's education $\to$ first job $\to$ current
        occupation, and structural equation modelling grew out of it.
  \item Path analysis had \alert{no definition of a causal effect} and no rule
        for which variables to include. The arrows were interpreted, not
        derived.
  \item \textbf{Pearl} (1995) supplied the missing half: a criterion, read off
        the graph, for \emph{when} an effect is identified from observational
        data and what must be conditioned on.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
DAGs are not an import into sociology. They are our own tradition, given its
identification theory.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Theory Into a Graph: Blau and Duncan (1967)}
The status-attainment model as they drew it: a theory of mechanisms as a
picture, before any regression.

\begin{frame}{What a DAG Is, and What It Leaves Out}
Write down how each variable is generated. For a treatment $D$, an outcome
$Y$, and a covariate $X$:
\begin{align*}
  X := \epsilon_X, \qquad
  D := f_D(X, \epsilon_D), \qquad
  Y := f_Y(D, X, \epsilon_Y) ,
\end{align*}
with independent $\epsilon$'s. Draw $A \to B$ whenever $A$ appears on the
right of $B$'s equation.
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.36\textwidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag]
  \node (X) at (0,0) {$X$};
  \node (D) at (-1.5,-1.8) {$D$};
  \node (Y) at (1.5,-1.8) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (X)--(D); \draw[arr] (X)--(Y); \draw[arr] (D)--(Y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.62\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item ``$:=$'' means \emph{is generated as}: a claim about what would
        happen if we intervened, not a correlation.
  \item \textbf{Acyclic}: no variable causes itself through a loop.
  \item The graph keeps which arrows exist and drops $f_D, f_Y$. Everything
        below holds for \emph{any} functional form.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Same Variable Can Play Two Roles}
$X$ causes $D$; $D$ causes $M$, which causes $Y$; $X$ and $D$ also affect $Y$
directly.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.85,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (X) at (0,0) {$X$}; \node (D) at (2.6,0) {$D$};
  \node (M) at (5.2,0) {$M$}; \node (Y) at (7.8,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (X)--(D); \draw[arr] (D)--(M); \draw[arr] (M)--(Y);
  \draw[arr] (X) to[bend right=30] (Y);
  \draw[arr] (D) to[bend left=30] (Y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Target: the effect of $D$ on $Y$. Then $X$ is a \textbf{confounder}
        (control for it) and $M$ a \textbf{mediator} (do not).
  \item Target: the \emph{total} effect of $X$ on $Y$. Then $D$ is a
        \textbf{mediator}, and controlling for it destroys the quantity you
        want.
  \item \alert{Nothing in the data distinguishes the two cases.} The control
        set depends on the effect you want, so the estimand comes before the
        specification. No control set is correct for all questions at once.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\end{center}
\begin{center}\small
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.05}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Structure & $A$ and $C$, unconditionally & conditioning on $B$ \\
\midrule
Chain $A\to B\to C$ & associated & \alert{blocks} the path \\
Fork $A \leftarrow B \to C$ & associated & \alert{blocks} the path \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The collider row is the one that surprises people, and the source of most of
the damage done by ``controlling for everything.''
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{$d$-Separation\core}
\begin{block}{Blocking}
A path between $D$ and $Y$ is \alert{blocked} by a set $Z$ if either
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item it contains a chain or fork whose middle node is \emph{in} $Z$, or
  \item it contains a collider that is \emph{not} in $Z$, and none of whose
        descendants is in $Z$.
\end{enumerate}
$D$ and $Y$ are \alert{$d$-separated} by $Z$ if $Z$ blocks every path between
them.
\end{block}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item $d$-separation is purely graphical; check it with a pencil.
  \item Its content is probabilistic: $d$-separation by $Z$ implies
        $D \indep Y \mid Z$ in \emph{every} distribution compatible with the
        graph.
  \item Clause 2's tail: conditioning on a \emph{descendant} of a collider also
        opens the path, partially. A proxy for a collider is still a collider.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Intervention Graph, and the $do$-Operator}
The target is what would happen if we \emph{set} $D$, written
$\Pr(Y \mid do(D = d))$: the distribution of $Y(d)$. Setting $D$ cuts every
arrow \emph{into} $D$:
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.85,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (X) at (0,0) {$X$}; \node[unobs] (U) at (3.0,0) {$U$};
  \node (D) at (-1.5,-1.6) {$D$}; \node (Y) at (1.5,-1.6) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (X)--(D); \draw[arr] (X)--(Y); \draw[arr] (D)--(Y); \draw[uarr] (U)--(Y);
  \node[lab] at (0.4,-2.4) {how the data were generated};
  \node (X2) at (8.0,0) {$X$}; \node[unobs] (U2) at (11.0,0) {$U$};
  \node[cond] (D2) at (6.5,-1.6) {$D$}; \node (Y2) at (9.5,-1.6) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (X2)--(Y2); \draw[arr] (D2)--(Y2); \draw[uarr] (U2)--(Y2);
  \node[lab] at (8.4,-2.4) {the intervention $do(D = d)$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item Observing $D = d$ tells you about the left graph; the effect is a
        contrast in the right one. Confusing the two is the problem.
  \item Randomization erases the arrows into $D$ by design: the graphs
        coincide and $\Pr(Y \mid do(D=d)) = \Pr(Y \mid D = d)$.
  \item Otherwise: condition on enough of $D$'s causes that within strata
        the graphs coincide, then average over strata.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Back-Door Criterion\core}
\begin{block}{Back-door criterion \srcp{Pearl 1995}}
$Z$ satisfies the back-door criterion for the effect of $D$ on $Y$ if
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $D$, and
  \item $Z$ blocks every path between $D$ and $Y$ that starts with an arrow
        \emph{into} $D$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\vskip2pt
Such paths are \emph{back doors}: association flowing into $D$ from a common
cause rather than out of $D$ to $Y$. If $Z$ closes all of them,
\begin{align*}
  \Pr\big(Y \mid do(D = d)\big) = \sum_z \Pr(Y \mid D = d, Z = z)\,\Pr(Z = z),
  \qquad
  Y(d) \indep D \mid Z .
\end{align*}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item Left: the \alert{adjustment formula}, the observed distribution of
        $Y$ among units with $D = d$, averaged across strata of $Z$ with the
        population's weights.
  \item Right: conditional ignorability from the previous section. One
        theory, two notations; the criterion says \emph{which} $Z$ works.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

Does a college degree ($D$) raise earnings at 40 ($Y$)? Family background
$X$ and a measured test score $A$ shape both; an unmeasured $U$ (school
quality) shapes the score and earnings; occupation $M$ is one channel from
degree to earnings.

\section[Colliders]{When Conditioning Goes Wrong}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Colliders: The Intuition First\core}
Admission to a selective college requires either a high test score or
athletic talent; the two are independent among applicants.
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag]
  \node (t) at (0,0) {$T$};  \node (a) at (3.2,0) {$A$};
  \node[cond] (c) at (1.6,-1.8) {$C$};
  \draw[arr] (t)--(c); \draw[arr] (a)--(c);
  \node[lab] at (0,0.8) {test score}; \node[lab] at (3.2,0.8) {athletic talent};
  \node[lab] at (1.6,-2.6) {admitted};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Among applicants, the test score says nothing about athletic
        talent: no arrow between $T$ and $A$, no common cause.
  \item \alert{Among admitted students}, someone with a mediocre score must
        have got in some other way, so they are probably a strong athlete.
        A \emph{negative} association appears from nothing.
  \item This is Berkson's paradox, and the mechanism behind ``selection
        bias'' and the birth-weight paradox below.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Algebra of a Collider\adv}
Give the picture equations, with independent $\mathcal N(0,1)$ shocks:
\begin{align*}
  T := \epsilon_T, \qquad A := \epsilon_A, \qquad C := T + A + \epsilon_C .
\end{align*}
$T$ and $A$ are independent by construction: $\E[T \mid A = a] = 0$ for
every $a$. Now condition on $C$. Since $T = (C - A) - \epsilon_C$,
\begin{align*}
  \E[T \mid A, C]
  &= \frac{\Cov(T,\ C - A)}{\Var(C - A)}\,(C - A)
  && \text{\footnotesize projection of $T$ on $C - A = T + \epsilon_C$} \\
  &= \frac{\Var(T)}{\Var(T) + \Var(\epsilon_C)}\,(C - A)
   = \tfrac12\,C - \tfrac12\,A
  && \text{\footnotesize $\Cov(T, T + \epsilon_C) = \Var(T) = 1$}
\end{align*}
\vskip2pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item At fixed $C$, a unit more talent goes with \alert{half a unit less
        test score}. The coefficient $-\tfrac12$ is entirely spurious; nothing
        in the model connects $A$ to $T$.
  \item Averaging over $C$ given $A = a$ gives $\E[T \mid A = a] = 0$ again,
        because $\E[C \mid A = a] = a$. The bias lives only in the
        comparison at \emph{fixed} $C$, which is what ``controlling for
        $C$'' does.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Damage Spreads to Ancestors}
Conditioning on a collider opens associations among its parents \alert{and
all of their ancestors}.
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.8,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (T) at (0,0) {$T$};  \node (A) at (3.0,0) {$A$};
  \node[cond] (C) at (1.5,-1.7) {$C$};
  \draw[arr] (T)--(C); \draw[arr] (A)--(C);
  \draw[bias] (T) to[bend left=40] (A);
  \node (X2) at (6.0,0) {$X$}; \node (T2) at (8.4,0) {$T$};
  \node (A2) at (11.4,0) {$A$}; \node (Y2) at (13.8,0) {$Y$};
  \node[cond] (C2) at (9.9,-1.7) {$C$};
  \draw[arr] (X2)--(T2); \draw[arr] (T2)--(C2);
  \draw[arr] (A2)--(C2); \draw[arr] (Y2)--(A2);
  \draw[bias] (X2) to[bend left=28] (Y2);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item On the right, $X$ and $Y$ are four steps apart with no common
        cause. Conditioning on $C$ makes them dependent anyway.
  \item A control can induce bias between variables it is not adjacent to.
        You cannot see this in a correlation table, only by tracing paths.
        Conditioning on a \emph{descendant} of $C$ does a milder version.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Birth-Weight Paradox}
A puzzle from US vital statistics
\srcp{Hern\'andez-D\'iaz, Schisterman, and Hern\'an 2006}:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Infants of smokers are more often of low birth weight (LBW).
  \item Infants of smokers have higher mortality.
  \item \alert{But among LBW infants, mortality is \emph{lower} for infants
        of smokers than of non-smokers.}
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
The naive reading: smoking protects low-birth-weight infants.
\vskip3pt
The structural reading: \textbf{low birth weight is a collider}. Smoking and
unobserved competing risks (congenital abnormalities, severe maternal illness)
both cause LBW, and those risks are far more lethal than smoking. Among LBW
infants, ``mother did not smoke'' is evidence for ``something worse.''
\vskip3pt
Same mechanism as admissions: two independent causes of the same event look
negatively related once you condition on the event.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Birth-Weight Paradox, Structurally\adv}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag]
  \node (S) at (0,1.2) {$S$};  \node[cond] (B) at (2.6,0) {$B$};
  \node (Y) at (5.2,0) {$Y$};  \node[unobs] (U) at (0,-1.2) {$U$};
  \draw[arr] (S)--(B); \draw[arr] (S) to[bend left=25] (Y);
  \draw[uarr] (U)--(B); \draw[uarr] (U) to[bend right=25] (Y);
  \draw[arr] (B)--(Y);
  \draw[bias] (S) to[bend right=40] (U);
  \node[lab] at (2.6,-2.0) {$S$ smoking, $B$ low birth weight, $U$ competing risk, $Y$ death};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{align*}
  B := S + U + \epsilon_B, \qquad
  Y := S + B + \kappa U + \epsilon_Y, \qquad
  S := \epsilon_S, \quad U := \epsilon_U,
\end{align*}
independent $\mathcal N(0,1)$ shocks. Smoking's total effect on death is
\emph{harmful}: direct $1$, plus $1 \times 1$ through birth weight, total
$+2$. Regressing $Y$ on $S$ alone recovers $+2$. Conditioning on $B$ opens
$S \to B \leftarrow U$ (dashed).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where the Sign Flips\adv}
Regressing $Y$ on $S$ \emph{and} $B$, ``controlling for birth weight'':
\begin{align*}
  \E[Y \mid S, B]
  &= S + B + \kappa\,\E[U \mid S, B]
  && \text{\footnotesize $\epsilon_Y$ is independent of $S, B$} \\
  &= S + B + \kappa \cdot \tfrac12 (B - S)
  && \text{\footnotesize collider algebra: $U = (B - S) - \epsilon_B$} \\
  &= \big(1 - \tfrac{\kappa}{2}\big) S + \big(1 + \tfrac{\kappa}{2}\big) B .
\end{align*}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item The coefficient on $S$ is no longer the direct effect $1$. It has
        acquired $-\kappa/2$, entirely from the induced negative association
        between $S$ and $U$ within strata of $B$.
  \item If the competing risks are far more lethal than smoking,
        $\kappa \gg 2$, smoking looks \alert{protective}.
  \item \alert{Nothing is wrong with the estimator.} The regression reports a
        conditional expectation faithfully. The error is reading it as a
        causal effect.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Selection Bias Is Collider Bias\core}
The most common invisible collider in sociology is \emph{the sample itself}
\srcp{Elwert and Winship 2014}.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item \textbf{Wage regressions on employed workers}: employment is caused by
        schooling and by the unobserved determinants of wages. Restricting to
        the employed conditions on a collider.
  \item \textbf{Studies of college students, surviving firms, intact
        marriages}: each is a conditioning operation.
  \item \textbf{Panel attrition}: staying in the sample is an outcome.
  \item \textbf{``Among those who report $X$''}: reporting is behaviour.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
\begin{alertblock}{The practical rule}
Draw the selection mechanism into your DAG as a node you have conditioned on.
Then read off what it opens. If the sample was constructed by any process
downstream of treatment or outcome, assume it opened something.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\section[Controls]{Good Controls and Bad Controls}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Taxonomy\core}
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Variable & Role & Control for it? \\
\midrule
Common cause of $D$ and $Y$ & confounder & \textbf{yes}; this is the point \\
Pre-treatment predictor of $Y$ only & precision variable & \textbf{yes}, reduces variance \\
Pre-treatment predictor of $D$ only & instrument-like & \emph{no}: inflates variance, amplifies bias \\
Consequence of $D$ on the path to $Y$ & mediator & \alert{no}, removes part of the effect \\
Common effect of $D$ and $Y$ & collider & \alert{no}, creates spurious association \\
Consequence of $Y$ & outcome proxy & \alert{no} \\
Sample selection on a collider & implicit conditioning & \alert{no}, and often invisible \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\vskip3pt
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Full taxonomy in Cinelli, Forney, and Pearl (2024).
  \item The third row again: a strong predictor of $D$ with no direct effect
        on $Y$ \emph{amplifies} whatever unmeasured confounding remains.
        ``More controls'' is not conservative.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Causes of the Outcome Only, or of the Treatment Only}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.75,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (Z1) at (0,0) {$Z$}; \node (D1) at (2.2,0) {$D$}; \node (Y1) at (4.4,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (D1)--(Y1); \draw[arr] (Z1) to[bend right=35] (Y1);
  \node[lab] at (2.2,-1.9) {1: $Z \to Y$ only};
  \node (Z2) at (6.4,0) {$Z$}; \node (D2) at (8.6,0) {$D$}; \node (Y2) at (10.8,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (Z2)--(D2); \draw[arr] (D2)--(Y2);
  \node[lab] at (8.6,-1.9) {2: $Z \to D$ only};
  \node (Z3) at (12.8,0) {$Z$}; \node (D3) at (15.0,0) {$D$}; \node (Y3) at (17.2,0) {$Y$};
  \node[unobs] (U3) at (16.1,-1.6) {$U$};
  \draw[arr] (Z3)--(D3); \draw[arr] (D3)--(Y3); \draw[uarr] (U3)--(D3); \draw[uarr] (U3)--(Y3);
  \node[lab] at (15.0,-2.5) {3: $Z \to D$ only, $U$ unmeasured};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{1, good.} $Z$ is a \emph{precision} variable: it absorbs
        residual variance in $Y$ and shrinks the standard error. Include it.
  \item \textbf{2, neutral for bias, bad for precision.} $Z$ removes
        variation in $D$; the estimate is unbiased but noisier.
  \item \textbf{3, bad.} Adjusting for $Z$ throws away the clean variation
        in $D$ and keeps the confounded part, so the remaining estimate
        leans harder on $U$: \alert{bias amplification}. Variables like $Z$
        are \emph{instruments} (Week 7); they identify an effect by a
        different route and must never be used as controls.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Post-Treatment Variables, and How They Sneak In}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Adjusting for a post-treatment variable is almost always a mistake,
        even when the coefficient ``looks better.''
  \item \alert{The greater danger is implicit conditioning}, through sample
        construction and variable definition rather than the regression
        formula:
  \begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
    \item the effect of education on wages, estimated on the \emph{employed};
    \item occupational outcomes among people who \emph{have} an occupation;
    \item an outcome defined as a ratio whose denominator responds to
          treatment;
    \item respondents who completed a follow-up wave.
  \end{itemize}
  \item None of these appears in the model equation. All of them are
        conditioning.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mediators, Their Descendants, and Their Causes}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.75,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (D1) at (0,0) {$D$}; \node[cond] (M1) at (2.2,0) {$M$}; \node (Y1) at (4.4,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (D1)--(M1); \draw[arr] (M1)--(Y1); \draw[arr] (D1) to[bend left=40] (Y1);
  \node[lab] at (2.2,-2.5) {control $M$: bad};
  \node (D2) at (6.4,0) {$D$}; \node (M2) at (8.6,0) {$M$}; \node (Y2) at (10.8,0) {$Y$};
  \node[cond] (Z2) at (8.6,-1.7) {$Z$};
  \draw[arr] (D2)--(M2); \draw[arr] (M2)--(Y2); \draw[arr] (D2) to[bend left=40] (Y2);
  \draw[arr] (M2)--(Z2);
  \node[lab] at (8.6,-2.5) {control $Z \leftarrow M$: bad};
  \node (D3) at (12.8,0) {$D$}; \node (M3) at (15.0,0) {$M$}; \node (Y3) at (17.2,0) {$Y$};
  \node[cond] (Z3) at (15.0,-1.7) {$Z$};
  \draw[arr] (D3)--(M3); \draw[arr] (M3)--(Y3); \draw[arr] (D3) to[bend left=40] (Y3);
  \draw[arr] (Z3)--(M3);
  \node[lab] at (15.0,-2.5) {control $Z \to M$: neutral};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Left.} $M$ carries part of the effect of $D$. Holding it
        fixed removes that part; what remains is not the total effect.
  \item \textbf{Middle.} $Z$ is a \emph{descendant} of the mediator, a noisy
        copy of it. Holding $Z$ fixed partly holds $M$ fixed: the same
        damage, diluted.
  \item \textbf{Right.} $Z$ causes the mediator but lies on no path from
        $D$ to $Y$. Controlling for it leaves the total effect alone. The
        rule is about \emph{paths}, not timing alone.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Sharper Cases}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Mediators and the direct effect}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.8,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (D) at (0,0) {$D$}; \node[cond] (M) at (2.4,0) {$M$};
  \node (Y) at (4.8,0) {$Y$};
  \node[unobs] (U) at (3.6,-1.7) {$U$};
  \draw[arr] (D)--(M); \draw[arr] (M)--(Y);

  \draw[uarr] (U)--(M); \draw[uarr] (U)--(Y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
{\small $M$ is a bad control \emph{even if you want the direct effect}:
conditioning on $M$ opens $D \to M \leftarrow U \to Y$. Direct effects need
\emph{stronger} assumptions than total effects.}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Consequences of the outcome}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.8,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (D) at (0,0) {$D$}; \node (Y) at (2.4,0) {$Y$};
  \node[cond] (Z) at (4.8,0) {$Z$};
  \draw[arr] (D)--(Y); \draw[arr] (Y)--(Z);

\end{column}

\vskip3pt
\alert{No statistical test distinguishes any of these from a confounder.}
The distinction is causal, and it must come from the DAG.
\end{frame}

\section[Front Door]{The Front-Door Criterion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Identification Without Measuring the Confounder}
Sometimes no back door can be closed, but the \emph{mechanism} can be
measured.
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag]
  \node[unobs] (u) at (2.6,1.9) {$U$};
  \node (d) at (0,0) {$D$};  \node (m) at (2.6,0) {$M$};  \node (y) at (5.2,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (d)--(m); \draw[arr] (m)--(y);
  \draw[uarr] (u)--(d); \draw[uarr] (u)--(y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{block}{The three conditions}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Complete mediation}: every directed path from $D$ to $Y$
        passes through $M$.
  \item \textbf{No open back door from $D$ to $M$}.
  \item \textbf{Every back door from $M$ to $Y$ is blocked by $D$}.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip4pt
$U$ is unobserved and confounds $D$ and $Y$, yet the effect of $D$ on $Y$
is identified. A surprising result, worth understanding rather than
memorizing.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why It Works: Two Identified Legs}
Split $D \to Y$ into two effects, each of which \emph{is} identified.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Leg 1, $D \to M$.} By condition 2 there is no back door, so
        $M(d)$ is independent of $D$ and $\Pr(M(d) = m) = \Pr(M = m \mid D = d)$.
        Read it straight off the data.
  \item \textbf{Leg 2, $M \to Y$.} The only back door from $M$ to $Y$ is
        $M \leftarrow D \leftarrow U \to Y$, and $D$ is a chain node on it.
        Conditioning on $D$ blocks it, so the effect of $M$ on $Y$ is
        identified by adjusting for $D$.
  \item \textbf{Compose.} The effect of $D$ on $Y$ is the effect of $D$ on
        $M$, pushed through the effect of $M$ on $Y$; by complete mediation
        there is nothing else.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\alert{Neither leg requires observing $U$.} The confounder sits outside both
sub-problems, and that is the whole trick.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Front-Door Formula\adv}
\begingroup\small
\begin{align*}
  \Pr\big(Y \mid do(D = d)\big)
  &= \sum_m \Pr\big(M = m \mid do(D = d)\big)\;\Pr\big(Y \mid do(M = m)\big) \\
  &= \sum_m \Pr(M = m \mid D = d)\;\Pr\big(Y \mid do(M = m)\big) \\
  &= \sum_m \Pr(M = m \mid D = d)
     \sum_{d'} \Pr\big(Y \mid M = m, D = d'\big)\,\Pr(D = d')
\end{align*}
\endgroup
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item Line 1: complete mediation, $D$ reaches $Y$ only through $M$.
        Line 2: leg 1, no back door from $D$ into $M$. Line 3: leg 2, the
        back-door adjustment for $M \to Y$ with $Z = D$.
  \item The inner sum averages over the \emph{marginal} distribution of $D$,
        note $d'$ and not $d$. In potential outcomes the left side is the
        distribution of $Y(d)$.
  \item Everything on the right is a proportion in the data; $U$ never
        appears. In linear models: the slope of $M$ on $D$ times the slope
        of $Y$ on $M$ controlling for $D$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Sociological Application: the Age--Period--Cohort Problem}
Attitudes change across time. Is that because people \emph{age}, because the
\emph{period} changed everyone, or because \emph{cohorts} raised differently
replace one another? The standard model,
\begin{align*}
  Y_{it} = \alpha + \beta_A A_{it} + \beta_P P_t + \beta_C C_i + \epsilon_{it},
  \qquad C_i = P_t - A_{it},
\end{align*}
cannot answer. Because cohort is period minus age, the three regressors are
perfectly collinear, and for any $\delta$
\begin{align*}
  (\beta_A + \delta) A + (\beta_P - \delta) P + (\beta_C + \delta)(P - A)
  = \beta_A A + \beta_P P + \beta_C (P - A)
\end{align*}
fits exactly as well. A one-parameter family of ``effects'' explains the data
identically.
\vskip3pt
Decades of proposed fixes, equality constraints, the intrinsic estimator,
choose a point on that family by assumption. They are conventions, not
identification.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Mechanism-Based Identification for APC}
\srct{Winship and Harding (2008)} apply the front door. Suppose the
\emph{cohort} effect works entirely through measured mechanisms $M$: cohort
size, educational attainment, the labour market at entry.
\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[dag,scale=0.65,every node/.append style={transform shape}]
  \node (A) at (0,1.5) {$A$}; \node (P) at (0,-1.5) {$P$};
  \node (C) at (2.4,0) {$C$}; \node (M) at (4.8,0) {$M$};
  \node (Y) at (7.2,0) {$Y$};
  \draw[arr] (A)--(C); \draw[arr] (P)--(C);
  \draw[arr] (C)--(M); \draw[arr] (M)--(Y);
  \draw[arr] (A) to[bend left=25] (Y); \draw[arr] (P) to[bend right=25] (Y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
  \item \textbf{Leg 2}, $M \to Y$ given age and period: the outcome
        equation has $A$, $P$, $M$ and no $C$. No collinearity.
  \item \textbf{Leg 1}, $C \to M$: identified when the mechanism equation
        \emph{excludes} age or period on substantive grounds. Completed
        schooling is fixed by 30 and ignores the current period.
  \item \textbf{Compose}: the cohort effect is leg 1 pushed through leg 2.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\alert{The collinearity was never the binding constraint.} A stated
mechanism, with an exclusion per leg, identifies a quantity the linear model
could not even define.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Critiques of Front-Door Identification}
Be sceptical of front-door claims, including your own.
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{Complete mediation is a strong claim.} Every effect of $D$
        on $Y$ must pass through the \emph{measured} $M$. One unmediated path
        breaks identification; a cohort plausibly acts through many
        unmeasured channels at once.
  \item \textbf{No hidden confounding of the legs.} $D \to M$ must have no
        back door, and $M \to Y$ must be identified by $D$ alone. Anything
        unobserved that drives both the mechanism and the outcome breaks
        leg 2.
  \item \textbf{Measurement.} In leg 1, $M$ is the outcome and classical error
        only adds noise. In leg 2, $M$ is conditioned on, and error there
        biases the leg and the composed estimate.
  \item \textbf{Sensitivity.} The assumptions are strong: report how the
        estimate moves as a small unmediated path is allowed.
\end{itemize}
\vskip3pt
A real result, rarely satisfied. Its value is clarifying \emph{what you
would need to know} to make a mechanism argument work.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Potential Outcomes and DAGs Are Complements}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\textbf{Potential outcomes}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item Precise about the \emph{estimand}: ATE, ATT, LATE.
  \item Natural home for heterogeneity, effect distributions, and
        design-based inference.
  \item Clumsy for reasoning about long chains of variables.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{DAGs}
\begin{itemize}\small\itemsep2pt
  \item Precise about \emph{identification}: which paths are open, what to
        condition on.
  \item Makes collider and mediator mistakes visible immediately.
  \item Silent about magnitude, heterogeneity, and which average you get.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vskip8pt
\begin{block}{Use both}
Draw the DAG to work out \emph{what to condition on}. Write the potential
outcomes to state \emph{what you are estimating}. They are one theory in two
notations; in practice each makes a different mistake easy to catch.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\section[Estimands]{Defining a Well-Posed Estimand}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\begin{frame}\sectionpage\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Standard Workflow Skips a Step\core}
\srct{Lundberg, Johnson, and Stewart (2021)}: most quantitative papers jump
from theory to a regression, leaving the target quantity implicit. Their
sequence:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep4pt
  \item \textbf{Theoretical estimand.} A \emph{unit-specific quantity} and a
        \emph{target population}. ``The average difference in earnings at
        40 between completing college and not, among US-born adults who
        completed high school.'' Stated before any data.
  \item \textbf{Empirical estimand.} Which identification assumptions turn
        the theoretical estimand into a function of observable distributions?
        This is the back-door argument.
  \item \textbf{Estimation strategy.} Only now: regression, matching,
        weighting, IV, machine learning.
\end{enumerate}
\vskip4pt
\alert{Most methodological disputes are disagreements at step 1 or 2}
conducted as if they were about step 3.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why This Discipline Pays}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep4pt
  \item It shows when the estimand was chosen \emph{by the method}: OLS's
        variance-weighted average (Week 1), the LATE for compliers (Week 7),
        the effect at the cutoff (Week 9).
  \item It makes ``the coefficient on $X_3$ in Model 4,'' a quantity nobody
        can state in words, an unacceptable answer.
  \item It separates two objections to a paper: ``your estimand is
        uninteresting'' and ``your estimate does not recover your estimand.''
  \item The target population is part of the estimand, so extrapolation is a
        stated claim rather than an implicit one.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt
\begin{alertblock}{A habit worth forming}
Write the theoretical estimand as one sentence, in words, before you write any
code. You will be asked to do exactly this on the final exam.
\end{alertblock}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Where This Leaves Us}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep3pt
  \item Causal inference is a missing data problem. We target averages because
        individual effects are unknowable.
  \item The naive comparison equals the ATT plus selection bias. Every design
        in this course is an argument \emph{about} that term: adjustment
        drives it to zero (Week 6), or another comparison sidesteps it at the
        price of a narrower estimand (Weeks 7--11).
  \item Conditional ignorability, positivity, consistency, and SUTVA identify
        the ATE. \alert{None is testable.}
  \item A DAG turns those assumptions into a picture you can check for
        colliders and mediators.
\end{itemize}
\vskip4pt

\textbf{Week 6}: if we believe conditional ignorability, how should we adjust?
Matching, propensity scores, weighting, doubly robust estimation, and why
regression is one option among several.
\textbf{Weeks 7--11}: what to do when we do \emph{not} believe it.
\end{block}
\end{frame}
